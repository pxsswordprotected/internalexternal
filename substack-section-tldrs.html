1. The Structural Ontology

  • Knowledge moves bidirectionally among embodied, representational, and notational layers along a compression gradient. Upward movement gains legibility by losing richness, while downward movement reconstructs rather than perfectly restores it. Live understanding requires a runnable representation, so language has meaning only when it evokes such a model, though cached output can bypass this layer.
  • The field is the active moment-to-moment configuration of priors, bodily state, context, attention, targets, and available actions. Its target is the currently prioritized preferred outcome, and it weights possible action sequences partly by their expected ability to reach that outcome. Through active inference, the system links prediction, action, and model updating, with precision controlling which evidence, outcomes, and actions carry the most weight.
  • The dependency tree organizes generative structure hierarchically, with deep priors at the trunk and specific or competing interpretations on the branches. Chunking compresses recurring branch structures into learned reusable dispositions, while a gestalt is the unified organization instantiated in present experience and may arise before any matching chunk has formed.
  • A thought is a discrete gestalt decoupled from present input, though it can still reshape the field and body. Memory is persistent generative structure or disposition, while recall decompresses that structure under current conditions into embodied, representational, or notational knowledge. Conscious recollection is the resulting gestalt marked by temporality and familiarity, and each use can retune the structure that later recall depends on.
  • Bow tie architecture models a recurrent flow in which rich input converges on target-relevant compression at a waist organized by the current field, then expands into context-sensitive perception, recollection, and action. The waist preserves stable relations across changing input and tracks variation needed for action, allowing different paths toward the same target.

2. The Dynamics of Fit

  • Finite processing capacity makes compression necessary for integrating constant sensory input. Good representations reduce relative integration cost across varied and novel inputs, while time, effort, or bodily tension can indicate a costly fit.
  • Felt tone tracks fit across both moments and longer stretches of activity. Momentary tone partly reflects the difference between predicted and registered integration cost, while active targets and a background tone shaped by bodily state and capacity already committed to contraction also affect experience. Over time, compression progress relative to active, nested, precision-weighted targets distinguishes "too easy," "productive struggle," and "too hard."
  • Fit operates through feedback across representational, embodied, and notational layers. Representations retune active priors, the body enacts the resulting field configuration, and notation gains meaning through feedback with the other layers. Haptic contraction gives somatic form to a defended or violated prediction, and its interoceptive signals can help stabilize the active prior or field configuration.
  • Slack enables the system to update rather than immediately restore familiar patterns. It is the context-dependent capacity not committed to holding the active field configuration, which lets competing branches and prediction errors remain active. With enough slack and sensory clarity, repeated release can weaken the precision and embodied reinforcement of deeper priors.

3. Learning and Expertise

  • Metaphor builds abstract representations by mapping embodied structures such as balance, containment, and movement onto an abstract target. Notation expresses the resulting representation as language, and the embodied layer enacts it as behavior.
  • Intuition is the field generating a salient pre-verbal representation or embodied feeling before explicit derivation. Repeated refinement, practice, and feedback calibrate the field, so expert intuition usually reflects better tuned priors within the trained domain than novice intuition does.
  • Resonance and friction are noticeable changes in felt tone produced when integration costs less or more than expected. They can guide movement among embodied, representational, and notational layers, but they indicate fit with the current field rather than objective truth.
  • The Loop of Refinement turns a felt sense into a probe, tests it for fit, coherence, and truth, then updates the representation and repeats. A probe can produce resonance, friction, or baseline, and a baseline response may call for a more specific probe. Closed loops test against internal constraints, and open loops introduce constraints from outside the current model.
  • Expertise develops as varied, accurate input, active practice, and corrective feedback repeatedly retune the field. This accumulated training shapes the distinctions, representations, intuitions, and actions that later become available in a domain.

4. Emergence, Thresholds, and Creativity

  • Emergence occurs when lower level parts and relations organize into a whole whose properties do not come from any component alone. A failed higher order gestalt retains recognizable lower level structure but never stabilizes as a whole, and it produces friction only when a dominant target predicts recognizability or requires comprehension.
  • Structure-preserving transformations can resolve failed gestalts by changing their form while retaining intended relations. Each reformulation acts as a probe that the field tests for a coherent organization.
  • Creativity searches for an organization that can stabilize as a new higher order gestalt. It can recombine learned chunks or gain otherwise unavailable constraints through action and feedback. Supporting structure may accumulate gradually even though crossing the threshold makes the gestalt appear abruptly.

5. Conflict, Control, and Suffering

  • An emotion is a context-sensitive field configuration that organizes bodily and environmental signals, felt tone, learned concepts, targets, and possible actions into a gestalt. A feeling is how that configuration becomes known from within, while a mood is a slower background configuration that biases interpretation.
  • Several distinct processes shape emotional gestalts. Compression gives emotion categories fuzzy boundaries, culture and conditioning shape available concepts and actions, and embodiment lets posture and interoceptive signals reinforce habitual responses. Precision weighting changes which signals and policies influence interpretation and action.
  • Field dissonance occurs when conflicting properties or action policies remain active even though the system must choose a workable organization or action. Control depends on precision weighting together with context, available policies, weighted properties, and field organization. Field dissonance can persist without suffering, which begins only when an active demand becomes highly weighted and its preferred state remains hard to reach.
  • Targets operate across different time horizons, so immediate comfort can displace a wider goal when the field reconfigures. Self-control keeps the wider target active, while discipline repeatedly reinstates it without blocking revision. Making intended actions easier to reach helps the wider target guide behavior.
  • Suffering begins when taṇhā turns first-order hurt or felt lack into a highly weighted demand expressed as craving for a preferred state or aversion to the present one. Low reachability and failed control can sustain the target, while haptic contraction adds unpleasant evidence that stabilizes the loop as an attractor. Equanimity weakens this loop by keeping targets and actions available without making them compulsory.

6. Transparency

  • Emptiness means no gestalt exists independently as it appears because causes, conditions, relations, and the field's organizing activity produce it, and recognizing this construction preserves practical reality while allowing other organizations of experience.
  • Ontological authority measures how strongly a gestalt governs later perception and action, while depth measures how many structures depend on it and reification hides its construction. High authority can maintain itself by treating ambiguous input as confirmation and making revision costly.
  • Transparency keeps the construction process apparent while a gestalt still organizes experience, which can reduce suffering by loosening rigid patterns of craving and aversion. Its target is visible, revisable authority, so a person can still commit and act without keeping every alternative active.
  • Seeing a gestalt as constructed does not establish its truth. Open refinement loops test it against external feedback that the system did not arrange, making truth-oriented correction possible.

7. Sanity, Wisdom, and Practice

  • Sanity requires holding gestalts with enough compression to act and enough transparency to revise them. It also requires moving between levels of abstraction and keeping models open to external feedback.
  • Wisdom directs sanity toward targets worth pursuing while releasing contractive pressure, which keeps multiple actions available and supports equanimity rather than apathy.
  • Practice reshapes the field through cumulative, weighted experience, so insight alone cannot establish stable abilities or virtues. Repetition and feedback retune dispositions and deeper priors over time.
  • Practice always trains a direction because repetition makes some patterns of perception and action easier to reach. Sanity keeps those patterns revisable, while wisdom orients training toward worthwhile targets rather than harm.
