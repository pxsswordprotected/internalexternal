The Problem of Expression

“There is indeed the inexpressible. This shows itself; it is the mystical.”

— Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 6.522

This one proposition by Wittgenstein shows us the ineffable. Some things, ethics, aesthetics, love, are only fully comprehensible when not put to language. For Wittgenstein, the mystical here is the boundary where language breaks down and cannot express what the one who speaks is attempting to convey. There remains a mystery, a sort of unexplainable frustration that the most important things, the things we dedicate our lives to, cannot be expressed without losing their immense meaning. This problem of the inexpressible remains a frustration we have to deal with. As long as we have to communicate in a finite, compressible manner, we will never be able to share what hangs in our bodies. Fortunately, not all is lost.

Language here is not a complete failure as it allows us to transport seemingly fleeting experiences into symbols that others can understand themselves. However, it fails in the sense that the fleeting experience is never fully captured because to turn it into tokens of interpretable symbols, we must use compression. Compression in this framework means preserving contextually important structure while leaving other characteristics out. As Wittgenstein put it, the inexpressible can only show itself, not tell itself. Lots of experience then is ineffable since a compressed expression that attempts to capture it cannot preserve the full richness of what it represents.

Knowledge (which I am defining here as information encoded in a form that can shape perception) shows up in the world through many forms such as a book, a television show, a conversation. But these are all expressions of something deeper. Below the obvious surface of notation are two more layers, each so dense with information that it is puzzling why they stay hidden. The paradox of these layers being hidden resolves in a single mechanism known as compression. Compression of knowledge loses richness but gains portability, stability, and shareability. To externalize deeper layers beyond surface level notation is to compress them, and most of what we know, what we feel, cannot be compressed in totality.

1. The Structural Ontology

1.1 The Compression Gradient

As soon as we put something into words, we devalue it in a strange way. We think we have plunged into the depths of the abyss, and when we return to the surface the drop of water on our pale fingertips no longer resembles the sea from which it comes. We delude ourselves that we have discovered a wonderful treasure trove, and when we return to the light of day we find that we have brought back only false stones and shards of glass; and yet the treasure goes on glimmering in the dark, unaltered.

― Maurice Maeterlinck

To externalize deeper layers of knowledge is to compress them. This is the limit Wittgenstein points to when he says there are things we cannot put into words, only show. Obvious knowledge, like writing, is therefore only the top of a three-layer stack.

At the bottom of the stack is the embodied layer. This is knowledge that lives in the body below immediate conscious awareness such as gut feelings and tacit knowledge. The embodied layer shapes experience before explicit description through deep, implicit structuring because aspects like physiological baselines sit here in this layer as well. Next in the stack is the representational layer. In this layer sit images, simulations, metaphors, concepts, and models that can be internally inspected or manipulated through mental activity, whether implicit or explicit. Lastly, the layer that sits at the top is the notational layer which holds external forms, such as writing, equations, and labels, that can be stabilized and shared.

As knowledge travels across these three layers, we need a way of effectively moving through them which is called compression gradience. As we move up the stack of the three layers, we are compressing knowledge into more legible forms which naturally leave out richness the bottom layers contain. For example, if I have a feeling of connection to what I call God and I attempt to put that connection into words, a massive amount of data will be lost in that process. As we move down the stack, we are in decompression which is reconstructing a richer representation or embodied understanding from a compressed notational expression. To move from the embodied to the notational layer is a sacrifice, an acknowledgment that you will lose out on the vastness and richness of the thing, but also an acknowledgment of practicality. Moving upward generally increases legibility while losing characteristics and moving downward reconstructs richness, but cannot perfectly recover the original state of the notation. This means one belief can traverse the stack while still just being one belief; it is simply expressed differently.

Importantly, compression and decompression within the stack are bidirectional. An embodied state can give way to a metaphor, while a metaphor can change posture and action (which are counted as the embodied layer). Similarly, notation like an English sentence can change the modeled metaphor you currently hold and that can change your notational expression. As a whole example, think of this process as moving from the notational layer of writing to the representational layer with models and simulations, then to the embodied layer with tacit knowledge. It should also be said that there is a temporal factor here: compressing is usually quicker than decompressing. Moving from notation to the embodied layer takes longer than the reverse, as you must reconstruct the internal richness from which the externalization originally came.

The representational layer cannot be skipped when understanding is occurring, but it can be bypassed when the system is producing cached outputs. Here we can turn to Lawrence Barsalou, an American psychologist and cognitive scientist, to see why this layer is so important. He argues that comprehension is often scaffolded by simulation; it is the re-enactment of perceptual and motor states. In “Perceptual Symbol Systems,” he rejects the view that “amodal symbols represent knowledge in semantic memory” and says that cognition runs on simulation: “Simulation is the reenactment of perceptual, motor, and introspective states acquired during experience with the world, body, and mind.” This means that if cognition runs on active simulation, an abstraction cannot be understood without first running an inner model of the thing itself. Philip Johnson-Laird arrives at a very similar conclusion regarding how we reason. His mental-models theory argues that we do not think by applying static, formal logical rules to language. Instead, we reason by actively constructing and manipulating internal representations whose structure mirrors the real-world structure of the situation we are trying to navigate. The key point here is that the representational layer is most active whenever any type of “attempt to understand” is occurring. It does not have to be activated at all times, such as when moving from the embodied layer to the notational layer by merely expressing a cached phrase without a live internal model. Of course, when expressing an automatic output, such as “Hi, how are you?”, it may activate an internal representation, which means the phrase stops merely being cached and becomes an act of comprehension, but this does not always happen.

To expand on how the representational layer functions, language must be addressed, since it is tempting to assume thought is just words running in the head. This is not entirely true, since internal representations are not necessarily made of words. A wordless model, like an image, can be held in mind, and it can be inspected, run, and understood without a word. Language and thought are separable, meaning a good bit of thinking runs on visual, spatial, and motor representations rather than words or sentences. Language is just one medium among several, and a representation is simply anything that can be run internally, whether an image, a metaphor, a simulation, or words. Language counts as a representation only when it evokes a model that can actually be run. For example, the phrase “invariance under rotation” might unfold into images and simulations for someone who has developed the corresponding knowledge in the representational and embodied layers, while for someone who has not, it remains dead notation with no meaning. While an image carries structure directly, language does not, which makes it more lossy. What language is specifically good at is labeling parts of a representation so they are easier to hold in mind; it parses big structures into smaller handles like “feedback loop”; it makes an intuitive representation explicit and easier to inspect; it shapes attention; and it supports abstraction through compression. Ultimately, the representation is the actual object of understanding, while language becomes a connecting interface.

1.2 The Field

The field of experience is the active, moment-to-moment configuration of our active priors, bodily state, context, attention, targets, and available actions which functions as a deep mobile frame that constantly reconfigures what is relevant to the mind-body-environment system. Priors are the probability distributions stored within a generative model, and the field is what they become when that model is instantiated in a moment of time. The field’s properties, like variables and constraints, are the active criteria and characteristics the field is identified and known by, which interact with input. By input, I mean both raw sensory experience, such as seeing or hearing, and the things that experience is organized into, like a dog, a cat, or a mathematical object. Properties of a field are not something added, but instead legible markers the instantiation presents, emerging from interactions with active priors, state and context.

A quick note on the substrate in which the field is embedded: this framework assumes priors are held in some persistent substrate, but does not claim to know what exactly that is or where it is located. One possibility is the nervous system, but work and research like Michael Levin’s on bioelectric pattern memory suggest that biological information storage and regulation may be distributed across non neural cells and tissues in ways we do not yet fully understand, which would provide a more full picture than just the nervous system. Later it is proposed that deep priors are stabilized in the body as contraction, but this is just one partial explanation. The main claim being made is that priors persist and are reliably regenerated context-to-context from the substrate they come from.

The field is always active; we are always using a sliding window through which to experience things, whether implicitly or explicitly. At any given moment, the field uses a subset of properties, which are downstream of deeper factors like active priors and bodily state, to determine if input fits within its current configuration. The most important properties here seem to be constraints, variables, and fit distance. These are properties specifically in place to test input's fit within the field. Variables are the parts that can change and alter the prediction of the system, constraints are the binding rules or limits that shape what can fit within it, and fit distance tells you whether input feels closer to or farther from the field’s properties, like a puzzle piece fitting into place. These are not the only three properties active within a field at once; others include actions that feel possible, felt tone (valence), and targets. Felt tone is valence, meaning the positive, negative, or neutral quality of experience.

A crucial mechanism helping the field is precision weighting. This is essentially "the estimated reliability or confidence assigned to priors or sensory evidence, which then determines their influence on updating." It dictates how much attention and trust is given to specific things over others. There are many specific directions precision can take such as precision of sensory evidence, precision of preferences or targets, and precision over policies or action selection. This weighting comes from conditioning through past experiences. Like the priors it acts on, precision is shaped by past input and inferred from current context, so the system learns which kinds of signals have been reliable in which contexts and weights them accordingly through interactions within a complex web of predictions. This means precision weighting can be conditioned over time (a person trained to treat their environment as dangerous will keep threat signals weighted high by default), but it can also shift rapidly in the moment as input and context change. When a threat is detected, the system heavily weights survival variables and constraints and turns down the importance of logical constraints, forcing input to pass a very different test for fit. This rapid reconfiguration of the field is how the system instantly shifts what is relevant without needing to build an entirely new one from scratch, which is a result of precision reweighting, which includes sensory precision, the confidence assigned to sensory input.

Another key job of precision weighting is that it can alter the current relative influence of competing prior preferences whose context-sensitive weighting helps determine the currently prioritized target. Prior preferences are prior probability distributions about which future outcomes the system prefers. In this framework, a target is the currently prioritized preferred outcome of a situation, while policies are possible action sequences selected partly by how well they are expected to reach the target. Simply, targets organize coordinated action by giving more or less weight to certain policy selections across short and long time horizons. For example, if conditioned enough, a survival target can gain control over social or long-term targets, but those other targets can still be “left over” if the survival target is fulfilled, and the leftover targets still have the ability to influence things. The relative weight among competing prior preferences determines which target currently dominates, while how rigidly that target is held is target precision. This means our current experience, which contains context (variables, interactions, etc.), changes both what evidence is trusted and which outcome is currently preferred.

For nuance, nested subsystems such as cells, tissue, organs, clusters of priors, and our nervous system can pursue different targets at once, which is why target relevance matters. This is something like Levin's multiscale competency architecture. Target relevance here refers to the dominant target or synthesis of targets currently organizing attention and action. Other targets can remain active beneath the relevant target, producing mixed responses when their preferred paths to fulfillment conflict. A conscious target could therefore dominate knowable attention while an embodied protective target constrains what feels possible in its current state. This explains why someone can explicitly want a relationship with someone else while their field still makes avoidance more available than connection. For this framework, when I am speaking of targets I am generally meaning the most relevant to the current context of a situation.

So to be clear, this framework uses precision in at least two ways: sensory precision determines what sensory evidence is trusted, and target precision determines how tightly a preferred outcome is held. Within that is a range of outcomes that will satisfy the target, showing that preferred outcomes contain a property of how narrow they are. Which target currently dominates the field is the relative weight of competing preferences set by context and interpretation.

In the literature surrounding this, there is also policy precision (γ, attributed to dopamine), which is how decisively the system commits to one course of action among competitors once a target is instantiated. Low policy precision across the board would mean something like freezing, where the system does not commit to a set of obvious actions. There also exists state transition precision (ω, attributed to noradrenaline), which is how reliably the current situation and context are expected to hold. Its inverse reads as volatility, a property of the field.

All of this to say, typically this framework just uses "precision" as a whole, since all of these point to the same process of giving weight to factors within the field. It's important to understand all the different ways our system weights and commits to certain interpretations and actions, but it's best not to get lost in detail that is overbearing. What's important to know is that precision is a core mechanic the system uses to weight factors that govern much of our experience, and knowing how precision works can give us insight into how we live, act, and believe things.

In every moment, our “default” field is active. This default field is incredibly deep and structures our experience in the most foundational ways. It is primarily instantiated and consciously felt through the embodied layer, without needing to become explicit. It is a result of deep priors, which structure encompassing abstractions like “thing-ness,” “center reference point of experience,” or “that is a person.” These deep priors make the field feel like reality itself because they shape the way in which we constantly experience things. They do the most work to compress sensory totality, by which I mean theoretical sensory input before perceptual organization.

In terms of how priors themselves are structured, they lie in a hierarchy. In the literature, the priors that are the most abstract and slowest to change are called high-level and rest at the top, and low-level is a term meant to convey priors closer to immediate sensory input and faster to update. This framework calls the high-level ones deep and foundational, and places them at the trunk of a tree structure with more specific interpretations branching out. The trunk of the tree is a structural position, so nothing about the literature's hierarchy is changed. Carhart-Harris and Friston write that psychedelics "relax the precision of high-level priors," and match the effect to "the highest or deepest level of the brain's functional architecture." In other words, psychedelics affect deep priors that make up the trunk of the tree.

For instance, the concept of “thing,” a deep prior for objecthood or thingness, sits at the trunk, while the concept of “water bottle” sits further out on the branches, being made up of not just “thing” but many other priors like “resource for survival,” each of which rests on different dependencies. There are also deep bodily priors called homeostatic priors, which are innate and evolutionarily ingrained and provide information about the body’s internal physiological state, such as temperature. These homeostatic priors allow us to continuously generate predictions that anticipate bodily needs and keep us within a survivable range through bodily functions and actions. Because they track survival directly, the system gives them very high precision, so they carry strong organizational authority over what depends on them and can be difficult to override. These priors give the system a physiological floor from which it makes predictions to ensure we do not bottom out at a biological level. Pushing this further, the body's current energy budget influences what an input is and what its affordances are, not just how it feels to us. A bruised apple is food when you feel depleted and gets thrown away when you don't need its resources. The sensory signals are the same, but their interpretation is influenced by energetic capacity, which changes which branches are committed to. Dependent on these priors is what researchers Micah Allen and Manos Tsakiris call the “first prior” which is a stable sense of self-awareness grounded in the body’s regulation. As shown, the default field is like the reference against which everything else is compared and on which everything else depends, so it cannot be "bypassed" easily.

In a way, the default lives in the embodied layer to “act out” its predictions and reduce the error between what is happening and what it predicts will happen. This is active inference, a process in which the system takes action to make input match its model. Incoming sensory experience is structured by default priors, and behavior expresses or fulfills predictions shaped by them. The default field is deeply conditioned, while additional field properties are situationally activated by context, such as representations, and are generally recognizable as an explicit lens. The transition from the default field to something “extra” is like a gradient, not a binary either-or phase change. Repetition makes the extra properties imported from situations and objects sink deeper into the embodied layer, into the field. Additional field properties that are activated and used long enough may eventually become the new default way of seeing things, hence why experts pick up on small things so quickly. The expert has default deep priors running in the embodied layer as a way of perceiving and acting.

I say the field constantly reconfigures based on moment-to-moment experience because ever-changing sensory input is always activating different priors. If I am talking to a friend one moment and then switch to using my phone in the middle of the conversation, the context and object of the phone bring new properties into the field. Talking to a friend activates priors like “I need to listen,” “I need to sit a certain way,” “conversation moves like this,” and “my face and tone matter,” while using the phone activates different priors like “I look down,” “my fingers move like this,” “awareness collapses,” and “my posture changes.” Action and the nervous system then come to matter a great deal, since the field shapes the body for what it prepares to do. Changing input has the ability to affect posture, muscle tension, awareness, attention, breathing, and what actions can be taken. This shows us that the field itself is embodied, as it is not just a way of structuring input but also a way of preparing, responding, moving, and regulating. Most of this stays non-conscious.

Another way to picture what the field is doing is through prediction trees, or a probability distribution over competing hypotheses and possible future trajectories. A prediction tree is the space of potential interpretations of input and the branches the field could resolve into, weighted by precision, which also come with their own action policies modulated by targets. Conscious perception usually commits to one dominant branch in moment-to-moment experience, while competing branches remain represented with lower probability and can still influence inference. Under active inference, one branch gaining enough posterior probability, meaning it becomes the most likely interpretation after taking current evidence into account, increases confidence in its associated action sequences. A dominant branch therefore organizes how the input is interpreted while increasing the weight of the action policies associated with that predicted state. Simply, the system selects actions expected to move it toward its preferred state. But this selection is not purely pragmatic. Epistemic value exists as the information a policy is expected to give about hidden states. Without this, the dark-room objection naturally comes up: if the goal were only to match predictions, the best policy is to sit in a silent dark room. With epistemic value, interactions that result in more information about the world in relation to selected action policies are favored so as to reduce uncertainty about the hidden states generating input. This gives meaning to actions like play and research, which feel worth doing, not intrinsically painful.

Through active inference, it minimizes prediction error, the gap between expected input and actual input, either by acting to make the world match the prediction or by updating its model. At the same time, the system’s actions can reduce target error, a term specific to this framework for the gap between the system’s current and preferred state (though related terms like risk in the free energy principle and error signal in perceptual control theory exist). Prediction error and target error often interact, but they describe different temporal variations. Prediction error signals mismatch with observations that have already occurred (tens of milliseconds to a few seconds), while target error looks in the future to measure the difference between expected future outcomes and preferred outcomes (seconds to years, depending on the target's horizon). Because the system cannot effectively explore every branch, it must narrow the tree through pruning methods like chunking. Chunking groups many separate prior structures into a single unit, such as “water bottle,” allowing the system to treat the package as one thing rather than evaluating each part separately. In doing so, a good deal of the lower-level richness is left out, but the search space shrinks and attention is freed for other input.

So far, the ideas presented can connect in what is called the dependency tree, which is the structure that makes the field’s current configuration possible. The hierarchy is the dependency structure, with deep priors at the trunk supporting everything that sits on them, while the branches represent competing predicted states made possible by that foundation. The perception that an object is a “water bottle” can only become a branch because “thing” is part of the foundation. There exists the trunk of the dependency tree which is not something you can simply choose from; it is the foundational set of priors that makes almost any branching or action possible at all. Branching mostly happens further out, where multiple interpretations are in competition, such as whether a shape is a water bottle or a thermos, while the deep prior that renders both as “a thing” remains relatively stable. It makes sense, then, that changing a branch is much easier than changing the trunk. Altering a deep foundational prior would disrupt much of what is dependent on it. Deep priors are slow to update for good reason, since changing them can present a massive threat to the system as a whole.

A short list of deep/foundational priors that make up the trunk of the dependency tree includes things like causality, time, tribal belonging, anthropomorphism, status and hierarchy, reputation, conformity, threat bias, disgust, body ownership, and a continuous self, which are all made up of even more foundational priors like thing-ness and agency. All of these are not necessarily at the same levels, and some may sit more downstream than others so they can be modulated more, but they are still foundational in the way the average human structures experience.

An internal representation, such as an image or metaphor, is not itself a total field. It is an object we “place onto” the field that has its own implicit or explicit properties, and in doing so, it reshapes the landscape of our current field’s properties. Take the metaphor “argument is war.” This metaphor introduces new branches and reweights existing ones, changing which interpretation becomes dominant and, through that shift, altering the field’s active properties towards war-like things, such as attacking and defending. These updated variables and constraints were not alive before the moment the “argument is war” metaphor became active; they only appeared once we took that metaphor as a way to look at things. In terms of the dependency tree, a representation mostly affects the outer branches rather than the trunk because it reweights shallow interpretations through precision, which is why it can restructure how you interpret input in a moment without changing the deep priors it rests on. Because of this, representations are typically cheap and fast to apply and remove, since they are not changing foundational structures. It’s important to note that a representation begins as a test case against the current field, and when it fits the field’s properties, it is integrated and reshapes the field. Over time, repeated use of a representation can train its structure into deeper parts of the dependency tree, reshaping the parts of the tree dependent on them.

For instance, if I am on the roof of a building and have intrusive thoughts of falling off, the image of myself falling matches the spatial awareness of being at a high elevation (variable), the physical laws of gravity (constraint), and the survival prior of “threat detection.” Crucially, a representation that fits your current field does not have to produce a positive feeling tone, as in the intrusive-thought example. This is because two properties of the field can come apart: valence and target. In the falling-off-the-roof example, the currently prioritized preferred outcome, the target, might be something like “survival by not falling off,” so representations that are coherent with these threat-related properties of the field will fit. However, the representation of falling off the building still produces negative valence because it is a signal that influences action selection to satisfy the survival-related properties of the field (i.e., generated unpleasant images of falling off the building cause you to avoid such a thing); this is active inference doing its thing. If the image were pleasant, we would be more motivated to fall off, but since it feels very bad, there is less incentive to give high precision to branches that lead to violation of survival.

Interestingly, representations do not only model present reality or intellectual knowledge; they can also preserve or simulate the properties of a target, a preferred state, that the current environment has made unavailable through processes like suppression. This is related to a function of fantasies in which they keep a preferred state active internally when the system does not believe that state can be realized externally due to conflicting reasons. By comparing several fantasies, or representations, the system can detect which properties remain stable across them, such as safety, agency, belonging, or adventure. Learning then consists of separating those deeper properties from the original form, like a fantasy, and finding new representations or actions that preserve the same properties. This is called a structure-preserving transformation. For example, a fantasy of living on another planet may contain targets like freedom, exploration, belonging, and wonder, which can then be separated from the fantasy setting and expressed through new actions or environments in ordinary life.

Phenomenologically, the field is like a thing we see through that can never itself be seen. This is phenomenal transparency, the characteristic of a representation whose construction is unavailable to attention, so only its content appears and never the process producing it. For instance, to look at things with an active representation is to deploy a kind of map in front of us, like putting on goggles that highlight some things and dim others in the latent space of knowing. Often, it is so implicit that we cannot get behind its curtain to watch how it applies structure to experience. As a distinction, raw experience, or sensory totality, is a theoretical, chaotic, high-entropy landscape that our systems never meet directly. Deep, high-level priors filter and reduce this entropy into the embodied field which will be explained later on through the mechanism of contraction. While the embodied layer is rich and feels like unfiltered reality, it is always structured when we perceive it. The field’s job is to select what is and is not relevant, leaving us with a workable set of options. Some might recall Aldous Huxley’s ‘reducing valve’ mechanism: “To make biological survival possible, Mind at Large has to be funnelled through the reducing valve of the brain and nervous system. What comes out at the other end is a measly trickle of the kind of consciousness which will help us to stay alive on the surface of this particular planet. To formulate and express the contents of this reduced awareness, man has invented and endlessly elaborated those symbol-systems and implicit philosophies which we call languages.” In other words, we must compress as we move up the three layers of the compression gradient because the richness of the bottom layers is not something we can act on directly. The field compresses sensory totality into a workable structure for us to live within, and as we move through the world, the field changes. Even when the embodied layer is perceived as a vast and rich landscape, that perception is itself a theoretical limited model.

Importantly, sensory totality itself is still a model, something that cannot be experienced directly, so any mention of it is theoretical. However, there is substantial evidence to support the claim that, even though we cannot experience this totality, overwhelming, uncompressed sensory input outside of normal structure can still occur. The first is the entropic brain hypothesis by Carhart-Harris & Friston. Their REBUS paper says psychedelics act “via their entropic effect on spontaneous cortical activity” and “relax the precision of high-level priors or beliefs, thereby liberating bottom-up information flow.” This suggests psychedelics increase neural entropy and reduce top-down constraint from the default mode network which predicts flooding, dissolvement of boundaries, and overwhelm. Interestingly, some Jhana and 5-MeO-DMT appear to increase brain entropy while producing less complex experience, like whiteouts or cessation, which adds more nuance to a simple “more entropy = more flooding” reading of sensory totality. Secondly, we can look at filtering pathologies like schizophrenia: “Sensory gating refers to the central nervous system’s ability to filter sensory inputs,” and poor gating may cause “sensory inundation and ‘flooding.’” We can also look at migraines as another example: migraines show “altered sensory processing” with “hypersensitivity to visual stimuli” and “a lack of habituation.” Third, we can look at the neuroscience of gating in general: “The TRN [thalamic reticular nucleus] gates the relay of sensory information to cortex,” and high gain can “disconnect” the relay from cortex. Information-theoretic estimates put human behavioral throughput at about “10 bits/s” against sensory inflow of “no less than 1 gigabit/s.” And lastly, meditation progress seemingly points to the phenomenology of this reduction in compressing sensory totality. As Laukkonen & Slagter point out, meditation acts as something like predictive deconstruction: “deconstructive meditation brings one closer to the here and now by disengaging anticipatory processes.” So even though sensory totality in itself as a whole cannot be directly pointed to, there is evidence our systems are doing some type of filtering of rich entropy which leads to our moment-to-moment experience.

The mechanism of attention should be addressed. Attention is a process that directs precision across the field, which includes the dependency tree, whether done automatically or manually. It is the control mechanism that determines which properties are weighted more or less in the moment. Attention has three core roles in this framework: moment-to-moment weighting, dwelling on layers, and sculpting.

Moment-to-moment weighting determines which properties and parts of experience count as being relevant, like placing attention on bodily signals rather than signals from other people. Dwelling on layers is the act of resting attention on a specific layer in the stack, which can result in downstream retuning of the field when the proper mechanisms are in place, such as safety and time. For example, staying with a sensation at the embodied layer long enough retunes the field to the sensation’s characteristics. This is a part of how “aha” moments happen and why background compression and decompression occur. Lastly, there is sculpting. Whatever receives repeated attention gets reinforced and eventually sinks into the trunk of the dependency tree, which is why years of practice develop an expert’s deep tacit knowledge which is a part of the embodied layer. These three roles map onto three timescales the literature separates with state inference belonging to a moment, parameter learning occurring across repetitions, and structure learning happening "offline." These different roles matter because attention is one of the only direct controls we have on the field, and most of the time we use it like it doesn't matter. What it changes depends on its temporal variation: moment-to-moment changes what is immediately available, dwelling retunes the field, and repetition hits the trunk. Insight alone is clearly not enough, since practices hit all three levels of attention.

It’s important to note that there is a map of different ways the field can be instantiated through richness. On one level of the map, we can have an explicit “lens” through which we view experience; on another level there is amodal perception which is the field affecting things so implicitly that it is hard to even recognize. An example of this would be the prediction that the back of your hand exists if you are looking at it from the front, or that when you turn a corner on the street you are walking on you will see a different side of a building you’re looking at. Awareness (which is less “obvious” than attention) can hold a wider but already structured configuration, while more amodal or abstract cognition compresses experience into highly integrated representations that are easier to manipulate but carry less of the original sensory richness. The present moment is therefore not instantiated in one fixed form, but rather the system can move between diffuse embodied richness and highly compressed abstract structure depending on its goals.

Because deep priors are foundational and often tied to survival itself, the system treats threats to them as a big deal, and the felt sense of safety by itself does not seem sufficient for updating them. What is actually needed is slack, which is capacity not currently committed to holding the active field configuration in place. This allows the system to encounter prediction error without immediately resolving it through habitual defensive active inference. Section 2.3 will expand on this more.

1.3 Chunking, Gestalts, and Attractors

Chunking can be seen as a way we save energy by compressing and encoding information so that its structure is preserved while its full richness only needs to be revealed when necessary. It is the process by which parts that once had to be integrated separately get compressed into a single unit that the field then treats as one thing. The process of chunking forms chunks which are learned, reusable compressed units that reduce future processing or search cost. The mechanism of precision helps here, in that we can prune many branches of the dependency tree into one usable unit, which shrinks the search space of interpretations and frees attention for other input. The current experiential result we perceive is a gestalt: the unified whole that appears in the present moment when priors are organized and glued together which becomes instantiated in experience. A novel image can suddenly become a gestalt even though you have never seen it before, which requires existing structure, like priors. Similarly, Lisa Feldman Barrett defines a category as “an event in which different things are treated as similar or equivalent in a particular situation.” Here, a category is a dynamic organization changing upon conditions, while chunks are the learned disposition that makes these organizations easier to reproduce.

If you encounter and use that organization of the novel image repeatedly, the system may store its relational structure as a chunk. This results in feedback loops of repetition, stable relational structure, and successful action that confirms our predictions.

To put this in terms of the dependency tree, lower-level priors predict separate characteristics and relational dynamics, and when input provides evidence for several of them to activate at the same time, such as shape, expected use, interactions, and weight, chunking compresses those priors into a higher-level one that can be used as one unit. When that higher-level prior successfully organizes current input, meaning the underlying lower-level priors make sense together, the field experiences the result as a gestalt. For example, if we take a water bottle, the high-level prior of “water bottle” is a gestalt formed from lower level priors that are chunked like shape and usage that interact together successfully.

Repetition builds the priors that let a bundle of parts resolve cheaply as a single gestalt, which is why mathematicians can parse a problem and have several pieces of knowledge quickly come online. Experts do not simply perceive richer chunks; they possess better-calibrated chunks and greater flexibility in moving between the whole and the details it leaves out when needed. Chunking also occurs across time, not just between the parts of an object in a single moment. The sense of a stable “now” is the field binding a stream of changing sensory input into one continuous gestalt, the same compression that pulls spatial parts into one thing applied to temporal change. This is what it means for deep priors to be re-instantiated moment to moment: a prior is a persistent dispositional structure (stored in some substrate not yet understood) that must still be actively reproduced among the field to be present in a given context. The trunk feels permanent because the regeneration is stable and reliable, not because nothing is stored somewhere. This means the trunk can change, since a truly permanent foundation could never shift. Skills like sensory clarity can de-chunk temporal gestalts, like explicit slices of time, allowing experience to appear as changing sensations and events rather than one continuous thing. Because chunks make experience practical, the goal is not to eliminate them but to recognize what characteristics they omit and regain access to their components when the current gestalt stops serving you.

Repetition of field configurations and their reinforcing loops produces attractors. These are configurations the system repeatedly settles into because its components reinforce one another, whether or not the system consciously prefers them. The dependency tree explains which structures support a given field configuration, while attractors explain why that configuration persists against competing options. When the same chunks repeatedly activate together, the field begins treating their relations as one even larger gestalt, so activating one part helps reinstate the rest. As this gestalt becomes easier to reproduce than competing configurations, it gains stability and increasingly pulls the field back toward itself. For example, an anxiety attractor might contain bodily arousal, threat-focused attention, catastrophic interpretation, and less precision on corrective evidence. This differs from targets because the person’s current target may actually be safety or calm, but the policies used to reach safety can reinforce the anxiety attractor. An attractor is also not one singular prior. It is a recurrently stabilized field configuration produced by interacting priors, precision weights, bodily states, attention patterns, environmental cues, and policies.

Attractors then have their own attractor basins which are ranges of states from which the system still returns to the same configuration. A person does not have to be fully anxious to remain inside an “anxiety basin” because if certain co-arising conditions come alive in relation to each other, like bodily arousal and threat signals from the environment, they can pull the rest of the field back online. Basins can differ in both width and depth. A wide basin can capture the field configuration from many different starting states, while a deep basin strongly resists disruption once held. Repetition, bodily reinforcement, environmental regularity, and enactment of associated policies can widen or deepen a basin, while corrective evidence, new chunks, alternative policies, and increased slack can make competing configurations more available for usage. The boundary between basins acts like a threshold because gradual changes may accumulate without an obvious shift until the current pattern stops reinforcing itself strongly enough and another pattern takes over. Since active field configurations and mind-body states select for biased evidence and action to reduce target and prediction error, entry into and exit from an attractor may also require different conditions like environment. A small threat signal may trigger an anxiety attractor, while leaving it may require strong safety signals which can be much harder to perceive and maintain than the conditions that led to the attractor itself. This asymmetry of entry and exit is a form of path dependence called hysteresis.

Hysteresis in this instance, the asymmetrical effort of entry and exit of states, is one formulation of a more general asymmetry that can show up: collapsing many possibilities into one is "cheap" in effort because many paths lead to one collapse, while gaining back the states or information that was left out of the collapse is "expensive" because you must rebuild many out of one. Another formulation of this generality is when we compress from the embodied layer to notation, since compression is less time-consuming and "cheaper." Putting an embodied feeling into language is quick; taking language you've never seen before and moving it to the embodied layer, moving from one-to-many, is slow and tedious.

Field configurations also come with a property of reachability. This describes whether a viable path to the attractor exists under the field’s currently available actions. Two attractors could have the same width and depth while differing in reachability because one is separated from the current state by lacking sufficient actionable moves from where the system is currently at. Reachability is load-bearing for configurations because changing it means changing what actions are available to the system to move from one state to another. If a property, like felt tone, of a field makes it so the reachability of a state is difficult to climb to, the property is load-bearing for that configuration. This becomes especially important when considering psychological changes in which barriers block different pathways that would usually be open if not for load-bearing properties that have been conditioned which result in persistent attractors. When many possible trajectories depend on one such constraint, reachability becomes load-bearing, since changing that constraint reorganizes what the field treats as possible.

Crucially, reachability is describing something specific. Within reachability, you are climbing towards a specific state, but the actions within that state matter. For example, one state might only describe if you can recognize something, while another state might mean you can act on the same thing. Recognizing where I need to do something and actually doing the thing are two different sets of possibilities. Reachability of a state for being self-aware of a harmful pattern is more than likely going to be much higher than that of a state that allows you to actually act on that self-awareness in an effective manner.

Because our perception and actions depend on the priors and chunks currently available, it is impossible to fully instantiate and realize a gestalt that we do not have the prerequisites for. We can imagine an approximation, a proxy gestalt, using existing structural dependencies, but the actual organization can only become available after new priors have been established and are available for precision. More on the importance of this later.

1.4 Thoughts & Memories

Folk categories, taxonomies of things named by their ordinary usage rather than their theoretical shaping, in this framework refer to three different things in general that share the same underlying shape: thoughts, memories, and emotions. These categories, again named by mass culture, bringing the notation layer into focus, name property clusters of the same thing rather than entirely different things with no relations. What they name are gestalts, pin-point constructions of things that have differing properties. It seems as though "thought" and "memory" are two interpretations of similar constructions, both sharing mechanics, but showing up differently in their final form and characteristics. As mentioned, emotions are similar in this aspect, but this will be touched upon later.

First we must answer: what is thought? This being a matter of definitional curiosity rather than metaphysical, this framework takes a specific stance. A thought is a gestalt that can be attended to as a discrete object, that is decoupled from present input, and that carries the semantics of what is compressed. In the example of a thought about a blue dog, the thought is a discrete object (a thing), decoupled from what is going on—that is, physical reality does not represent this thought—and it carries the semantics of "blue" and "dog" through language and associative images. When "decoupled from present input" is spoken of, it is not meant to negate the fact that thoughts have their own properties and influence over the field. Thoughts are still organizing gestalts that can take hold of the field which then has effects on the mind and body and the three layers of the compression gradient. A mental image is a thought that holds a medium that lies in the representational layer, for instance. However, the system itself does not interpret this "decoupling" as a hard boundary, as a binary. This is why a thought of something anxiety-inducing can raise your heart rate: active inference treats this gestalt as it would input from the "physical world."

It is important to mention that the boundaries of this decoupling from physical reality can become murky. For instance, a gut feeling about someone or a situation in front of you, which is a part of the embodied layer, is input driven, but thoughts produced out of this are still decoupled in the sense that they are not attached or tagged onto the physical layer.

When speaking of thoughts carrying semantics about what is being compressed, the sense of "aboutness" of a thing comes from compression itself, since a gestalt points to what is being compressed through organization. "Water bottle" is about bottles because it organizes bottle relations and associative properties. As mentioned in the field section, the system contains numerous scales of attention and awareness, like amodal perception being extremely implicit and baked into our system in which it is extremely difficult to notice. Due to this, most "products" of cognitive processes do not become thoughts because they are not discrete objects. You never have a thought about the back of your hand being there because this process is so baked in and deep. It is also extremely hard to catch this cognitive mechanism taking place and turning it into a discrete thought.

Getting more abstract, having a thought and noticing a thought, self-awareness of thoughts, is not coming from an outside privileged point of view. It's just that when we think we have noticed a thought, our model of perception of thoughts becomes as though there is something separate having an outside perspective of the thing rather than being a part of the thing itself. Self-awareness about thoughts exists precisely because the inference move activates priors dealing with selfhood, like being a separate thing outside of object-like thoughts. Thoughts themselves and a thinker differ in persistence and depth, not in medium. The observer, the thinker, the doer, is another gestalt, the same kind as the thoughts themselves. How could it be anything else? Because of this, thoughts seem to multiply the moment you actively start participating in them. Hence, rumination and overthinking are thought-loops reinstating clusters of field-configurations, like when going over a conversation in your head late at night which brings online the associated state.

As Michael Levin puts it, "I think we are massive, self-persistent thought patterns (in metabolic, anatomical, behavioral, and other media). We spawn off smaller ones, and host others (some of which have agendas and different degrees of competency). It's thoughts all the way down (and up). The distinction between patterns in excitable media and "real things", between data and machine, between thought and thinker, is a false dichotomy - just names for parts of a spectrum." In this way, Levin positions thoughts as a sort of nested hierarchical connected network, with some thoughts having seemingly more agency (like having more degrees of competency). What this means is that some thoughts might be more animated than others, in the sense that they draw more responses from all three layers of the compression gradient, which then has downstream effects like the field interpreting an embodied response of thoughts as a marker that the thought is more "real" than if it did not have an embodied response, or thoughts seemingly spawning differing levels of complexity of internal representations, like simulations.

Speaking to the hierarchical model and depth of thoughts, one aspect of this is that they can sit at every level of the dependency tree. For instance, a passing image, which sits shallow and far out in the tree's branches, contrasts with the background ongoing sense of being a person, a structure that underpins most thoughts, which sits very deep near the trunk. This also speaks to the differing levels of attention and awareness mentioned in the field section. Moment-to-moment attention influences thoughts through small temporal variations of thoughts about properties of things. This room is shaped this way; this sound is like this; this sensation in my foot feels like this. Reweighting these "fast" properties in every moment changes our thoughts by affecting the field, not thought content directly. Then you have dwelling attention – resting attention on a thought has the ability to retune the field, creating a feedback loop between thoughts and perception. Lastly there is sculpting through attention, which here is the act of sinking thoughts and their associative field configurations deep into the embodied layer, like repeatedly thinking the same things which makes those thoughts more available and the associative characteristics of those thoughts more engrained.

All of this is possible because thoughts and mind-objects are generated, not only fetched. If they were just fetched from past experiences, it would be impossible to have thoughts of things never occurring, like fantasy or thoughts of the future. You've never seen a blue dog in real life, but you can easily generate a mind-object of a blue dog, which can be attended to and is decoupled from current input outside of the thought. This has important implications, like the field being a core contributor to what thoughts can be generated in the first place, since they are constrained by factors like priors and context, not infinitely random. Equally as important are the implications this has about memory.

Getting into memory, its process can be defined as learning persistent dispositions that change what the system can reproduce, such as bodily actions to ride a bike. This reproduction or recall is a dynamic act of decompression, meaning that through generation, structure, which changes over time, runs in the present moment and generates an output. One product of this process looks like pulling something out, which is a gestalt whose semantics carry a temporal marker, whether implicit or explicit, such as a thought about a past event that seemingly occurred. See how thoughts and memories seem to be folk categories of similar mechanisms? Recalling what you had for breakfast, for instance, carries compressed images and language that is associated with a temporal marker of "this morning." It can be said then that the essence of a thing being a memory is itself a cluster of properties, recalled gestalts dealing with temporality and familiarity. Déjà vu, an interesting case here, carries the "familiar" bodily markers—embodied signals—but it does not match the temporal content of what a memory should be as the input lies in the present moment which differs from the cluster of things we would normally call the past and a memory. An objection that might come up is "Something is clearly persistent, though. What is it?" What persists in what we call memory is a disposition the field holds towards the regeneration of the configuration dealing with the memory, not content itself. Memories are structural changes that are about conditions of reproduction. As an example, learning to ride a bike changes what your body can reproduce, dealing with the embodied layer; it is not a stored film sitting in isolated storage waiting to be pulled out by itself. Analogous to memorization of bodily actions in humans is that of adult moths preserving "memories" of their "caterpillar self" in order to retain learned behavioral patterns after metamorphosis, in which the physical substrate changes and something is still "remembered" and transferred over.

To speak of memories is to recognize that things, dispositions, have to be accessed. This means something must persist, but what? Here, this persistence is memory, meaning that landscapes, not specific states, are the medium through which things travel. Basins, field configurations, are a consequence of parameters being tuned, not a thing ready to be accessed independently of context. Take a model of a spider tower, but instead of rubber bands, it uses reverberating strings that respond to weight. This tower has levels which represent the hierarchical model of priors that structure experience, in which priors that sit in the trunk of the dependency tree sit at the top of the tower (imagine input as a ball being dropped into the tower, first being processed through deep priors like "thing-ness"). Now, anytime a string is pulled back and let go, whether through input or external cues, it reliably produces a ripple effect throughout the system, which produces certain outputs. These outputs are not stored anywhere "inside" of the strings themselves; rather, the outputs are a result of the properties inside the layers of the strings, like their tension and length, interacting. The output is what the properties produce when an event occurs, when we pull some strings back and let them interact. If we adjust a string or a layer, such as its length, we get a different output. What occurred was not the direct editing of an output object, which would be a statically stored memory, but the editing of the structure that generated the object, the memory, such as bodily context. In this model, if we take a ball being dropped inside as input, it is crucial to recognize that the ball's traversal through the system has the ability to change the structure as well. The tower retunes itself as it is used; it not only is changed through external factors. One string hitting another, due to input making it move, which over time alters the impacted string, is the tower changing itself, for instance.

To make clear what the role exactly is of storage in terms of memory and dispositions, it should be viewed through the lens of cascading and nested connective schemas. When the spider tower model is spoken of, something still must be stored in order for it to be used. What is being stored then is better thought of as "data structures" and "algorithms" that can record durable changes. Synapses, connectivity, excitability, and associative engram relations are different ways these data structures and algorithms take hold of our system, which is something like changing the tension or length of a string in the spider tower. Memory then can be thought of here as a compressed set of dispositions, while conscious memory, like active recall, is construction from these sets. Construction here is better thought of as RE-construction, as we are decompressing content generated from "past" structure which is compressed itself from the field, to result in information seemingly coming from the past.

To make this more clear, what decompression produces is not a memory, but knowledge that is a part of the three-layer stack: notational, representational, or embodied. Which form the knowledge comes out as is dependent on current conditions and the field, not the original form it was pulled from. The three layers describe how knowledge takes form when it is decompressed from memory as it is processed through the field. This is the bow tie architecture, with the field sitting at the point of maximum compression that all input must pass through.

If we were to view memory through a "pulling from isolated storage" lens (a common, incorrect model of memory), then the storage size would need to directly scale with memory itself as it grows, like more "storage slots" increasing. The view would also predict that new memories "crowd out" old ones, making them proportionally less in impact than what they were. However, the view of memory in this framework predicts that storage does not have to scale linearly with the number of things memorized, and new learning actually distorts old recall through cascading effects rather than simply making old recall disappear due to crowding out. This is precisely why learning something new can interact with old memories and change their foundation, because the structure itself was never isolated. Changing the strings, the structure, of production fundamentally alters its output, instead of old memories simply being untouched through new learning. This is precisely why a thought can be had without the object of the thought being stored in memory like a blue dog: a stored lookup table cannot produce that; rules and structure can, constrained by the field.

This act of decompression and re-running through current structure rather than storing the memory as it originally occurred actually serves a crucial function: fidelity is not the important measurement here; salience is. Unpacking knowledge through recall is an act of saving biological resources, not an act of degradation. Metabolic limits force you to leave out details of memory, since it would be impossible for survival if we had to maintain and recall the richness of the original memory. This means fidelity is not really a useful measurement here, since biological systems as a standard must commit to sacrificing detail for usefulness. In every moment of recall, the system is optimizing for salience, picking out what matters for the current moment. The way it picks out what matters is through the field, meaning perception bends recall. Bending of memory is purposeful so the system can decide salience; memory is not unreliable in the traditional sense, but rather it is meant to be distorted for salience. The case of the caterpillar's memory being preserved within the moth makes this clear: most of the caterpillar's detailed memories are useless for the moth, since the substrate changed, but what matters for survival and can be transferred over is preserved through re-fitting, processed through the new structure. Holding this view also reframes the act of forgetting something, since forgetting serves the purpose of declining to maintain what stopped mattering. From a broader perspective, this can be seen through larger time frames. If the system has not recalled something in a long time, say 3 years, that thing is no longer salient; therefore it is marked as okay to be "removed" by reducing reachability and slowly degrading underlying structure. And over a long time horizon, a memory can be distorted in the direction the structure moves towards, such as a memory becoming more "happy" due to the system re-interpreting things differently from the original event. That said, salience of memory does not tell us about goodness. For instance, trauma is this system working as intended, unpacking cues and memories within a frame of danger.

Storage here explains continuity, the sense that something is persistent across time, while recall and active memory explain why the memory and structure can change. This is what Michael Levin means when he says "you don't have access to the past. What you have access to are the memory traces, the engrams, that have been left by your interaction with the past in your mind, your brain and body… and now it's up to you to reinterpret it. And you might reinterpret it in exactly the same way, but you might not. And you don't have to — you don't have any allegiance to that." You might reinterpret the traces differently due to the structure being different in the present moment compared to when the input of the memory was experienced.

None of this negates retrieval itself. When we encounter the problem of multiplying 3x6, we do not necessarily need to actively recover the mechanism and structure of multiplication. The answer of 18 is intuitive, quick, and feels instantly produced. However, the argument is that the answer of 18 is retrieval-like not statically stored. Meaning, 3x6=18 is a chunk that is deep and easy to reach, given the input and cue of the notation "3x6." It reinstates a configuration, the structure we use to navigate the dependency tree, so reliably and so fast due to conditioning with no competing branches in the dependency tree showing up. This is fluency, the easy and reliable movement between these configurations and dispositions; the conditions for the reproduction of the state can be met easily. If storage was static and entered into lookup tables as records, reproducing "3x6" would be just as easy as "7x8," which is not the case. This also predicts errors themselves are structured, meaning errors are dependent on the structure, the strings of the spider tower, which means clusters of chunks often become tangled. If one were to get 3x6 wrong, it would be an answer like 24, rather than, say, 156. The same thing shows up when we attempt to recall memories: we do not mistake the memory for something fantastical that clearly does not represent real-life constraints; the mistakes we make in recall cluster around the same structures. Mistakes in recall are made in related regions of strings in the spider tower, branches of the dependency tree, not in trying to access static, isolated storage. Relatedly, memories bend in a certain direction, shifting towards your current mood and other influencing factors. And because generating the memory retunes the structure that produced it through propagating effects, this shift persists after the effects that produced it, like mood, are no longer present. A static storage system cannot reproduce this mechanism. Just as "any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic," any sufficiently advanced generative process of gestalts is indistinguishable from retrieval from storage.

Memories also have a reachability property, how easy it is to reach and enact right now. Interestingly, how strongly a memory can be retained can sometimes persist despite low reachability. Meaning, something can be well-learned and held in the system, while still being difficult to reach. When you "forget" a memory, or a cluster of inter-connected memories like a language, you are not forgetting its specific qualities; those are still "in you" inside generative structure. What is lost is your ability to go back and reach that structure; you cannot get to "there" from where you currently are. This is precisely why spaced repetition is effective: reaching something when it is hard changes the structure that generated the memory. Reaching the memory when it is easy doesn't move the system due to low variability; the system need not learn anything new about its structure. Difficulty of recall can be due to low reachability which means retuning of the structure is occurring, which is not a bad thing. Hence why the act of cramming, attempting to memorize and learn new things within a very small amount of time feels good, and can work in the short-term, but is not very effective in deeply retuning the structure which lasts longer-term. To be clear, memories can still be transformed or lost through active decay, meaning reachability alone does not explain why we forget certain things.

Another reason spaced repetition works is that context also matters for reachability, because the structure becomes sensitive to new factors that retune the structure itself like mood, environment, and social situations. If you review content through generation on different days, you get different contexts each time which means the structure strengthens across a wider field of variability; the memories become more reachable across the board from different starting points. This is basin widening, the same mechanism that occurs when trauma is learned and conditioned across more contexts if it is not kept in line. All of this works precisely because memory is not just pulling from isolated storage; the act of producing content is what strengthens memory, like writing things down rather than just re-reading notation.

In this sense, our view of memory cannot be separated from culture and environment. This has the ability to change the structure of memory itself, by changing what we attend to and how we organize information. This is made clear when you realize repeated use of certain practices, like writing, has an ability to influence specific characteristics of how we attend to and recall memories. The practice of writing about memory leaves out details, for instance, as laid out in the compression gradient, while a practice like photography, a practice that can be seen as "freezing" a memory in time through image, influences how we envision things. These different practices highlight certain aspects of memory that otherwise would be collapsed in the dependency tree. So, culture does not create the basic mechanisms of memory, but it has the ability to reshape organization and interpretation of what is remembered, which then feeds back into memory itself.

The problem of isolated facts being memorized is an interesting problem in this framework due to the three layers of the compression gradient. What is meant by this is that often times the way we evaluate memory is through a limited perspective of notation. For instance, flashcards typically deal with language, having a question and answer. What is perceived is that these are isolated nodes of notation, meaning memorizing one is added to our bank of memory. As laid out above, this is not the case, and instead what is changed is generative structure. This is important for recognizing what it means to memorize something, because memorizing something means an effect across all three layers of the compression gradient. The memory of the fact that the USA is a country echoes throughout the layers. For instance, in the embodied layer, patterns of vocal cord movements are now produced from the structure, an internal model is changed, and notation like writing is changed. This results in a system of change across varying levels of compression due to the field changing, which is what this framework already predicted. Crucially, a marker of change does not have to sit in the same layer as it comes from. "3x5" is perceived as notation, which then reverberates across the layers and the field, but part of what "tells" you the answer is 15 comes from the embodied layer, like the feeling of counting itself or vocal cord movements. This is all a result of structure and its reverberating effects, not isolated nodes.

Given that structure can be broad, meaning "3x5" and "3x6" share fundamental structure but differ in their wide branches of used inference, certain structure can collapse options before effort is put in to remember something. For instance, "3x5" has multiplication structure that can be applied to a large field of cues, while someone's name does not necessarily have the same structure. When trying to remember a person's name, there are few structure-sensitive rules that collapse options. So someone's face could be "Bob," "Kevin," or "John" all the same, while "3x5" immediately collapses around hard-struck rules. "3x5" cannot be anything, which is why you don't get answers like "200"; rather, you might get an answer like "20." Nothing about a face or body constrains the product because there are no rules to be applied. There is no branch in the dependency tree routing face features to name sounds, but there are branches routing numbers to how they interact with mathematical rules. This is why arbitrary pairs with no underlying shared structure need more repetition to be more easily accessed. And it is why mnemonics work: they provide shared branches that discriminate gestalts at the margins.

Seen through this lens, development of memory, crucial in expertise, can be seen as a parallel to the skill of sensory clarity, mentioned in the previous section. What this means is that memorizing "3x3" and "3x4" is the same move as de-chunking a sensation gestalt into working, smaller parts. Both of these are individuating structure at its fine points so the system can discern detailed differences. Both operations use and grow branches that come out of a shared structure.

The perception of recall, like the evaluation of whether a memory has been accessed successfully, is dependent on the target you're using to evaluate whether recall has been successful. As mentioned in the field section, these targets are often arbitrary, learned and generated through specific conditioning. For instance, when judging our ability to memorize a speech, our judgment is dependent on how much of the speech we should have memorized. If my target is to fully memorize all of the words in the correct order, anything else is considered a failure, not correctly memorized. Failing to memorize the entire speech would be considered target error. This is intuitive but has interesting implications. For instance, standard testing is taking a frame, a target of memorizing things which is dependent on the instructor's targets themselves. If you were to get a multiple-choice question wrong, you would get a binary wrong or right, even if the choice you chose was close in relation to the correct answer. Ability to memorize things is not always a binary yes or no; it is dependent on the framing effects you place upon the output. Targets also matter in categorizing the identity of memories, which will be discussed at the end here.

Philosophically, it is interesting to consider the question as to what it means for a thing to be the same memory. If I hold a memory in one instance of time that produces certain embodied outputs, like a fast heartbeat, and then I recall the memory a year later and it produces different embodied outputs, like calmness, does this mean the memory is still the same memory? At what point does a memory fundamentally become a new thing? If I hold a memory one way in a given context, like when around certain people, but hold the same memory differently when I'm alone, is that the "same" memory? Most people would probably answer, "Yes, that's the same memory," but why is this? What is being preserved? Some of the structure that produced the memory has to be changed since the output is slightly different. Is it that the perceived content of the memory, like what is going on in the thought, is what matters? But if this were the case, we would have to exclude associative embodied feelings, decoupling them from the content, when in reality, these embodied feelings have a chance to change on their own, which then changes the content itself, like noticing new details we originally covered up. In a way, memories and thoughts can be seen as being "alive", in the sense that they respond to structure, which is a product of a multitude of interacting things like environment and bodily state, and error-correct by responding to targets we hold. Because of this, we can never truly compare a memory to the original event, since the original event can never occur again with the same structure we were holding, nor can it occur in such a way that we can "objectively" see it in its totality.

Because we can't compare a memory to its original causal event, and because memories error-correct through current targets, evaluating "sameness" of a memory across time is complicated. Let's say you form a memory at 18 years old, then a year later you recall it and it elicits a certain bodily response. Next, you recall the "same" memory at 20, and the bodily response is different. Now, checking if these are the same memories might depend on the targets you are using when recalling. If the target is something like "did this happen to me," then yes, the memory would be the same. If the target is "does this memory produce suffering," then the answer would be no; this is not the same memory. So in a way, memories have their own way of reducing error prediction when trying to match the current field that is doing the recall, bending them in a certain direction. Targets are not the only factor at play though, since identity of memories also depends on relations, people, continuity, and location. Because a memory is a disposition to regenerate gestalts, and that regeneration is dependent on current input, environment, mood, and other factors, the output is more like a context-sensitive process than a fixed object. The identity of such things in this framework can be seen as organizational, drawing different responses from the dependency tree, which then affects the field, which then loops back into the product.

1.5 Bow Tie Architecture

To tie (hah) all of the previous parts together, we can model the synthesis in such a way that is seemingly already existent within different fields of science. This model or architecture is in the shape of a bow tie, with one side starting wide, converging into a middle narrow point, then expanding back to a wide funnel. This type of model was first introduced by Marie Csete and John Doyle in their paper 'Bow ties, metabolism and disease'. This marks the core biological bow tie formulation, in which nutrients feed into a small core of molecules and then are spit back out into "building blocks" and macromolecules. Expanding on this, Hiroaki Kitano in his paper 'Biological robustness' states that "there are specific architectural requirements for robust and evolvable systems — genetic buffering, robust modules and bow-tie architecture. These architectural requirements are the basis for the system's robustness against environmental perturbations, but congruent with genetic perturbations; they facilitate generation of a flexible phenotype." Essentially, robust biological systems stay stable by funneling many different inputs through a small shared core, then use that core to produce many different outputs. Lots of other papers cite this type of architecture in domains from data analysis to the internet, even if not explicitly spelled out. Most importantly, Michael Levin uses the bow tie architecture to illustrate how we compress information when learning and reinterpret those related memories in the present moment through construction. Levin directly draws upon many papers already cited in this framework, such as 'Constructive episodic retrieval processes underlying memory distortion contribute to creative thinking and everyday problem solving' and 'The computational nature of memory modification'. He draws upon autoencoders, neural networks that compress high-dimensional input into a smaller latent representation and then decode it to reconstruct an output, explaining that “Bowtie architectures feature a low-dimensional compressed medium at the center through which information must come, and active decoding and context-sensitive interpretation on the output.” In the middle of Levin's bow tie is the "bottleneck," the portion of the system that limits data richness, while enabling abstraction and generalization. It is the section that enables constructive reinterpretation of input using "stored" units, memory engrams, which, as Levin puts it, is essential: "the interpretation by the right side of the bowtie (or the future Self) must be creative, not only algorithmically deductive, in interpreting it in future contexts (as both the environment and body internals shift)." There exists a paper called 'Optimal forgetting: Semantic compression of episodic memories' (Nagy, Török & Orbán), in which it is argued that memory works like saving a compressed description that lets you rebuild something similar later. This sounds quite similar to what Levin is essentially arguing for, but interestingly he does not cite it in his 2024 paper. The full 2020 paper from Nagy et al. uses semantic compression, a process for preserving latent structure that is useful for reconstruction, to illustrate how we use a generative model of the world. Essentially, we have rich experiences; we "make" compressed latent representations; then this results in reconstructed memory. They also use "optimal forgetting" to claim that forgetting information isn't simply random deterioration; it is an optimal process of discarding the least useful details. To quote the paper, "If memory resources are to be distributed rationally, this memory decay should not result in random forgetting as information theory provides a principled way of discarding information so that memories degrade gracefully." It should be clear as to how this is related to Levin's paper: it is (in a way) a computational implementation of Levin's bow tie ideas. The paper explains the middle bottleneck in such a way to make sense of it: the bottleneck keeps latent structure or semantic gist rather than every rich detail, which results in a generative model expanding that into what we know as recollection. This is stated in the previous section: "recall is a dynamic act of decompression, meaning through generation, structure, which changes over time, runs in the present moment and generates an output."

It is implied that human perception itself, as a whole, can be modeled this way as well. Drawing upon the paper, 'How higher goals are constructed and collapse under stress: A hierarchical Bayesian control systems perspective', it is possible to say that this bow tie architecture can be applied to frameworks like predictive coding and active inference. Goekoop and de Kleijn describe organisms as hierarchical Bayesian control systems with “information bottleneck (bow-tie) network structure.” Even Levin in his 2024 paper states that "this is a kind of top-down causation or pattern completion (low-level mechanisms affected by the higher-level gestalt) akin to predictive processing in cognitive systems." Even more directly, Levin and Friston have worked on this type of synthesis together in the paper 'Knowing one's place'. They model biological morphogenesis using the FEP, arguing that cells behave as if they possess a generative model of the target organism and act to make the actual body conform to that model. Sounds quite constructive to me. Why is all of this important? Across these models, many inflows of information—whether biological information, sensory information, or some type of data point—converge into one point, while expanding upon its outflow points. It is not clear as to why exactly this occurs, but a conclusion can be inferred based on a large synthesis across the work already done: the world has more detail than an adaptive system can model through its structure, so surviving requires discovering lower-dimensional causes and relationships that preserve what matters and then using those discoveries generatively. We can draw upon the previous parts of this section to see what this looks like from a holistic point of view. This results in something like an open-ended bow tie architecture, in which both ends form a loop, rather than a closed-off pipeline going in one direction.

As inferred from the cited and discussed papers, this model uses the same general outline of "raw" input coming in; a bottleneck point is used to process this; then that is decompressed into output, whether it be any of the three layers of knowledge as outlined in the compression gradient, or specific muscle movements. It should be made clear here that the smallest point of compression here, the bottleneck, is not to be understood as a singular place, but rather a distributed network of structure that operates across differing time scales and levels of abstraction. This can be observed through processes like hierarchical cortical processing, where sensory regions exhibit relatively short intrinsic timescales while association and prefrontal regions integrate information over progressively longer windows (~50–350 ms), and memory consolidation, where synaptic consolidation occurs over minutes to hours, each operating across its “own timescales.” So, there isn't a clean moment where the first half of the bow tie finishes and hands something to the second half. The bow tie is better imagined as a recurrent cycle than a linear conveyor belt. In fact, from a broad perspective, bow ties can be thought of as continuously overlapping in such a way to generate continuously evolving patterns within persistent architecture. Information is constantly flowing, but some type of structure must persist in the first place, or we would not be able to find patterns that we can generate from, which would result in the termination of our adaptive system. Ultimately, there are many temporally overlapping processes that "begin" and "end" within each other, resulting in a complex system of flows of information.

As stated in the field section, our current field is constantly changing based on rapid perceptual changes. Now, because of this, we must ask the question then how exactly a stitched-together continuous experience is perceived if our sensory experience is constantly changing, which feeds back into the changing of the structure and field itself, in more shallow ways. To expand on this, it must change in shallow ways (when dealing with small timescales) because if, for example, structure dealing with the sense of self was constantly in flux, our system would end very quickly due to ineffective action within the context it finds itself in. If we keep the changes "shallow", we can take effective action and use the invariant compressed variables produced from the bottleneck to support these shallow changes. This results in relative stability across nested timescales when measuring the reliability of deep priors. This follows from the model: the priors closest to the bottleneck point are seemingly the most compressed so as to be processed as quickly as possible. And what this results in is a perceived stitched-together continuous experience. Every "new" moment inherits a large amount from the previous moment, like the sense of self. Then nested timescales come into play by varying levels of interpretation: fast variables, like changing light, depend and are constrained by slow variables, like "I am me." Fast variables are constrained by slower ones, meaning their perceptual implications have to fall within the compressed boundaries of slower variables. The light changing cannot be opposite to "I am perceiving this light" if that is a slower prior your system is using. Speaking to the loop, what you just experienced changes your internal state, which changes how the next new input is interpreted, which changes output. Because many of these processes overlap and occur within each other, we cannot definitely draw boundaries between temporal depths and timescales, like trying to say that was then and this is now. So continuous experience, which is a thing and things are constructed, seems stitched together because each present state of being is a result of multiple complex overlapping processes whose slower variables persist while faster ones continuously change. In this sense, continuity is not the preservation of something frozen, but the repeated regeneration of deeper invariant relations across changing moments. Each overlapping bow tie cycle is therefore something like a structure-preserving transformation of the last while enough organizing structure remains "the same" for the system to experience sequences of moments as one ongoing gestalt.

It's important to consider what is actually going on at the middle of this process, the point of the most compression, a seemingly narrow bottleneck point of transfer between the two sides of the bow-tie. As mentioned earlier, this is not literally one point on a map that can be pinned down, but a distributed process working across different nested layers of processes. So, how this middle point is viewed really is dependent on what process and temporal variation you're observing, but for now I consider a "singular present moment" in relation to a task at hand. If we consider a target in a given moment, the bottleneck point is a scale-relative point of "maximum" salient compression.

It is the smallest currently sufficient latent organization for whatever target is being considered at that temporal level. More precisely, it marks the minimum sufficient target-relevant state.

As a very isolated example, if we are hungry and have a dominating target of finding something to eat, a rotted apple compresses input interpretation towards something like available food, while if we are not hungry and have a target of "find the red object," the property of redness will be more salient instead of the apple acting as a thing to satisfy hunger. Naturally, a question arises about the process occurring in the left side of the bow-tie. As discussed before, going from left to right, from the most decompressed input to the most compressed, is a process of distributed hierarchical inference. Sensory systems, like the retina and early visual cortex, filter and encode (give meaning to) input, and progressively higher levels infer latent causes, meaning hidden underlying states that best explain the sensory patterns being received, such as inferring an object from changing properties. At the same time (within the bow-tie), top-down priors and current salient targets determine which interpretations matter. Then, it is important to address where those targets come from, since they are essentially dominating our experience. My answer is persistence structure and memory. Structure here means the relatively persistent physical organization that makes particular organizing tools, like priors, reproducible. What falls under the category of structure are things like bioelectric patterns and patterns of synaptic connectivity/strength. Memory then is what that structure has learned to regenerate, like dispositions. For instance, a learned survival-related target is not regenerated every 100ms; instead, the pattern is learned to reinstantiate when current input is encoded in such a way to make that target salient, which then drives compression towards it. How is this known? Because stable behavior persists across moments that do not share context and change sensory states, so the information that regenerates those targets must exist in some slower-changing way rather than being reconstructed from scratch. There's also a plausible survivable/evolutionary reason here: adaptive living systems that can preserve and rapidly reinstate useful structure, which implies targets are in memory, do not have to relearn basic constraints in every moment, making behavior faster and "cheaper."

Next, we must turn to the field and how it relates to the bottleneck point. I posit that the field is what is instantiated now, while memory and structure are what can be, filled with optionality. The field is an object of currently instantiated variables, not a hidden latent space. The field is a configuration, emergent from conditions like active priors, context, and bodily state. It therefore determines and tells us what target-relevant compression is working towards and means in a given moment. Theoretically, if we were able to model this field mathematically, it would give us insight into which properties are active and which targets are salient, and therefore which interpretations and actions are most likely to be generated from the present state. Think of the field as an inspectable model of the whole current configuration emerging from input, structure, and memory, rather than as a separate agent within the system. This is not to subtract from the importance of the field, but to make clear that the field is not a separate thing acting on or within the system, but a description of the system’s currently instantiated configuration. Then, the field we know "sits" near or at the bottleneck of the bow-tie, since it is heavily compressed.

Getting into the right side of the bow-tie, we can label this side as "output." This side marks the act of decompression, creativity, and regenerative freedom. Here, the encoded input is spread across a space of time, meaning we interpret some level of input, dependent on what we're measuring like from 100ms to a year, and then the interpretation of the input produces what we output, which would be considered regeneration. This is modeled through the dependency tree: nearest to the bottleneck of the bow-tie lie deep priors which first interpret input, and farthest to the waist lie the most shallow priors, those that are most specific. Decompression can be thought of as going from priors that constrain almost everything to priors that constrain less and less generation. When considering action and active inference, this process can continue from abstract goals to very specific proprioceptive predictions. This means there is not a hard cognition/bodily split in these branches, but rather each branch provides a certain constraint which then provides a certain action to take to meet that branch's prediction. A very simple example might be like seeing a water bottle, interpreting those input signals; broad priors mark the bottle as a separate object, while more specific patterns bring about muscle/reflex activity to reach for the bottle. Again, these patterns are known through the structure and memory that "came before" the interpretation and action in relation to the water bottle. However, it's not that simple a model if we consider overlapping and nested temporal targets and bow-ties.

If we are analyzing an overarching target in a moment, say a target to drink the water, this introduces nested targets and bow-ties "within" that specific target we are analyzing. Then, the bottleneck portion of the bow-tie we are observing is also relative to the target at hand, whether it be a broad or specific target. Because of this, a slow latent target can constrain many "smaller" targets, which constrain even more fast inference-action cycles of interpretation and action. Deep temporal active-inference models already consider this, marking the importance of constraints on downstream targets, which emphasizes the need to understand that what we model with this bow-tie architecture is just a slice of what is actually occurring in reality. There is no true "frozen" state of being; instead, these nested architectures are constantly running, overlapping deeply. As a simple example, a target of "solve this math equation" lasts for one minute, while producing many constrained sub-targets like "subtract 5" which branch out of the organizing target and have their own bow-ties, which then produce even smaller targets like hand movements.

It's important to mark the difference between inference and learning, as the two clearly differ in their mechanics. Fast cycles, inference, mostly instantiate and update the current field, while repeated prediction error and "successful" action can more slowly retune the underlying structure and memory that give way to the field configuration. This emphasizes the importance of studying and understanding structure and memory and how it changes: if we can deeply understand how these things work, we have the potential to do many great things, like speed up memory processes, and reduce suffering. Vasocomputation is one example of this, as knowing how contracted smooth latches form can give us insight into how that structure gives way to field configurations filled with negative valence. This then draws a (not so hard) boundary between output that becomes new input right away, like bodily signals, and learned structure that eventually becomes persistent across moments of time.

Lastly, and the most interesting idea here, I want to note the importance of invariance and structure-preserving transformations (SPT for short). This core property is one reason why this architecture works so well for our system. Compression here, moving from left to right in the bow-tie model, is important because it discards transformations that do not matter to the currently weighted target, while preserving those that do matter. What is meant by transformations? By transformations, I mean changes in the input that can occur while some target-relevant relation remains the same. For example, a water bottle can be moved or rotated while still being interpreted as the same object; the surface-level changes are transformations, while the invariant relations, the "bundling together" of priors, useful to the target, are preserved. Under SPTs between slices of input, the inference side discards what does not matter for the slice's observed target, making the generative side preserve broad variables, while regenerating context-specific ones. Importantly, these more specific variables are constrained by the broad ones, meaning "water bottle" must come from a branch that is downstream of one that interprets the input as an object. Equally as important in SPTs is the tracking of action-relevant variation through equivariance. The bottleneck does not "erase" every difference that occurs in input; rather, it can preserve both a stable organization and structured information about how its present interpretation has changed. So, a SPT is therefore a change that alters the surface interpretation(s) while preserving some deeper organization that matters to the system because of salience.

The orientation of the object changes, but object identity and other deeper organizations remain invariant and allow for broader patterns to remain stable. Object-ness stays, while angle calculation, the more specific interpretation, changes.

Invariance's sibling is equivariance. While invariance means that some representation remains stable across a transformation or change to input, equivariance means that the representation changes in a predictable way along with the transformation.

This bottle rotates, and its identity as a bottle can remain invariant while its represented orientation changes equivariantly. Our subsequent actions will then match that transformation. Our muscles might move in a different way to match the rotation of the bottle, but the identity of the bottle remains the same. We must differentiate between these two concepts because compression does not simply discard all variation. Some variation must remain encoded in an organized form when it is relevant for prediction and subsequent action. In this sense, invariance can emerge through the integration of equivariant changes as the system learns what lower-level variables change together while a higher-level relation remains stable across lots of input.

The inference side of the bow-tie, the side dealing with structure, memory and compression, can be thought of as "preparing" input for what needs to be preserved and what can be thrown away depending on current context and targets. Certain variables must be preserved so the system can survive, as having to reproduce broad organizing tools from scratch in every single moment would cost too many resources, as in time and energy for example.

The right side then "reverses" this process by using generative freedom. What this means is that the system can take the preserved deeper invariants and branch from those in such a way that looks like creativity. Michael Levin states that precisely: “you have to be creative when you interpret your own engrams.” Again, crucially, how agents act creatively is in service of current concerns, not some universally privileged position. Context, like broader social narratives, is always shaping our creativity, how we use SPTs to navigate complexity. The right side then is creative in the sense that we must search through a space containing a very large amount of possibility, and we must commit to actionable branches. This gives us more understanding about deep priors as well: a deep prior is one that remains stable across a larger range of input and therefore has a larger invariance span. This would be in comparison to a more specific prior, one that is more shallow, which would be more volatile and have a lower invariance span. More specific priors often track how the current paths to a target change within stable constraints or boundaries, making their expressions more equivariant. For example, the broad predictions about a bottle can remain stable while more specific ones of its position or orientation change with the present context. Moving towards the bottleneck of the bow-tie can therefore be seen as increasing invariance span, while moving outward often increases specificity in equivariant transformation and subsequent action. Keep in mind this is a general pattern rather than a strict rule, since invariance and equivariance can be observed at every level of the hierarchy depending on the privileged perspective one is taking.

I believe this line of thinking directly expands to intelligence itself, at least what we know of as intelligence in humans. William James (indirectly) defines intelligence as "reaching the same goal by different means" in the context of goal-directed problem solving under changing context, where the end remains fixed but the path(s) used to reach it can vary. Well, crucially, here the "same goal" can be thought of as an invariant, while the different means can be the specific branches that spawn out of that deeper organization. From this perspective, intelligence involves both discovering what should remain invariant and tracking relevant changes equivariantly to ensure output is matched accordingly. The system must preserve what matters across ranges of input without becoming blind to the specific differences needed for "representation" (the knowing of interpretations) and action. It can then use this combination of broad stability and structured variation in specifics to find different means of reaching the same goal.

Say we have the variables A, B, C. These may look different at the surface, like maybe 3 facts that seem isolated, but intelligence can discover some relation that preserves these 3 facts, so R, the relation, is invariant across all 3. We can then use R to generate a novel variable, D.

This mechanism can be observed across many adaptive systems, such as within salamanders and moths. In the case of salamanders, specifically polyploid newts, extra copies of the genome make individual cells much larger, yet the system still builds a normal-sized kidney tubule by changing how it is constructed. Instead of 8-10 cells coming together to form the tube, one giant cell can bend around itself to produce the same final structure. As Levin's paper on this states, “in the service of a large-scale anatomical goal, different molecular mechanisms … are deployed as needed for an unexpected change.” The invariant here, the more robust "broad" target, can be thought of as working towards the correct tubule morphology, while the transformation itself comes from cellular implementation. In my view, this is something like a biological SPT, which is structure itself transferring salient information while discarding what is not needed. In regards to the bow-tie, I believe this is ultimately what is occurring on the left side while input is being compressed into a useable point from which the particular system can commit and find different paths to the same means. The system "understands" from the compression process that what survived is what is valuable, so it can then act that out without extra work. This seems like a very efficient system as there's no need to parse input while creatively attempting to reach an end point (setting aside nested processes and systems). Crucially, invariance, preserving what is important, is always in service of a target and the scale at play. "This food should not be thrown away" does not have to be fully stable and invariant across contexts. For instance, if you're not hungry, the food might be marked as "okay to throw away" because finding something to eat is not a relevant target at that time. And because of this, sub-targets, like reaching for the food, will remain constrained by their top-down parents, such as wanting to satisfy hunger. This illustrates the importance of understanding behavior and beliefs within complex, nested architectures that are not immediately obvious. It is quite naive, in my opinion, to view generation within a confined, isolated frame not taking into account how input arrives or what other top-down constraints are at play.

Clearly, this idea of the system using SPTs to efficiently compress and decompress maps onto active inference by using targets as active constraints on behavior to reach future preferred states. However, constraints do not mean they lock down the system to just one path; instead, the SPT preserves the ultimate target as the end, and policy selection works towards that target and weighs different means. In the dependency tree, many pathways may be available to take, but the system must commit to only one. And linking this to learning, holding and preserving the same variables gives our system, structure and memory, the chance to develop and maintain the dispositions that give way to these important invariants. We can go from a novel solution we've not yet seen, to the process of practice, to maturing the relevant pathways, which leads to the relevant priors being reliably instantiated when they are called for.

Having said all this, invariance on its own does not mean goodness; it is simply the way our system evolved for survival. This idea of intelligence can be good or bad (in relation to suffering), all depending on the context. A person preserving the organization of "everything is dangerous" across a vast amount of diverse input would be considered generally bad. Same thing with a rigid ideology that is harmful to others: the invariants track, even when coming across opposing evidence.

Now, something underlying all of this talk so far has been the bow-tie model itself, which must be analyzed from a privileged-human perspective which brings about interesting dynamics of modeling. Meaning, specificity, and width of bow-ties can vary depending on the analysis one is performing. This changes the questions one might ask about an organism or adaptive system; thus, it changes the answers you will find, so it is important to recognize how we can cleanly model the whole of something. So, getting into it, it can be said that the size of a bow-tie is relative to the spatiotemporal and problem-space sizing of the target being analyzed. If we are looking at a top-down target of an adaptive system, like "survive," this dictates the sizing of all three levels of the bow-tie: the inference side, the bottleneck, and the generative side. Larger-horizon targets, those that span across a large spatiotemporal scale, require the integration of a broader range of relevant input and can organize larger trees of nested subtargets and information, while smaller-horizon targets integrate and coordinate comparatively local states.

Think of a large-horizon target like "become a doctor." This requires the system to "consider" both larger subtargets, like going to med school, and extremely specific targets like "reach towards the notebook" for when you're studying. Through this, the system must consider a vast range of input that is being analyzed for what is considered relevant for the long-term target of becoming a doctor, which constrains the nested targets. These nested targets are of course smaller on both a spatiotemporal scale and a problem-solving level—how many means there are to reach the same endpoint—which means the sizing of these bow-ties will be smaller in comparison to that of the constraining, more dominant ones. Because of this sizing difference, we find that the model itself is now constrained in new ways: the overall size of the inference side and the specificity level of the generative side are both scaled in proportion to that of the target and context being considered. When looking at the target of becoming a doctor, the generative side of this bow-tie will be massively specific in terms of ways the dependency tree manifests itself. Looking at a smaller scale, like a cell wanting to maintain ion balance, it's clear both the inference and generative sides will be smaller in scale, meaning the system has to integrate and act on a smaller range of variables and possible states. A larger bow-tie can imply the system is acting on a larger temporal scale, possibly a large spatial scale, lots of nested subtargets, more constraints, and a larger space of knowledge. Crucially, the high-level top-down state of a bow-tie is not necessarily specific in its nature. In fact, these high-level organizing target states are quite abstract. Becoming a doctor is more abstract than "contract this finger muscle," and the reason this is is precisely because it spawns less specific generative output. More abstract constraints drive more long-term specificity. This also appears in the bottleneck section: in the case of the cell, that maximum compression point that is useful to commit from will be massively "small" compared to a much more complex system like a human wanting to become a doctor. In a large bottleneck, many invariants are kept in place and branch out into the generative side, and are acted upon through nested trees, making the larger system more specific in its own top-down tree.

And again, these bow-ties continuously overlap, for they do not just cleanly map out in a linear fashion. Slower, large-scale targets constrain faster, smaller-scale inference-cycles. For example, while reading a paragraph, the target of understanding the text overlaps with smaller-scale targets involving eye movements, maintaining attention, and other small-scale targets which unfold together across the same span of time.

Coming off this, I believe Levin's idea of the cognitive light cone fits in neatly. This light cone concept is demarcated as "the scale of the largest goal, in both space and time, which a system is capable of working towards." Originally I had made the assumption that we can simply model a system's light cone by looking at its "whole" bow-tie, but quickly this comes apart when it is recognized that a system does not have one singular bow-tie or target operating at once, even in simple systems that might seemingly have one high-level target spawning nested bow-ties. The cognitive light cone instead marks the outer boundary of the largest-scale target the system is capable of pursuing, not the size of every process running within the system. This naturally produces a question concerning how we know the largest-scale target a system is capable of pursuing. In general, this can become known through testing and tracking what occurs in the system when you prescribe perturbations that offset invariant variables. When you can "stop" observation in accordance with the system as a whole is when you can no longer find evidence that the larger state is being actively restored across many disturbances. Crucially, we must first begin observation with a potential target that might track across many perturbations. Without a starting prediction, it will become impossible to course-correct findings into knowing the relevant highest-level target.

Let's say we have a starting prediction that a person wants to get home safely. We might add in many perturbations like delaying their train, their phone dying, or a stranger talking to them. When these perturbations occur, it is crucial to ask if behavior still reaches towards the target of getting home safely. If the target remains invariant, and nested actions are in service of the target, we can say that at that level of analysis, the preserved higher-order variable is "getting home safe." Now, to find out the cognitive light cone of the whole system, we need to ask a different question. For this, we might start with something like "survival" or "maintain viability." Many lower-level variables change continuously, like eating different things, changing cells, turnover of molecules, or changing internal temperatures, while some high-level organization persists, constraining all of these low-level variables. Here, though, we must be careful not to confuse this with pure "survival." Here, viability is simply a useful high-level invariant for explaining a broad range of output, but it does not mean the system is acting in agentic ways towards the known mechanism of survival. Levin specifically notes that collective cells can scale from local homeostatic variables toward much larger target states such as anatomical configurations, enlarging the spatiotemporal scale of targets they can pursue: "the scaling enables cellular collectives to enlarge their 'cognitive light cone' – the spatio-temporal scale of the target states they are able to pursue." So then, the light cone of what we are observing is like an outer envelope surrounding the largest bow-tie the system maintains across its existence. This implies that a larger cognitive light cone enables the capacity for larger-scale bow ties to exist. In a way, the generative side of the largest bow-tie of an adaptive system can be said to reflect the scale of its cognitive light cone.

If a cognitive light cone of a system specifies the largest spatiotemporal goal the system can pursue, and the scale of a bow-tie specifies the range of salient variables integrated and possible actions organized for a goal, then the light cone can be treated as an upper bound on the scale of goal-relative bow ties possible to that system.

I want to speak to the physical constraints of this bow-tie architecture. All three parts, the inference side, the bottleneck, and the generative side, are constrained by the system's architecture itself. Every part faces binding constraints that make the system what it is. Different embodiments bring about different measurable variables and offer different affordances on the world. It might be possible to find principles that apply to each part that can give us insight into the system's level of complexity, which determines the sizing of the bow-tie and the system's capabilities. My guess goes like this: bow ties are bounded on the inference side by what they can sense, at the bottleneck by what they can integrate and preserve as target-salient organization, and on the generative side by what they can control. In humans, for instance, we can sense light, sound, chemicals, touch, and internal bodily states; we can integrate and preserve a certain amount of information because of structure and memory; and we can control external things through our physical bodies. The cognitive light cone then would be constrained by what makes the bow-tie possible in the first place: physical architecture. One might argue that capabilities that contribute to cognitive light cone size, like human thoughts, are not necessarily "physical." Without getting into arguments surrounding physicalism and emergence, I would push back on this by saying these capabilities are only possible because of the underlying architecture at play, which gives rise to what is possible. This probably means there might be problems in mathematically modeling this, as emergent capabilities are inherently surprising, but nonetheless through privileged external observation, these capabilities are still observable as constraints on what the system is capable of doing. So, if we are trying to find a more general labeling of the three parts that scale up continuously across all living systems, it might go roughly: observability → internal control state → controllability → loop.

2. The Dynamics of Fit

2.1 The Energetic Triad

This framework’s ontology rests heavily on how the mind-body system manages metabolic and computational cost. I use “cost” here in a relative sense, not as a measure of calories burned. At any moment, the brain has limited processing capacity and attention to go around, and different possible interpretations of what you’re perceiving compete for resources. “Cost” means how much of that limited capacity an input demands and how much additional capacity is used to process it.

Information costs energy, which is why the amount we can spend is finite. This is also why compression is a necessary mechanism our system uses, not one that is done by choice; an agent that would try to track and memorize everything would die before it could capture what it's after. What I mean by "cost" throughout is the relative usage of that allocation.

Our system runs on a finite budget of processing capacity and spends much of it maintaining a stable, workable interpretation of ongoing input. For every input, the system estimates how much processing it will require and then registers how much capacity it actually demands. This is always happening in experience because sensory input is constant. Here, processing refers broadly to the resources used to handle an input, while integrating it means attempting to “place it into” the field, a process that subsequently produces a felt sense if deviations are consciously noticeable.

One main source of an input’s obvious feeling tone is the system reporting the difference between the estimated cost and the registered cost of processing it. Put simply, this is integration-cost prediction error pertaining to the predicted versus actual integration cost of an input. Successful compression here can reduce the cost of integrating an input by allowing the field to organize previously separate, unchunked elements into a single workable structure.

Some practical proxies for whether an input is high cost to integrate are time (rereading a passage), effort (using “extra focus”), and bodily tension (like a furrowed brow). For an input that is low cost to integrate, some proxies include immediacy (clicks fast), freed attention (gained capacity for more input), and immediate tension release (relaxation).

However, cost difference is not the only source of feeling tone; a second source is an input’s relation to the current field’s target. When you face threatening input, say if you saw a lion, the field’s most heavily weighted target would be something like “survival at all costs.” The input can integrate cleanly and quickly, as when you immediately recognize the lion, but because the situation threatens the heavily weighted target of being in a state of remaining alive, the felt tone is negative. These two sources of feeling tone can come apart at the edges, which is why an intrusive thought, like being eaten by the lion, can integrate easily but still feel bad. It should also be noted that these two sources can contribute simultaneously to the felt tone of an experience and with different strengths. For example, if the lion is hidden in some grass and making noise, the input may both cost more to integrate than expected when trying to disambiguate hearing from seeing, and threaten the target of remaining alive, causing the two sources to reinforce each other.

Setting aside targets to focus on the main structure here, this pattern of a fit-baseline with positive and negative spikes shows up across pretty much all situations in experience. The fit-baseline is the level at which integration cost roughly matches the predicted cost, while spikes or differences in either direction occur when processing cost is more or less than what the system expected. It is important to separate two things here that may get confused for being one "baseline." The fit-baseline describes moment-to-moment processing of input, while background felt tone is the overall felt tone level experience lies at, set by factors like physiology and by how much capacity the system has in general. This capacity is bottlenecked by held contraction and the pushing away or pull towards targets the system cannot satisfy (later expanded on through taṇhā).

As a helpful metaphor, we might describe this whole thing as the ocean. The background felt tone is the level at which the ocean sits, while the fit-baseline is the surface resting at that level. Most of the time, water is calm, meaning input is processed using capacity that is correctly predicted. However, sometimes waves occur which we can call positive or negative differences from the fit-baseline level. A swell up is when input is cheaper to process than predicted and a down trough (or an imagined "reverse wave") is when input comes in more costly to process than predicted. These are both deviations from the relatively stable, flat surface of the calm ocean. Crucially, a singular wave of the same size will hit different levels based on the ocean level that day. If it's high tide, the same wave will crest high, while barely reaching on low tide. Here, what changed was the baseline level, or where the ocean was sitting at overall. This represents that the same insight that is processed more cheaply than predicted can be experienced as either mundane one day or exciting another day due to the background felt tone sitting at different levels.

Because input is constant and most input integrates near its predicted cost, these fit-baseline-level integrations produce a cumulative embodied tone of fit at whatever level the floor sits at, a gestalt that organizes our experience of processing costs. This fit-baseline is what differences are compared against. Note that the fit-baseline is not the same as a constant positive difference, since a positive difference is a noticeable deviation, not a baseline level. You can see this three-level pattern, positive spike, fit-baseline, negative spike, across aesthetic experiences, a math proof, a painting, or sensations in the body, since part of an input's feeling tone comes from the system registering these deviations against the level it is currently sitting at.

For the three levels, we can categorize them as positive, fit-baseline, and negative, with positive and negative representing differences in opposing directions. Fit-baseline is where registered cost roughly matches predicted cost, with the trunk of the dependency tree structuring incoming input without major surprises in either direction. A negative difference occurs when an input costs more to integrate than predicted, often because it cannot be compressed into a single gestalt, so the system spends extra resources parsing it. A fully predictable input that is also predicted to be cheap to integrate, like elevator music, produces little or no difference and sinks into the fit-baseline as mundane rather than producing an obvious positive difference. A positive difference occurs when an input expected to be costly, perhaps because of its perceived richness or complexity, instead fits neatly into the active field as a valid gestalt and integrates more cheaply than predicted, requiring fewer processing resources than expected. In fast cases like seeing an object, this can happen within fractions of a second, though slower forms exist, such as a math proof that unfolds over minutes.

This is one reason why input that is hard to organize, like going to an event for the first time, can feel bad on its own, separate from anything threatening. Going to an unfamiliar event costs capacity to process as the system attempts to calculate and organize input, which is very hard to do with input there is not much history about.

This helps explain why internal representations matter so much. Representations can retune the field’s properties at a given moment, lowering the cost of integrating an input by making fit more substantial. This is why an already well-modeled input can integrate cheaply, while a poorly modeled one produces an obvious negative difference. A poor model will have you attempting to integrate the input, like a question, but there will be lots of struggle because of increased processing cost. A good model lowers the integration cost of inputs relative to encountering them without it, and it does so across many input types. For instance, a math problem is much easier to parse when the necessary prerequisites are already in place, making a model with those prerequisites better. Remember, the claim is that a good model lowers the cost of integrating future and novel inputs, not that low cost in the present necessarily means learning is happening, since our models need to integrate a range of varied and conflicting inputs. A flat-earther can update their model by watching YouTube videos and easily integrate a great deal of input, but only within a restricted subset, such as an echo chamber. The key is that good representations continue reducing integration costs for inputs you cannot plan for and across long time horizons.

This triad connects back to the dependency tree in important ways. The size of any spike compared to the fit-baseline depends on where in the tree the affected priors sit. A branch further out on the tree (i.e., not dealing with foundational priors) is cheap to change and hardly registers as an event, while violations that occur deeper in the tree affect deep priors and force changes to everything else that depends on them, resulting in a much larger and more obvious felt tone difference. Hence why some surprises or changes in experience feel trivial while others feel like deep fragmentation: the same core mechanism occurs, but the depth and magnitude of the priors being affected differ greatly in cost. Because the tree rests on deep priors, updating them is incredibly expensive for our system to integrate, so the negative felt tone produced when deep priors are violated reflects the system accounting for that cost. This is why shame can hurt so badly, since it affects deep priors dealing with the sense of self and security, such as “I am bad,” which produces many discrepancies in the integration of input, even if low-level. The foundational trunk of the dependency tree is therefore slow to update because, according to the triad, such integration is extremely costly, while branches further out are much cheaper to change. But keep in mind that outer branches are where most updating happens (which can have cascading effects toward the trunk), whereas deep priors, which are active in nearly every moment and rarely violated, contribute to much of our ongoing feeling tone, mostly through steady fit-baseline integration with occasional obvious spikes of felt tone.

It’s important to mention the temporal variation that comes with these processes. This section so far has focused on one-shot differences tracking fast integration time slices, but what about the way we track feeling tone across longer slices of time? The main measurement we can use here is compression progress, the rate at which integration cost is reduced relative to a target. We can model this in three broad ways: too easy, productive struggle, and too hard. Of course, these slopes do not always appear in such simple forms; they are just practical proxies for measuring feeling tone over time. The “too easy” signal tracks experiences over slices of time in which there is little integration cost left to reduce, which typically results in feelings like boredom. The “productive struggle” signal tracks experiences in which integration cost decreases over time with input that starts as costly. If cost falls steadily, this can result in feelings like enjoyment or flow. Interestingly, there seems to be a “Goldilocks Effect” in cognitive science and learning in which people disengage from sequences that are either too predictable or too surprising. The “Eighty-Five Percent Rule” demonstrates something very similar in which “there is a sweet spot in which training is neither too easy nor too hard, and where learning progresses most quickly.” Lastly, the “too hard” signal tracks experiences in which integration cost does not decrease over time or even increases, which commonly results in feelings like frustration or overwhelm.

It is also possible to extrapolate this cost-slope function into a temporal property of phenomenology, meaning the rate at which an ongoing experience becomes more structured relative to what the system is trying to do. For instance, “too easy” means the field is producing dominant, low-cost gestalts, so experience feels mundane. “Productive unfolding” means uncertainty remains, but the field is gradually finding structure, producing curiosity, absorption, insight, aesthetic pleasure, or flow. “Too hard” means that no workable structure emerges in experience, so unresolved input accumulates into tension and overwhelm. This is not a complete theory of feeling tone but one proposed contributor to phenomenological experience.

Crucially, compression progress is always relative to a target, like finishing a problem set today or becoming a mathematician over years, which operate across different time slices. An activity is not by itself too easy or too hard; it is evaluated relative to what the system is currently trying to accomplish through its heavily weighted targets. So the same activity, such as solving a problem set, can show different rates of progress relative to each target. For example, solving a problem set in a math textbook may feel like little immediate progress while still feeling meaningful based on the long-term target of becoming a mathematician. Likewise, a more difficult problem may feel locally productive while seeming irrelevant to a larger target, like learning side branches in math that aren’t necessary for becoming a mathematician. Several targets can remain active at once, and precision weighting determines which of their progress signals contributes most strongly to the feeling tone of the moment. When these targets align, short-term work can inherit meaning from longer-term targets. When they conflict, the same activity can produce mixed feelings. Felt tone in any moment is therefore shaped by compression-rate signals across several nested targets, such as short-term targets within long-term ones, with deeper, more heavily precision-weighted targets carrying greater weight and tending to dominate feeling tone. In this way, felt tone of experience over time is a signal of whether integration cost is falling as quickly as the field predicts relative to its active targets.

This cost-prediction model is not meant to claim that the brain literally reports exact metabolic cost in experience. It is more like a phenomenological synthesis of predictive processing, interoception, allostasis, processing fluency, and aesthetic prediction error. The Free Energy Principle models perception and action as processes organized around reducing uncertainty or prediction error. Active inference and allostatic models then help inform this synthesis through bodily regulation, where we predict and manage our physiological needs rather than reacting after the fact. Dealing with aesthetics, processing fluency theory says that “easier” processing tends to produce more positive aesthetic response, and predictive models of art say that pleasure often comes from the reintroduction of order after prediction has been disrupted due to complex input. All this supports the fact that the result of the difference between expected cost and actual integration cost of input is one contributor to the felt tone, or valence, of the field rather than felt tone itself. The test is obvious: a stable stream of input, like reading a book, produces differences in feeling when harder-to-integrate passages are read. Because nothing about threat or reward changed between differing levels of passages, the change in cost to integrate is an isolated variable, not tied to another factor. However, it is not the only thing moving felt tone levels, since factors like satisfaction of targets can come into play as well.

Lastly, I want to touch on the importance of biological and physiological factors at play here. A big part of the background felt tone, which contributes to the fit-baseline, is not fixed but is set by the body’s overall state, which is where physiology comes to matter. The felt tone of this triad is heavily modulated by allostasis, which is the body’s proactive regulation of its own resources: “Efficient regulation [of the body] requires anticipating needs and preparing to satisfy them before they arise.” Other modulators at play include the state of the nervous system, stress hormones like cortisol, and inflammation, which are all processed into the field through interoception, which is “the process by which the nervous system senses, interprets, and integrates signals originating from within the body, providing a moment-by-moment mapping of the body’s internal landscape across conscious and unconscious levels.” Another well-supported finding is that inflammation can produce sickness-like behavior such as fatigue, minimal activity, social withdrawal, and a depressed mood. Relatedly, heavy stress can bias perception and attention toward potential threat, which causes feedback loops of threat-detection. This means a drained or threatened body with little slack has lower capacity to handle complexity and interprets more inputs as costly to integrate before any specific input is received.

What is being described here is not the fit-baseline that positive or negative spikes are compared against, which lies wherever the system is currently at, but the background felt tone the whole system uses. What modulates the background felt tone is how much capacity is already used before input is integrated, which includes capacity spent holding contraction and running second-order targets on experience. By releasing held contraction that is not globally useful and lowering the precision on second-order targets, we can gain capacity and raise the background felt tone, the way chronic stress and bad health lower it. Put simply, the background felt tone (which is a force multiplier) partly reflects the brain’s expectations about bodily state and available resources.

Importantly, these expectations can include long horizon targets, not just short-term bodily needs. A future target is held in the current field as an expectation that present actions are moving the system toward it, and the body can regulate differently depending on whether that target feels achieved, achievable, or threatened. This helps explain why someone can feel a sense of deep okay-ness while doing difficult work if the system trusts the current actions to reach the long-term target, or remain tense when feeling pleasure if a deeper target still feels unachieved. In this sense, feeling tone is partly about whether the system models its highly weighted targets as sufficiently handled, not just pleasure. Handled here can mean either changing the world to reach the target, changing the action(s) that are supposed to reach the target, or simply changing the target itself. To be clear, the general claim that bodily state sets a background influence on affect is well supported, but the more specific predictive-processing mappings (like tying neuromodulators like dopamine or serotonin to specific prediction roles) are debated, which I treat more loosely but still consider meaningful. To be clear about the three-layer stack of the compression gradient, all of these things, the physiological factors, are part of the embodied layer that sets the background felt tone, not something separate.

Luckily, we have agency over a portion of this physiological aspect of the background felt tone since it is not exactly fixed. The things that are so far best studied to help are exercise, sleep, morning light, slow breathing, humming, and dancing, but I suspect the literature is far behind what is possible by an individual, so I would recommend not constraining yourself to what the current “science says.” For instance, much of the literature on meditation pertains to novices or relatively inexperienced practitioners, and advanced practitioners/states are understudied, which is a limitation when trying to understand the upper limits of human agency in this area. If you were to experiment, finding ways to reduce background processing demand or increase the system’s available capacity, whether by releasing held contraction (more on this below), loosening rigid priors, or supporting the body so it has greater physiological and psychological capacity, would be a helpful guiding principle.

2.2 Embodiment and Somatic Stabilization

I want to expand on the importance of the body in this framework and explain in more detail how it connects to the dependency tree, the Energetic Triad, and the three layers by showing how the field is physically held and enacted in the body.

First, a definition: a haptic contraction is a felt tightening or clenching in bodily sensation, whether muscular, visceral, or vascular. It is the somatic form that a defended or violated prediction arising from prior structures takes in direct experience. Its counterpart, expansion, is simply the release of this kind of contraction. In this sense, contraction refers more specifically to tension that participates in maintaining or responding to an active field configuration. It is unclear if ALL bodily tension can be mapped to some type of relationship to priors.

(One possible mechanistic account is Michael Edward Johnson’s “vasocomputation” hypothesis, which proposes that active-inference predictions may be physically instantiated through vascular tension. In this account, a contraction first collapses an ambiguous neural pattern into a more definite one, holds it as a form of medium-term memory, and, if sustained, latches into a durable prior that resists updating until the prediction resolves or the latch is released. Through this frame, taṇhā, or craving, is this compression-clench, which is why craving and the somatic contraction that holds it can be understood as the same process expressed through different channels.)

These haptic contractions may match the depth of the priors being held, meaning that deeper priors produce stronger, more persistent, and “physically deeper” contractions. For example, a deep prior held as tension is trunk-level, which is one reason chronic contractions can be so hard to release: releasing them means changing a foundational structure that experience relies on. Tacit knowledge becomes an obvious example here. Deeply conditioned priors can be expressed and stabilized through recurring haptic contraction patterns so they can be enacted. These patterns do not instantiate only single priors, but instead help instantiate whole field configurations, each of which may come with a characteristic pattern of haptic contraction and release that supports its properties like targets and constraints. These patterns may also keep the targets associated with those priors active after the original situation has passed so they can be resolved. By holding unresolved prediction or target error in the embodied layer, they keep attention and action weighted toward signals that might reduce the discrepancy or satisfy the target. In this sense, haptic contraction can function like an embodied bookmark, keeping later perception and action organized around an unfinished target so the error can be reduced. Importantly, a bidirectional feedback loop is running here. Active priors can generate contraction, while the resulting interoceptive signals provide new evidence that the predicted state is real and meaningful. This develops a feedback loop in which the body helps maintain the field that originally organized the body. Episodic recall here is doing something similar in which reinstating the configuration dealing with the memory, the internal representations, reinstates its somatic pattern, since this is how the configuration is enacted.

If we take a look at a mathematician in the middle of solving a problem, their tacit knowledge, embodied through conditioned patterns of haptic contraction, is activated by the current problem and target to form a specific field configuration that comes with its own unique properties. Each new representation, such as a mental image, is then tested against the embodied felt sense of that field, with a negative difference in felt tone signaling that it does not fit and a positive difference signaling fit.

(Again I turn to the vasocomputation hypothesis as well as this tweet by Michael Johnson himself that proposes something very similar, which I did not see until after I put together the above paragraph. He describes these recurring patterns of muscular and vascular tension as “stances,” which closely parallels the claim here that field configurations have characteristic somatic patterns that stabilize predictions and make certain actions available.)

Some tension can be released within seconds because it is associated with priors on outer branches of the tree, while other tension remains stable across many situations because it is associated with deeper, more foundational priors. Noticeable bodily contraction is the somatic expression of a negative difference in felt tone because it arises when the system registers input that conflicts with a held prior or attempts to prevent that prior from being updated. The magnitude and persistence of the contraction may scale with how deep the prior is. When input conflicts with a deep prior, the resulting negative difference in felt tone can be especially strong because updating that prior would require revising many of the structures that depend on it.

Taken together, these three accounts describe the same process from different sides: the dependency tree gives the structure its depth, the Energetic Triad explains the magnitude of the resulting spikes, and haptic contraction provides a physical substrate through which the structure is stabilized and enacted. Depth in the tree predicts how costly a prior is to update and how firmly its associated tension is latched. This means the meditation/contemplative work of releasing contraction and the epistemic work of updating knowledge may be closely related mechanistically, since both loosen the precision and embodied reinforcement that keep a deep prior stable, making the underlying model more available for updating.

As stated earlier, single releases of contraction usually affect outer branches of the dependency tree, but repeated releases can gradually propagate toward the trunk as the field learns that the contraction is no longer necessary. With greater sensory clarity and regulatory slack, meditation allows the system to remain with prediction error without immediately restoring the old contraction through active inference. Over time, this can reduce the precision and embodied reinforcement of deeper priors, making increasingly load-bearing patterns available for updating. This is the same general process through which tacit knowledge develops, but applied in the opposite direction: repeated experience can both embody useful structures and loosen embodied structures that no longer serve their original function. Indeed, this mechanism helps explain why meditation can occasionally produce psychedelic-like moments through the release of deep tension, as psychedelics similarly loosen these rigid, deep priors.

Connecting this to the representational layer, all representations bring their own properties into our field and reweight active priors. A representation imports properties into the field, and the body enacts them via active inference, so the somatic change is downstream of the field re-tuning, not the representation mysteriously touching muscle. It’s important to remember that these changes that the representation brings to the field are typically more surface level, affecting prediction branches further out, so the somatic changes it produces are usually the fast, unlatched kind (you tense or relax in the moment, and it releases when the representation drops), whereas the deep latched contractions that come from deep priors don’t change just because you adopted a new representation. For example, a representation of safety won’t do much if the body remains braced since interoceptive evidence still signals danger. Reframing can shift your state of being momentarily but doesn’t dissolve deep somatic holding unless that framing is held until it can sink deeper into the dependency tree. Now, nuance here is needed since there are instances in which representations, over time, can indeed affect the system deeply, like the practice of ideal parent figure protocol developed by Dr. Daniel P. Brown and David Elliott. In this instance, repeated imagery of attuned caregivers gives the field a stable representation of secure attachment that can be reinstantiated across sessions. In the terms of this framework, repeatedly using that representation while it produces an embodied sense of safety may gradually reduce the precision of older attachment predictions and train the new pattern(s) into the embodied layer, allowing an initially temporary representation to influence deeper priors and their coupled contractions. This results in a new field configuration and destabilizes the old attractor state and its basin, allowing for more flexibility. Importantly, time and repetition alone are not sufficient for a representation to reach deeper priors. The representation must generate trusted evidence and support actions that repeatedly produce outcomes the system can use to override its older predictions. So, representations re-tune the embodied layer when they are used, the body enacts the imported field via active inference, and this typically operates further out on the branches of the dependency tree unless done so at a large enough time scale.

Then moving to the notational layer, we must first remember anything in this layer requires compression of at least the embodied layer. Insight originates as an embodied felt sense and gets compressed into representation or notation, such as Einstein’s “muscular type” thoughts: “the above-mentioned elements are, in my case, of visual and some of muscular type.” Now, since the embodied layer is so involved with this process of bringing forth notation, and we have established that priors are physically held as tension/contraction, then the physical intuition behind these insights is the body’s stored prior structure coming alive as a felt sense. So embodiment-in-the-layer-sense and embodiment-in-the-somatic-sense are the same substrate doing the same thing. We can come to an implied conclusion that pure symbol interaction with no grounded intuition produces no genuine insight. Because notation is always a lossy translation of the embodied, with no felt sense driving the interaction, there’s nothing being compressed and no positive difference in felt tone guiding which interactions are meaningful. We’ve all felt this before: we are reading something, and five minutes later we realize we have not retained almost a single thing because no loop is running to get feedback from our embodied layer; it’s just pure symbol input. This does not mean notational manipulation is useless without the embodied layer, since notation can function as external scaffolding and generate structures that can become embodied later. It’s that notation becomes meaningful to the system through feedback with the representational and embodied layers rather than through notation alone.

Just as an example, we can take the names OpenAI vs ClosedAI. Both these words import different properties into the field, like open being expansive and safe and closed being contracted and guarded, along with all the implications associated with each that propagate through the system. The priors associated with “open” and “closed” run deep and are activated “automatically” here, since the notation is so common that its implications have been trained into the field over lots of repetition. These properties re-tune the field, which is felt somatically by using different patterns of tension to lean closer to open or closed. The field is embodied, so a representation that imports branches associated with “closed” produces actual haptic contraction, and one that imports branches associated with “open” produces a sense of release, even if very subtly. That said, the notation of “open” or “closed” can bring about representations that affect the field, but not always, since the priors associated with open and closed are deep enough to instantiate on their own without a deliberate representation being placed, which is why the embodied orientation can happen before you have consciously pictured anything. Keep in mind all of this is related to the individual and dependent on context and conditioning. “Open” can imply freedom in one field and vulnerability in another, while “closed” can imply restriction or protection depending on the active target.

2.3 Slack

Up to this point slack has been used multiple times without a useful definition: in section 1.2 deep priors needed "sufficient slack" to update and the energetic triad used capacity as a way to measure spent resources before we process input. The quantity and measurement of slack now need to be explored before going further.

Put simply, slack is the portion of the system's capacity not currently committed to holding its active field configuration in place. Capacity here means the system's practical ability to process experience in a given moment, governed through the channels of attention, regulation, representations, physiology, and time. Slack allows competing branches of the dependency tree to be active at the same time instead of closing down on one, which means the system can hold errors as meaningful rather than immediately resolving them through habitual active inference.

Slack is a property of the field, just like felt tone, fit distance, and reachability. It is not a fuel tank that simply depletes with use. "Mental energy runs out" (ego depletion) is not a reliable measurement since it has failed to replicate in the literature. Slack is also not simply metabolic budget, or the ability to "think harder." Your brain is already consuming most of its required energy simply by being awake and functioning, so focused thought only adds a small amount of cost. When mental effort feels too costly to the system, the issue is that the brain decides effort is not worth allocating within the given context. When the system seems to run "out of fuel", the brain has changed how worthwhile an action feels. Factors like pain and fatigue can make the same action feel much harder, causing avoidance or choosing an easier habit to enact. Slack is also not calmness, since a person can appear calm because they have collapsed their options.

Phenomenologically, because slack is a field property and not something external, it is only visible under disturbance. Just as you cannot see through the field itself, you cannot read your own slack from the inside. For instance, how long you take to recover from a disturbance can be a proxy for the amount of slack the system holds in relation to that context, rather than simply staying calm.

Slack is relational, meaning slack is always slack for a specific thing. Remember, it is not simply a fuel tank that holds universally across all contexts; it is in relation to the task at hand. A person may handle intellectual uncertainty well but have little capacity for social rejection. Slack is useless without bearing in mind the demand at hand. This mirrors physiological reserve since reserve is the "difference between the basal level of a system and its maximal capacity to respond." Essentially, it is the gap between what your body is currently doing and the maximum extra demand it can withstand before it stops recovering normally. This also makes environment important, since environment can raise slack by lowering the cost of holding a field configuration, like being with a trusted person. Furthermore, just as slack is relational and responds to certain patterns, a contracted latch in the body is slack permanently used somewhere. It is capacity spent holding one interpretation as fixed, which means less available action and further updating. So releasing a deep latch in the body is not adding something new to experience, but releasing a taxing component that gives more room to the system.

When it comes to environment, predictability matters since it reduces how much spare capacity you need to operate. If actions and interpretations arrive predictably, like having a daily routine, you can operate close to your slack limit without being overwhelmed. Routines and rituals therefore function like slack by reducing surprise the system would have to compute for. Chaotic environments that are unpredictable require more unused slack to handle the same task. The same person would need more slack in a volatile context to do the same updating as one within a predictable context.

Slack is important because a system with little to no slack acts out existing habits and priors. With no capacity to hold error or competing branches in the dependency tree, the system will revert to a deep configuration and increase precision on existing models rather than exploring and considering alternatives. This implies that low slack simplifies experience before it fragments it. With low slack, the field will collapse competing distinctions it would otherwise hold, first dropping the ones least relevant to a current dominant target. This is why a depleted person’s world goes flat and simplified rather than chaotic. In section 1.3, an asymmetry was noted: collapsing many-to-one is easier than expanding one-to-many; this is because slack is needed to expand. Low slack also narrows diversity of interpretations and options rather than completely closing them in general. A system under threat with low slack is not simply shut off from doing anything; the system will simply update fast towards things that it is already oriented towards. Other options become unavailable; the only thing that matters is going towards the already known thing.

Again, slack is not just one simple number; it is context dependent. Sleep loss can wreck sustained attention but keep up reasoning, and stress can impair flexibility while improving inhibition. There is no "how much slack do I have" number. Instead we might measure it across different dimensions that can interact with each other, like physiology, attentional and working-memory capacity, expected controllability, availability of alternative configurations, environmental and social support, and time to consolidate. Interestingly, some dimensions can "substitute" for one another, like external notation replacing working memory. Because of this, these different dimensions of slack open up different phases of change. At one point you might need enough capacity for attentional mechanisms to work in a certain way to create change, while at another point you might need enough time and safety so a new pattern can stabilize. Simply saying we need "more slack" is too vague; what matters is the interaction type between old and new patterns.

Slack is bound by reachability. It only matters if you can access it from your current state. You might be intelligent and flexible while working, but these abilities can become unreachable when stressed or anxious. The capacity itself exists across certain configurations (since slack is a property), but it is bound to certain causes and conditions and cannot always be accessed in patterns where you are stuck. Capacity that is not held within the configuration you need is not that helpful.

When it comes to slack, having several different ways to achieve the same field configuration is more robust and helpful than having several copies of one path. For instance, three breathing techniques are mostly redundant because anxiety could impair all three, while diverse paths like breaking, walking, and social contact utilize different systems, so there is more of a chance one will work. Diverse paths protect you against problems you might not anticipate, while similar paths tend to fail for the same reason. If there is a novel disturbance in the system you have not planned for, one reliable path may be "blocked" and capacity will be withheld.

Slack can become needlessly spent if not kept in line. The mind tends to use any spare capacity to keep predicting and solving for uncertainty, so when "extra" openness appears, the system may spend it by collapsing on another interpretation or action instead of holding it open. Equanimity, as discussed later, has a job here: it lets several possibilities remain open without collapse or a rush to resolve them. This allows slack to exist to be spent wisely instead of being instantly consumed by the system's optimization function.

3. Learning and Expertise

3.1 Metaphors

One of the main ways an abstract thing becomes meaningful to the mind-body system is through metaphor. Metaphor is a translation mechanism that dates back to the origins of language and is a part of the representational layer. Philosophers have long argued that early humans did not invent literal words first and embellish them later; rather, they used the physical world around them as an immediate landscape to grasp abstract realities. This marks the importance of metaphor: it is one of the clearest forms of content within the representational layer.

In Metaphors We Live By, George Lakoff and Mark Johnson establish an important thesis: “Metaphor is pervasive in everyday life, not just in language but in thought and action. Our ordinary conceptual system, in terms of which we both think and act, is fundamentally metaphorical in nature.” What they mean is that metaphors are not merely poetic devices or devices used in language, but they are unconscious structures that shape how we perceive reality, interact with others, and make decisions. We use language because we conceive of things that way, and we act according to how we conceive of them.

Returning to the example of “argument is war,” when we are having a disagreement, we can experience the argument as a battle. This is the act of conceiving a thing a certain way, which changes our actions due to changing field configurations. Using the war metaphor, our actions jump to winning and defending, the same way we would when fighting an enemy. If we were to instead use the metaphor “argument is a dance” on a societal level, our behavior might change to a more balanced and aesthetically pleasing interaction. The main point stands: metaphor in the representational layer shapes both the notational and embodied layers through language and behavior.

The embodied layer here plays a sneaky but important role: it is where metaphor earns its place. “Argument is war” works because you already know, in your body, what war-like conflict feels like, such as the bracing and the charge. “Time is money” works because you have dealt with scarce resources and felt the weight of using and running out. The metaphor makes the abstract felt sense intelligible by mapping it onto embodied structure; it is not arbitrary. This is what Johnson calls an image schema in The Body in the Mind: a recurring bodily pattern like containment, balance, or moving along a path, laid down by physical experience before any words. A felt sense that has no internal representation of its own can be made explicit through metaphor by using the embodied layer as crucial feedback. So the chain runs bottom to top, where the embodied layer supplies the felt sense, metaphor maps that embodied structure onto an abstract target to build a representation, and that representation is expressed through notation as language and enacted through the embodied layer as behavior. Without the embodied layer grounding it, the metaphor would not mean anything to the system; it would be hollow. The reason “argument is war” can move us to defend and attack is that the body already knows what defending and attacking feel like. We can also trace the stack back down as we process the notation, moving into the image of war, which activates the embodied characteristics associated with war. To give one more example, “feeling down” or “being on top of the world” relies on your physical experience of gravity, where standing upright can feel powerful and collapsing can feel disempowering.

Going back to mathematics to demonstrate metaphor’s importance for understanding, we see that mathematics is a domain in which many of its objects are not directly sensory (you can’t touch a number), which is why understanding them often depends on grounding through metaphor and the embodied layer. This is why mathematicians reach for spatial and physical language constantly. As William Thurston puts it, “People have amazing facilities for sensing something without knowing where it comes from (intuition); for sensing that some phenomenon or situation or object is like something else (association); and for building and testing connections and comparisons, holding two things in mind at the same time (metaphor). These facilities are quite important for mathematics. Personally, I put a lot of effort into ‘listening’ to my intuitions and associations, and building them into metaphors and connections. This involves a kind of simultaneous quieting and focusing of my mind. Words, logic, and detailed pictures rattling around can inhibit intuitions and associations.” This illustrates why the notational layer is the “weakest” of the three in terms of richness: it can never capture the full felt sense of the underlying internal representation.

As a reminder, the representational layer is not only composed of metaphor. What matters is the general principle shared across the entire layer, which is that understanding is built by constructing a representation that fits the field. This could be a metaphor taken from the body, an image, or a mental simulation. Metaphor is simply the clearest usage of representation because it maps bodily structure onto the abstract, but it is just one method among several for developing representations within the layer where understanding actually resides.

3.2 Intuition

Intuition is your priors, your implicit structure, speaking directly. It is the field generating a salient representation or feeling before any explicit derivation. It is salient because the field gives it enough precision to make it worth attending to. Intuition is not experienced as algorithmic, but is a valuable signal about experience. The result is fast and pre-verbal, and it does not land in the notational layer, but in either the representational or embodied layer. Intuition is not itself a fit signal: a fit signal reports whether something satisfies the current field, while intuition is the rapid process by which something salient is generated. Results of intuition, like having a feeling that two things connect, are handed to you by the field, which is why an externalization or representation generated from intuition can reveal information about experience.

Intuition reaches toward possibilities through positive differences in felt tone and pulls back through negative differences. This is why a feeling of something being “off” is still a matter of intuition: the field is handing you a pre-verbal embodied assessment of fit before you have derived it step by step.

Neuroscientist Patrick House recounts a story about a young military driver taking an officer down a dangerous road in Iraq who suddenly slammed the brakes and turned around, saying, “I don’t have a good feeling.” In this framework, his trained field may have detected a violation: the road was unusually silent. This violation then surfaced as an embodied “off” feeling through a negative difference in felt tone, produced by the external input conflicting with the field. Hours later, the driver compressed that embodied feeling into notation, suggesting that the mothers may have kept their children inside because they expected danger. This is exactly what intuition is: the trained field delivering an embodied, pre-verbal feeling before notation can name the properties of the field being violated. This is the same mechanism that can contribute to “aha” moments. It is background field processing generating a result, just occurring on different time horizons.

There exists an optional step after this pre-verbal intuition surfaces, which is explicitly naming what properties in the field have been satisfied or violated. Sometimes this is easy, as in, “I have a feeling that these two things connect because of X and Y”; at other times, we must derive the properties slowly, by hand. When this explicit step occurs, we are compressing that intuition up into notation, which will not capture the entirety of it. Articulability of an intuition measures how compressible it is, not whether it is an intuition in the first place. If we do not have the proper notational knowledge in place, it will be harder to name that intuition since we don’t have the proper ways into which to compress it.

This also explains why intuition can be right or wrong. The answer lies in our field, and luckily our field can be fine-tuned through how we shape it. Untrained intuition, like that of a novice, is a rapid output from default priors that are not well tuned to the current context. Trained intuition, however, like that of an expert, is a rapid output from a field that is well tuned to what the context calls for, hence why the tacit knowledge of expertise is so valuable: it is the actionable expression of these intuitions that have been conditioned substantially. As David Bessis puts it, “Mathematical intuition is the same intuition we use every day, but developed and solidified by its confrontation with language and logic.” This confrontation has a name that I like to call “the loop of refinement.” Without this loop, one may confidently believe that an intuition from a miscalibrated field is correct, since it can feel identical to an intuition coming from a calibrated field. Intuition is only as trustworthy as the field it comes from, which means a refinement loop is needed to calibrate it well.

Mathematics makes this process especially visible because it forces pre-verbal intuition to be translated into explicit notation and then tested through formal proof.

3.3 Mathematics

To develop a deeper picture of The Compression Gradient, we can turn to mathematics, which can reveal what the phenomenology of traversing these layers is like. Fortunately, many great mathematicians and mathematical thinkers have given us subjective reports of what it feels like to use these layers in practice. Ironically, they are reporting on their own tacit knowledge, so these reports will be lossy.

Let’s start with one of the most well-known figures in this space, Einstein. In his response to Jacques Hadamard’s survey of mathematicians’ mental processes, he wrote: “The words or the language, as they are written or spoken, do not seem to play any role in my mechanism of thought. The psychical entities which seem to serve as elements in thought are certain signs and more or less clear images which can be ‘voluntarily’ reproduced and combined.” He continues: “this combinatory play seems to be the essential feature in productive thought, before there is any connection with logical construction in words or other kinds of signs which can be communicated to others.” On the secondary status of notation, he wrote: “The above-mentioned elements are, in my case, of visual and some of muscular type. Conventional words or other signs have to be sought for laboriously only in a secondary stage.” Jacques Hadamard framed the survey around this question: “It would be very helpful for the purpose of psychological investigation to know what internal or mental images, what kind of ‘internal words’ mathematicians make use of; whether they are motor, auditory, visual, or mixed.” Taken together, these reports suggest that at least some epistemic invention is largely pre-verbal, with notation and language coming at a later stage in the process.

Next, let us turn to Alexander Grothendieck. In Récoltes et Semailles, Grothendieck gives us a vivid account of the representational layer: “What drives and dominates my work, its soul and reason for being, are the mental images formed during the course of the work to apprehend the reality of mathematical things... All my life I’ve been unable to read a mathematical text, however trivial or simple it may be, unless I’m able to give this text a ‘meaning’ in terms of my experience of mathematical things, that is unless the text arouses in me mental images, intuitions that will give it life.” Later, he says, “Interrogating things, listening to the voice of things, means trying to imagine them, examining the mental images that form within you, seeking to solidify these images and make them clearer, working at unveiling more and more details, as when you try to recall a dream.” And: “[…] my fundamental difficulty in following the thought of others, without first translating it into my own images, and rethinking it in my own style.” What Grothendieck is most obviously trying to communicate, just like Einstein, is that the representational layer is being manipulated more directly than the notational layer. The notation may be changed, such as by writing or deleting parts of a formula, but it is the representational knowledge that is being expressed and simultaneously updated. This is one example of the bidirectional feedback between representation and notation described earlier.

A more practical example comes from Terence Tao, a leading contemporary mathematician, who shows how a shared internal representation lets two people communicate with far less notation while maintaining richness: “I find there is a world of difference between explaining things to a colleague, and explaining things to a close collaborator. With the latter, one really can communicate at the intuitive level, because one already has a reasonable idea of what the other person’s mental model of the problem is. In some ways, I find that throwing out things to a collaborator is closer to the mathematical thought process than just thinking about maths on one’s own, if that makes any sense.” What he’s trying to say is that if two collaborators already share a vivid internal representation of the thing they are communicating about, their communication can be compressed more heavily because each can reconstruct structure that the other leaves unstated. It is possible to share a rough gesture, and the other person can decompress it correctly using the shared internal representation they already carry. With a peer or someone you are just conversing with, you may lack that shared internal representation, so more of the structure must be made explicit in notation, and the other person must reconstruct a model from that still-lossy expression. Shared context lets you transmit the representational and embodied layers more easily through notation, since the structure of the content does not need to be rebuilt from scratch. The more shared models two people have, the more heavily they can compress their communication without losing usable structure.

Lastly, there is David Bessis and his book Mathematica. He says that “the magic power of mathematicians isn’t logic but intuition,” and that the written proofs we see are downstream products, while the central activity is developing inner intuition through mental imagery, just as Grothendieck describes. Bessis places great stock in intuition; it is the actionable component of layers coming alive within the present context. He argues, “Secret math, also known as mathematical intuition, can be found in the heads of mathematicians. It consists of mental representations and abstract sensations, often visual, that are for them quite obvious, and that give them a great deal of pleasure. But when it comes to sharing these sensations with the rest of the world, mathematicians are often at a loss. What had seemed so evident to them is suddenly less so. To transcribe their ideas, mathematicians have had to invent that esoteric language and those indecipherable symbols, just as musicians had to invent a complex musical notation in order to transcribe their compositions.” That sounds a lot like traversing the Compression Gradient! Mathematicians must, as Bessis puts it, transcribe their representations and intuitions in a legible way so others can understand what they are holding internally. Without this compression, collaboration would not be possible and progress would be lost, so it is a matter of pragmatism. In terms of this framework, Bessis gives the embodied and representational layers a central role when he states, “mathematicians’ intuition is their masterpiece, their lifetime accomplishment.” The vast and rich bottom two layers are so important to this account that the trained intuition they support becomes the mathematician’s deeper accomplishment. I highly recommend Mathematica if you want to learn more about intuition and the representational layer and how they apply to doing mathematics, especially at a high level.

Based on these reports, it is not unreasonable to say that many great mathematicians agree on an implicit truth: notation is not the whole difficult part of the process; it is also knowing when and how the internal representation corresponds with mathematical reality or truth. We see this in Grothendieck’s work to “solidify these images and make them clearer,” Bessis developing intuition until it becomes obvious and provides “a great deal of pleasure,” and Einstein combining images in “combinatory play.” These reports do not provide a complete formal rule or system for generating and evaluating those images. In the pre-formal stage, there is not always an explicit methodology for checking whether a mental image has captured the thing accurately; there is often first an internal sense of direction, like a compass. Formal verification, like proof writing, becomes possible as the intuition is compressed into notation, though feedback from notation can then revise the intuition. This compass lies below the notational layer, most of the time below articulation. Its signal does not guarantee truth; as the intuition section argued, it is only as reliable as the field that produces it. This leads us to a signal the compass gives, something we might call resonance.

3.4 Resonance & Friction

To talk about the importance of resonance, we must turn to Eugene Gendlin. Gendlin trained as a philosopher and later became widely known for his work in psychotherapy and Focusing. In the 1950s, he worked with Carl Rogers’s group at the University of Chicago, using a clinical setting to study how people symbolize experience and how felt experience becomes words, concepts, and change. Gendlin himself thought many therapy theories were too focused on content, such as “the client has hostility.” He did not declare the content wrong; instead, he believed that descriptions alone do not explain how change actually happens. In “A Theory of Personality Change,” he argued that knowing what is wrong is different from the felt process of changing it: “Yet it is clear that there is a major difference between knowing the conceptual explanation of personality (which one can devise in a few hours) and the actual feeling process of changing (which often requires years).” His research moved from what the client talks about to how the client talks and senses while talking. A client can express the same thing in two completely different ways depending on their bodily connection and presence. Experiencing is about the client’s manner of relating to experience, not only the content of speech. Skipping ahead, Gendlin and his colleagues arrived at a central finding in their research: clients who improved paused, turned their attention inward, sensed something unclear but bodily present, searched for words that fit it, and then felt some kind of movement or shift. This is the core of his process known as Focusing.

Next, we should turn to the felt sense, a term coined by Gendlin. The felt sense is not just an emotion; it is a vague, holistic bodily sense of a situation. A common example of the felt sense is when, while talking to someone, a murky feeling comes up in the body, somewhere like the stomach. It’s not yet a clear thought; it’s more like a bodily holding of the situation before language finds a way to compress it. This sense might make you feel like something is off or that you want to exit the conversation, but the feeling is not yet entirely clear to you. This should be differentiated from felt tone since the felt sense is the implicit bodily whole of a situation, while felt tone is its positive, negative, or neutral quality.

Working from the felt sense, Gendlin then named the felt shift. This is when you find a word, image, or sentence, which he calls a handle, that fits the felt sense, and something in you changes physically or emotionally. This feels like a release of contraction resulting in expansion, a sort of lightness. This, for Gendlin, is where the real change happens. The person experiences a whole new way of relating to the thing they were holding onto. We can extrapolate this beyond a clinical setting to a broader pair of phenomena called resonance and friction. Resonance is a noticeable positive felt response that occurs when an input fits the field and integrates more cheaply than expected. Friction is the corresponding noticeable negative felt response that occurs when an input costs more to integrate than expected. Because an input’s relation to a target can also produce positive or negative felt tone, not every pleasant response is resonance and not every unpleasant response is friction.

Moreover, friction can also signal that a constraint is blocking movement toward a valued attractor or that targets at different levels of the system are in conflict. This goes back to the field section of this framework. If friction persists after adjustments of a current state, then the reachability of the state you are trying to reach needs to be resurrected in a manner that makes more viable pathways available. Keep in mind that these two generators of friction, barriers to attractor states and conflicting targets, still contain the underlying principle that the attempted transition through the field costs more than the system predicted.

Resonance is that feeling when a word or name is on the tip of your tongue, and once it is found and said out loud, it feels like a relief. There is a shift like, “Oh yeah, I got it, so I can move on now.” Before finding the right name, someone else might say other options out loud, like “Is it X, Y, or Z?” while the response is, “No, no, not that one.” Finally, once the other person names it, it feels like a matching puzzle piece for your current understanding. During the initial attempt to think of the name, there was a holding onto something, an attempt to search through a vast internal landscape of intuition, as Bessis would put it. Then, when the correct name comes out, there is an obvious felt sense of correctness. To quote Grothendieck, “It is the naming that makes the awareness that has appeared irreversible, ineffaceable… [it is] a silent welcoming of a knowledge appearing in deeper layers of our being, where thought has no access.”

(In a related manner, the Bio-Emotive Framework by Dr. Douglas Tataryn uses what it calls Resonance Testing to identify and express feelings, sometimes producing expansion or crying.)

Crucially, resonance is only a felt sense of fit with our current field, not an objective truth-seeking mechanism. What one finds resonant is highly individualized and dependent on conditioning. Of course, in some ways, we are all conditioned by many of the same things, especially if we are part of the same culture. The felt sense of rightness is partly driven by how fluently an answer comes to mind, not by whether it is factually correct. As the common saying goes, “repetition builds intuition.” If we are constantly repeating something that is factually wrong but fits according to our embodied layer, an image can still produce resonance even if it is not based on “truth.” This is visible in trauma-related hypervigilance, where a threat-oriented field may make safe cues unexpectedly costly to integrate while making threat interpretations unusually fluent. In the same way, we might carry an incorrect representation of a math formula, but that image still remains resonant because we have yet to update it accordingly. This updating occurs through the loop of refinement which will be described later. Friction is equally dependent on the current field because it indicates that something was unexpectedly costly to integrate, not that the input was objectively false. A true but unfamiliar idea may produce friction, while a false but conditioned one may produce resonance. Similarly, an incorrect representation may remain fluent and easy to integrate because it fits a field configuration, but it produces resonance only when it integrates more cheaply than expected.

Returning to the field ontology of this framework, resonance and friction are pre-verbal embodied responses to input being tested against the field’s current configuration. They are changes in felt tone, not the same thing as Gendlin’s general felt sense. Resonance and friction are the two opposing directions an integration-cost difference can take, with the former signaling input that integrates more cheaply than expected and the latter signaling input that costs more to integrate than expected. When an input satisfies the field’s active properties, it fits; if it also integrates more cheaply than expected, we feel resonance. When an input violates or strains a sufficient property and consequently costs more to integrate than expected, we feel friction, even before we can name what was violated. Because there is always a default field active through deep, fundamental priors like “thing-ness,” there is a background feeling of fit in experience, in which things are constantly tested for fit and happen to satisfy the field. Because of this, an obvious sense of “clicking” or feeling “off” reflects how far an input’s felt response deviates from the constant background baseline, with deeper priors producing stronger resonance or friction. As the Energetic Triad established earlier, resonance and friction can be understood as opposing differences between predicted and actual integration cost.

Importantly within the field, resonance and friction also can change the field’s configuration. Under active inference, these two properties can change what happens next, which therefore has feedback loops of action that can reinforce interpretations and behavior. Resonance has the ability to increase the precision of the successful branch in the dependency tree taken pertaining to the input, which then reinforces precision and capacity allocated. Friction can redirect precision away from the branch taken, which can result in updating models or taking action in another direction. Both of these things reinforce a loop of integration and action that retunes the field in the direction of where precision is steered.

To put this in the frame of The Compression Gradient, resonance and friction can become especially useful signals when moving between layers. Each time you compress a felt sense up into words or decompress someone’s words back down into your own embodied layer, resonance or friction can tell you whether the translation integrates more or less successfully than expected. The felt shift can signal a successful move between layers, where the sentence corresponds with your field and fits the embodied material more successfully than expected. When a move costs more to complete than expected, friction is introduced. Friction means the system sensed something that violates a constraint of the field; the attempt to find fit with input in the layer below or above the one you are moving from does not produce a correspondence that works without additional processing. Because the two lower layers are not fully legible from the outside, resonance and friction act as internal compasses for figuring out what works and what does not. They guide exploration, but they do not replace external testing or formal verification.

This is where the field’s “fit distance” property matters. This property tells you whether an input sits closer to or farther from the field’s current configuration. Take the tip-of-the-tongue example. There might be active variables and constraints like “the name starts with a certain letter” or “the name is of a celebrity.” If an externalization is presented that violates these properties, it will not resonate with the current field. The presented name sits at the far end of the fit-distance spectrum, so it fails as an input for the current search even if it remains salient. If processing that mismatch costs more than expected, the candidate may also produce friction. Fit distance affects whether an input can integrate, but closeness alone does not guarantee resonance and distance alone does not guarantee friction; the cost must differ noticeably from what the field predicted.

This also explains the experience of feeling that something is “off” about an externalization. In this case, the system detects friction but has not yet built an explicit representation of the cause of that friction. The embodied layer registers this negative difference in felt tone before the representational layer can identify which variable or constraint was violated. There is noticeable friction but no located cause yet. Having no explanation for the friction does not mean the signal is a failure; it simply means an explanation has not yet been constructed. A clear example of this is demonstrated in the TV show Mad Men, season 3, episode 2. In one scene, the team produces a commercial and shows it to a client. Both the client and, later, one of the ad men, Harry Crane, agree that it is a good commercial but that something about it is off. None of them, however, can say what makes it feel off. Ultimately, the client rejects the commercial even though it is almost exactly what they asked for and regards it as a failure. Both the client and Harry are describing the embodied layer registering a mismatch between the ad and their felt sense of what it should be, so the system registers friction. However, the key point is that no one has activated an explicit representation that can locate which property is producing the friction. They can feel that something was violated without first having to name it or put it into a representation. It could be said that this is part of the point of hiring experts like these in the first place: they can detect that variables or constraints were violated in a field before they can necessarily determine which ones. They can turn the vague felt sense of something being off into concrete work that identifies and reduces that friction. The power of a well-calibrated field is that it can register subtle contextual violations quickly, though locating their cause may require further work.

Importantly, checking for fit internally does not always happen slowly or consciously. A representation does not need to show up as a vivid image or a live simulation for resonance or friction to occur. However, implicit or rapid fit does not automatically count as resonance, and an ordinary expected mismatch does not automatically count as friction. In speech especially, which is a form of notation, the checking process often runs implicitly and quickly. Deep priors and repeated use matter because using a cached expression like “how are you doing” means the system does not need to actively search for whether it fits. The representation is either very “small,” like a quick flash, or bypassed because the expression has become a cached output. Because this expression is expected to integrate cheaply, it will usually sink into the baseline rather than produce resonance. However, when someone is speaking from a felt sense, the process changes. When speaking, successive words or sentences may be checked against the field in real time. When a person pauses and says something like, “What I’m trying to say is…,” they are feeling friction between the words they just expressed and the active field. When a later phrase lands unexpectedly well, the resulting positive difference becomes noticeable as resonance. Fluent externalizations such as cached speech can appear to skip activating an explicit representation, but repeated use and conditioning make them fit the field so reliably that the checking happens quickly and implicitly, without becoming noticeable.

To hit on a point made earlier about background compression and decompression, it seems as though most “aha!” moments of insight are resonant. This is because, as our system works in the background without conscious direction, it implicitly reorganizes the field and the representations active within it. When the embodied layer registers this update as an unexpectedly successful fit, it releases the small cognitive tension it was holding, just as a well-fitting handle does when it lands. A sudden moment of clarity in the shower is the system updating itself, which feels resonant, and because it is going on in the background rather than through our steering, it is a surprise when it happens. This is why the process is something like this: you’re not really thinking about the thing → a sudden insight comes that feels resonant → you say something out loud like “finally,” which is not the insight itself but the notational expression of the cognitive tension being released, resulting in a sense of expansion. As a reminder, resonance means an unexpectedly successful fit, not factual correctness. A moment of insight may fit your system at the moment, but that does not mean the insight cannot later be updated.

An interesting thing about resonance and friction is that they fluctuate temporally. This means the same object can be resonant one day and less so the next or produce friction in one context but not another. I can cry when I watch a film because something in it resolves or fits unexpectedly well, and when I rewatch it the next month, I can feel close to nothing. As our field is changing moment to moment on varying scales based on context, it makes sense that different properties will be salient at different points in time, which determines whether we feel resonance or friction. A wide variety of things seem to influence this, like sleep, mood, recent input, physical state, current worries, the environment and physical context, and novelty or repetition. Considering the field is a deployment of our priors that are weighted moment to moment, it makes sense that salient properties will change based on the specific context. It also seems like the integration of a thing into our field, say a film, influences the intensity of felt senses, since the differences lose strength. Watching the same film over and over can generally cause the stream of input to be integrated into the baseline tone, which lessens the spike in felt sense and makes the film feel dull because it is predicted to be easy to integrate due to repetition. Potential for resonance can also return once lost if the properties of the current field are ones that make the input resonate. Maybe a year later, I watch the film again, and a recent life event allows old disconnected material to organize through it in a new way, producing resonance.

3.5 The Loop of Refinement

One must start with error and lead it over into truth. That is, one must uncover the source of the error; otherwise hearing the truth is useless to us. It cannot enter as long as something else occupies its place. To convince someone of the truth, it is not enough to state the truth; one must find the path from error to truth. I must again and again dive beneath the water of doubt.

Ludwig Wittgenstein, Remarks on Frazer’s Golden Bough

We have two facts that make refinement necessary: resonance does not guarantee factual correctness, and notation is always lossy because it compresses knowledge as it moves up the layers. An internal representation can feel right but still not correspond with reality. Understanding therefore cannot be a single act of finding the right expression; it must be part of a greater whole, something like a loop of refinement.

The felt sense of “I understand this” is not enough. Take educational YouTube videos, which have become a tragedy of modern learning. A viewer may have a sense of understanding the thing, yet if they were to write an essay on what they learned or understood, the paper might remain mostly blank. This is why the testing effect is so important. Because of cognitive limitations, we must find a loop to ensure our understanding is being correctly iterated upon and tested for correspondence with reality. This active iteration is one central mechanism of learning.

This loop tests three things that are important for understanding: fit, coherence, and truth. Fit is how well a probe satisfies the current field; coherence is how well your model holds together internally; and truth is correspondence with reality. Accurate correspondence means something like the way you model the problem being in accordance with the actual shape of the thing, which gives intended/wanted feedback from reality. This requires keeping standard nuances in mind like framing effects and the fact that the map is never the territory, etc.

There are two ways of running the loop, either open or closed. A closed loop tests a representation using properties already available within the system, like when thinking about an idea on a walk by yourself. An open loop introduces information or constraints from outside the current model, like working with a tutor to learn a subject. To note, just because you are running an open loop does not mean you are inviting correspondence with reality. Someone like a flat-earther could indeed be running an open loop but arrive at obviously false claims. Open loops make external correction possible, but do not guarantee it. What improves the chance of moving toward correspondence with reality is a complex combination of accurate feedback, falsifiable probes, beliefs favoring truth, and enough slack for contradictory evidence to be integrated rather than defended against.

In totality, this loop looks something like this:

felt sense → internal or external probe → test against the field → resonance, friction, or baseline → locate the source of the response → update representation → new felt sense → ↻

As established earlier, intuition needs this loop to become well calibrated and attuned to the relevant context you are working within. However, the loop also needs intuition to run; it is a contingent relationship. Feeling resonance or friction is not the external feedback itself, but the change in felt tone produced once the field interprets that feedback. Locating the source of friction may involve intuition catching which properties were violated or satisfied, deliberate analysis, or using both (which is common). Intuition and the loop work together as you use intuition to generate and interpret each pass, and each pass tunes the field which then reshapes intuition.

It is important to note that we do not only use this loop when we deliberately sit down to learn or think. Implicit re-ordering happens constantly and mostly below immediate awareness, since sensory input is continuously being tested against the field and small updates occur as we move through the world. Most passes of the loop are quick and implicit and never come to be recognized as an explicit turn, like the micro-adjustments we make when interacting with others. Deliberate usage of this refinement loop is a more specific version of this process where a representation is kept stable and scrutinized enough to be interacted with, like when writing an essay or solving a math problem.

Relatedly, closed and open loops also run in parallel rather than only one being run individually. You can be changing a representation internally by imagining how something works, and in the next moment external feedback can affect your modeling of the thing through contradiction. The field integrates both channels nearly in parallel. The loop is therefore better modeled as many overlapping cycles happening continuously at different temporal variations and differing levels of awareness. The clean process laid out here is a simplification of one pass compared to the more dense and mostly automatic version that exists “in the real world.” Still, understanding its core mechanics allows us to use it deliberately and wisely.

Felt sense

We must begin with our felt sense of understanding, our current model of how a thing works, which is experienced first as an embodied sense. This is how the field sits in the body at a given moment; it’s our most immediate access to where the layers currently stand, although it can still be incomplete. The felt sense includes the default field and any additional properties introduced by representations, like using a metaphor to describe your current embodied sense of what you know. These are Gendlin’s findings at play.

Internal or external probe

A probe is an expression of what your felt sense is “saying.” It is a candidate expression of what the field currently holds, like “I think this works like this…” or “A metaphor I would use to describe this is…” It does not matter yet if this probe is factually correct; what matters is that it expresses your current understanding clearly enough to be tested. As Wittgenstein puts it, “One must start with error and lead it over into truth.”

There are two ways this probe can manifest: internal or external. An internal probe is run inside the mind using a representation like a simulation or an image. An external probe compresses the felt sense of understanding into notation, like writing about it.

To present a probe is to objectify your current model. You are making it a stable object you can inspect and manipulate. We do this internally by turning attention toward the representation we are holding or externally by translating it into notation. Externalization is especially powerful because it makes the probe legible enough for other people and systems.

Roger Thisdell offers a useful structural parallel in his streamlined enlightenment protocol. He argues that before a phenomenon can be deobjectified, it must first be objectified, isolated clearly enough that the mind can recognize it as an object of experience. Only then can it be seen through as empty, contingent, and not ultimately solid. In non-meditation jargon, before we can deconstruct our perception of something, we must first bring it into immediate experience and make sure our system registers it as an inspectable object.

The epistemic loop uses an analogous move. To refine embodied or representational knowledge, we must first objectify it by making it stable enough for the mind to inspect. Attention can do this internally by sustaining a representation like an image, while notation can do it externally through a fixed sentence. Thisdell’s deobjectification and epistemic testing are not identical operations since his work concerns contemplative action which is geared towards phenomenological changes, while this loop tests where a model breaks against internal or external properties. The shared principle is that implicit material must first be isolated before its construction can be examined.

Remember that externalizing is lossy because of compression, so forming a probe does not capture the full essence of understanding, but makes part of it usable and inspectable. If your understanding is not rich enough yet, the externalization will be especially imprecise because the knowledge has not yet developed deeply within the bottom two layers.

Working memory places an additional limit on the internal loop because, in simple unchunked tasks, we can typically hold only around three or four items or chunks in focal attention at once. This limitation is one reason a vague felt sense of understanding can be misleading: the system may be holding only a highly compressed outline rather than the whole structure. To work around this constraint, we can offload items into external notation or develop organized chunks in long-term memory. As Justin Skycak puts it, “Whereas a beginner perceives individual isolated pieces of information, an expert perceives ‘chunks’ of information organized into meaningful patterns and structures. These chunks are physically encoded as wiring in the expert’s long-term memory, and they are the building blocks that make up the expert’s representation of what they’re looking at in working memory.” Externalization and expertise therefore extend the loop in complementary ways: one adds structure into the environment, while the other compresses it into retrievable internal structure.

Test the probe: resonance, friction, or baseline

This part covers how the field responds when the probe is tested. If the probe produces resonance, this does not mean the probe is entirely truthful; the field itself may not correspond with reality. It simply means that the probe integrated more cheaply or satisfyingly than the system expected, producing a positive difference in felt tone. If we hit friction, we are encountering input that is more costly or difficult to integrate than expected, producing a negative difference in felt tone. If the probe fits about as expected, the response may remain at baseline and provide little noticeable signal at all.

Because resonance and friction are differences from the background baseline, neither is guaranteed to appear every time a probe fits or fails to fit. A baseline response may mean that the probe is unsurprising, which does not produce a noticeable spike.

There are four places where a mismatch can become detectable. The first is the embodied layer: you might write a sentence explaining something and, when you do, your body disagrees. There’s a slight twinge that tells you to try again. Second is the representational layer: through externalizing the model, it becomes clear what is missing, how your representation was incomplete, or how your memory is not up to date. Third is the notational layer: the sentence has implications or contradictions you could not have registered while it was abstract. Lastly, external evidence can produce friction after being interpreted by the field. This can happen by sending a probe out and having the world push back, such as a tutor saying it is wrong, or by taking in new information that contradicts your model. In this final case, external reality gives the constraint, while friction is the internal felt response to it.

It is important to parse out these four locations and categorize the first three as closed, meaning the model is tested using internally available constraints, and the fourth as open, meaning a constraint enters from outside the current model. Closed loops primarily test fit and coherence, while open loops can test correspondence with reality. Even then, the outside source may itself be mistaken, so open feedback must also be evaluated.

This testing can be fast and implicit, like saying a phrase out loud, such as “How are you?” and seeing how it lands in the system, or receiving feedback from others and immediately wondering, “Why did they say that?”

Locate the source of friction or resonance

Because friction simply tells you something is off, not what is off, we need a diagnostic step to understand how we can precisely update our understanding. With this step, we can know that something has failed to integrate and then investigate whether our model is actually what needs to change.

We first must locate where the response arose. Is it coming from the embodied, representational, or notational layer, or was it triggered by external evidence? Is the friction coming from some new information I just read or from my internal representation not being complete? We can also locate which field property is being violated, like a variable or constraint. For example, “This variable cannot be this, so that is where the friction is coming from.”

As a caveat, just because friction occurs does not mean updating needs to happen. The response could be coming from mistaken notation, a body in fight-or-flight mode, or a misreading of the question. With a diagnosis, we can better determine whether our model is incorrect and ensure that we are updating in light of accuracy.

This step is one place where expertise matters: within a well-calibrated domain, experts can locate the source of friction much faster and more accurately than a novice. Because they understand their field so well, the violated properties become clearer, and they can surgically manipulate the things causing the friction. Expertise does not make the response truthful, however, especially outside the domain and context in which that intuition was trained.

The other side of locating the source of friction is locating where resonance is coming from. I would argue this part is not as needed to train understanding, but it is helpful in conditioning what your system finds good. For example, if I generate a metaphor in my head that I then explain and it checks out as being accurate, it might be helpful to assess why this is. Is it the feedback loops that made it a good model? What about its variables? This can then let us in on different principles that could be extrapolated elsewhere, like, “Okay, this shape takes this form when X or Y, so I can apply this to this other problem.” This is training intuition on the resonant side.

If the response stays at baseline, a new question comes to light that asks: Was the probe already expected or was it too weak to test the relevant structure? In that case, the next step may be to construct a more specific probe rather than just update the model.

Update representation

After encountering friction and locating its source, we can update our representation of the thing to be a better fit, either externally or internally. When open, we are updating it toward better correspondence with the available input we believe to be of higher order; when closed, we are updating it toward fit and coherence abiding by the internal substrate. In any scenario, revising the representation retunes the field and its properties, like variables and constraints, which is why the embodied felt sense may eventually shift after an explicit update.

It must be said that there is generally an update-speed hierarchy. Notation updating is fast: we can quickly erase a sentence we just wrote and change it to match what is better supported. A representation update is slower: we must change its interactions, what it looks like, its feedback loops, or its general shape in our mind. Surface-level somatic retuning can also happen quickly when the representation changes, but deeply embodied updates are usually the slowest because they involve deep priors that structure perception in the first place. One contradiction won’t rewrite a prior repeated thousands of times, just as doing a task one time won’t generate true tacit knowledge.

To address the field of experience, this loop makes the landscape clearer, like making the edges of a thing more visible so you know where you stand. The refinement loop makes the field more navigable, like a foreigner becoming accustomed to new land. By iterating on how we model the thing, we redraw the boundaries, slopes, and paths of the field to better align with reality. Externalizations help us redraw the landscape, while feedback reveals where the new map still fails. Friction can make violated variables and constraints visible enough that the internal representation can be updated accordingly. The mistakes created by each lossy externalization reveal hidden constraints, potentially making the next expression less wrong and the field itself more accurate and differentiated. Over time, territory that was once foreign becomes familiar as it is slowly untangled, like a messy bundle of yarn being cleanly unwound.

This act of making representations and the field clearer seems similar to the skill of sensory clarity in meditation. Sensory clarity involves learning to distinguish phenomena in experience without immediately becoming lost in their movement or story. It’s like tracking the different sensations occurring in one specific area of your left foot; it’s a skill that takes time to develop. This parallels the process of making internal knowledge clearer by untangling confusion. Sensory clarity is resolution-building at the embodied layer, while refinement is resolution-building at the representational layer. Both skills use a similar move of taking an abstract blob and differentiating it over time to see what is actually there. In this limited sense, sensory clarity can reduce suffering while representational clarity can reduce ignorance.

One caveat here is that updating toward resonance alone without an independent constraint can make wrongness feel true, even if it has no correspondence with reality. A field can become internally coherent while remaining detached from the world. Empirical models therefore require empirical feedback, while formal models require constraints like proof. Other people and “factual” information can provide additional checks. Some independent source of constraint is needed to keep refinement from becoming only the production of increasingly satisfying representations.

New felt sense → loop

For an update to be “successful,” it must eventually become integrated into the field, which may generate a new felt sense. This new felt sense does not need to be deep, like a core belief; it can just be a new way of looking at things. For example, maybe you updated a metaphor to be more accurate, and now the update has generated new constraints you did not have in mind before. If the notation changes but the felt sense does not, this may indicate that the update has not yet propagated into the representational or embodied layers.

Now that this new felt sense exists, we can restart the loop with our new understanding and form another probe. Each new probe can become better calibrated as long as reliable feedback is incorporated, allowing the field to become more accurate and differentiated.

This loop is not a one-and-done thing. Many passes are needed for deep change, especially for embodied knowledge, which is where expertise lives. Repeated passes can also propagate surface-level representational changes into deeper branches of the tree, much like repeated practice gradually turns explicit knowledge into tacit knowledge.

This active iteration is a central mechanism of learning: it uses the mechanisms of knowledge and their different layers to loop on updating toward truth. Passive input, like educational YouTube videos, pales in comparison to actively participating in this loop of refining internal states, although passive input can still supply material for later testing and refinement.

The end state of this loop is not to reach perfect truth, but to progressively improve calibration. Through fine-tuning our field, we are orienting ourselves toward experience in a manner that is better aligned with available feedback while remaining useful for our targets.

I believe Grothendieck almost perfectly captures the essence of refinement:

When I am curious about something, mathematical or otherwise, I question/[interroge] it. I question it, without caring if my question is perhaps stupid or if it will appear so, without it being carefully weighed. Often the question takes the form of an assertion - an assertion which, in truth, is a knocking probe. I will then believe more or believe less in the assertion, which depends of course on where I stand in the comprehension of the things I’m looking at. Often, especially at the beginning of a research, the assertion is completely false - but this still had to be done to convince yourself. Often, it suffices to write it down for it to become obvious that it is false, whereas before writing it down there was a vagueness/[flou], like an uneasiness/[malaise], instead of obviousness. It then enables us to return to the probing with less ignorance, with a question-assertion that is perhaps a little less “off the mark”. More often still, the affirmation taken literally turns out to be false, but the intuition which, still clumsily, tries to convey itself through this is correct, though remaining vague. This intuition will, little by little, be decanted from an equally shapeless gangue of false or inadequate ideas; it will gradually emerge from the limbo of the misunderstood which only asks to be understood, of the unknown which only asks to be known, to take a form which is her own, to refine and sharpen its contours, as the questions I ask of these things in front of me become more precise or more pertinent, to surround them more and more closely.

Alexander Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles

3.6 Real-making vs Truth-making

In this loop, there exist two main orientations that it can take, which I am calling real-making and truth-making. Just as the open and closed versions of the loop can be run in parallel, these two orientations of either version can be run simultaneously to reinforce each other.

Real-making, in general, is the process by which something becomes embodied and actionable within the field. Repetition, attention, aesthetics, emotion, social reinforcement, and practice can all deepen its priors and make it a more reachable field configuration from a global point of view. It is making a thing more “real” in the sense of it mattering more to the system. To real-make something is to make it meaningful within a present configuration of the field. This process is more so correlated with closed loops because what is being repeatedly tested is simply internal with no meaningful external feedback. Of course it is possible we can detect logical errors and misnomers in our models, but this is much harder to do in a closed loop because it is relying on your current field configuration to supply you with the necessary working properties.

Resonance helps push real-making further because the feeling can make a representation easier to embody and take seriously as a concept rather than simply discarding it. When a thing is resonant, there’s like a hook attached to it that is hard to let go of; this of course can make things seem a lot more meaningful than without resonance. However, other orthogonal feelings can real-make a thing too, like fear and authority, so it should be made clear resonance is not necessarily needed for the process. Real-making is about integration and stabilization, not necessarily positive felt tone, so any mechanism that contributes to those two multipliers is what matters here.

Truth-making, on the other hand, is the process of testing and updating a model so that it better corresponds with reality. The process depends on constraints, like evidence, argument, and other people, that push back against the current field, so naturally the process is closely correlated with open loops. The process of truth-making is essential to making things reliable so external feedback gives consistent and useful results. This dynamic shifts when viewed across different levels of abstraction. For example, someone may be “truth-making” within a frame of religion, like debating about ancient texts, but from a higher-order outside perspective of a non-believer they are simply real-making false claims. What the outsider does not understand is that the debaters are indeed truth-making within the ontological constraints of the frame they are operating in, and because we are always operating in some type of environment that places structure onto what is possible, all truth-making is ultimately relative to the boundaries and rules set by that environment. Of course resonance is not required for truth-making since accurate information may initially produce friction because it conflicts with existing priors.

To be clear, the loop of refinement itself is a neutral process; it contains no inherent orientation. The loop does not move towards truth on its own; it just modulates whatever its targets and feedback environment select for. An echo chamber contains genuine refinement loops, but they may refine a belief for a target that is not in favor of external correspondence, such as social acceptance or emotional resonance.

Real-making can be useful before truth-making because an idea may initially be too vague or fragile to test. If we “incubate” the idea long enough through real-making, it can allow us to stabilize and scrutinize the idea instead of us instantly discarding it when we encounter friction. Michael Nielsen speaks of something similar by using the concept of “creative cocoons”: “To be creative, you need to recognize those barely formed thoughts, thoughts which are usually wrong and poorly formed in many ways, but which have some kernel of originality and importance and truth. And if they seem important enough to be worth pursuing, you construct a creative cocoon around them, a set of stories you tell yourself to protect the idea not just from others, but from your own self doubts. The purpose of those stories isn’t to be an air tight defence. It’s to give you the confidence to nurture the idea, possibly for years, to find out if there’s something really there.” That set of stories is there precisely to real-make the thing so it can be protected and registered with priors as meaningful. The danger here occurs when the protective process seals the cluster off from outside feedback due to sealing itself off.

Crucially, the main things driving the truth-making orientation on a global scale, meaning an orientation set outside the current frame and operating at a higher level of abstraction, are higher order targets for accuracy and correspondence with reality (targets that stay stabilized long-term), alongside a willingness to expose local frames to constraints they cannot control. It means having the ability to ensure field configurations reach a stable attractor state in which targets aiming for truth exist and matter to the system. Ironically, we must “real-make” goals of truth-seeking and error-correction so that correspondence with reality becomes an active priority within the system. This is why I have long believed that learning and “reach towards” truth is more of a spiritual endeavor than an intellectual pursuit as it requires sources from within that must summon the courage to surrender control and look outside established priors that exist only to protect the current model. This is why a certain amount of slack, like felt safety, is needed for quality truth-making: without it, the system will simply prioritize protecting deep priors which would be expensive to update. Externalizing a local frame to expose it to constraints it cannot control or plan for costs slack, since it affects the system's ability to commit or not commit to certain configurations. So orientation towards truth is partly a matter of affordability, not just values or domain. A system that cannot afford exposure to external feedback due to low slack will produce real-making that feels like truth-making, like when someone doubles down on a belief when stressed.

3.7 Training Data

I want to touch on the importance of training data and why high quality training data is so important. By training data, I mean the books, articles, art, conversations, social situations, and other experiences that train how our system perceives and acts. In one sense, all sensory input is training data, but I am using the term more narrowly here to emphasize what we consume or repeatedly engage with in ways that shape perception. Your field, which determines what resonates, carries a compressed history of what you have been trained on, in a sense. Because of this, the quality and diversity of your training data strongly shape the loaded distinctions, chunks, relations, and models available to perception, which in turn constrains what you can parse and externalize.

Importantly, training data helps build the field that the Loop of Refinement uses as its substrate while also supplying many of the open-loop constraints through which that field can be corrected. The new information you take in or the feedback you get affects how your model is updated, so bad training data has the potential to erode the corrections the loop tries to make. Put simply, training data gives the evidence from which priors can be learned, while feedback helps determine which of those remain calibrated to reality, or the frame you are working in.

Our fields are not arbitrary, of course. Their structure emerges from the interaction between the trunk of the dependency tree and a lifetime of training data and action. Different instances of training data are weighted very differently depending on their precision, context, repetition, emotional importance, source credibility, and relation to active targets. For example, an “I love you” coming from your mom is going to be weighted much more heavily than a stranger on the internet saying the same thing. And a fact coming from someone you respect and admire will matter much more to the system than the same fact coming from someone you find untrustworthy.

Importantly, training data matters not only because it tunes your field but also because it constrains the structures that the field can generate and recombine. What we can think or express is constrained by the priors and chunks already available to us. Novelty can then arise by recombining, decomposing, abstracting, and reorganizing those structures into configurations that have never previously been instantiated. But then this begs the question: how do new things come to be that seem novel? My answer is that existing chunks and properties can be reorganized while the field makes a new thing out of the configuration, even if in full it has never appeared in training data before. We may also be able to describe a possible state using our current chunks without being able to instantiate the gestalt itself because the priors necessary to organize experience in that way have not yet been learned or cannot be activated on command. For example, a student can understand the formal definition of a math proof, but still struggle to construct one because they lack the underlying processes to know which path to take for construction.

So your field, which is “the frame” from which we act, is trained through this accumulated history. This gives us a principle of garbage in, garbage out: a field built on narrow or inaccurate training data can produce a resonant, but wrong understanding and the actions that follow from it. Of course, what counts as a bad action also depends on the active field’s targets and predictions. A field trained to anticipate rejection may experience acceptance as friction because the “good” outcome of acceptance violates what the field expected.

This is also why the distribution of training data matters. Remaining inside an echo chamber can produce bad overgeneralization because the field never integrates the variation that would force its properties to update toward a more “complete” model. This results in a type of expert who might be in a domain for a long time but only sees one way to handle things because their training data has been too narrow, resulting in a field with rigid variables and constraints. Broader and well-chosen training data can reduce this kind of overfitting by making the field distinguish what generalizes from what works only under a narrow set of conditions. A field can therefore become internally coherent and highly resonant while remaining poorly calibrated because the training data it has used never forces wise revision.

Repeated, sufficiently weighted training data can gradually train its interpreted properties into the default field, particularly when those properties are repeatedly reinforced through feedback. What you repeatedly consume and engage with can become part of your baseline perception, felt as reality itself, so repeated training data should be chosen carefully. Because we have some choice in this matter, choosing your training data is partly choosing what your future default field may become if it is repeated and weighted for long enough. As an example, my aesthetic taste has undoubtedly been refined through years of scrolling Pinterest and Tumblr, slowly but surely training the variables and constraints my field uses. Over time, repeated exposure and selection have trained an increasingly high-dimensional aesthetic target into my embodied layer. I do not need to explicitly state all of its properties when evaluating a new image. The accumulated structure first appears as an immediate intuition about what fits, and language comes afterwards to compress that intuition into words.

Quality of training data is important as well. Think of the difference between content optimized to get clicks vs an expert compressing decades of experience into a careful explanation. The higher quality training data can tune the field to finer variables and constraints, sharpening your internal compass for resonance. In this instance, you are not directly downloading the expert’s embodied knowledge. Instead, you are using their externalization to reconstruct some of the chunks and relations that took them years to learn. This can shorten the path, but those structures must still be rebuilt and tested within your own field to reach into the representational and embodied layers. However, low quality training data can also shape the field, only toward inaccurate, shallow, or poorly calibrated structure. Of course, low and high quality depend partly on your target. When the target is truth-making, quality depends on whether the training data helps the field form chunks and heuristics that remain calibrated across different ontological systems.

This matters especially when the system is attempting to confirm harmful priors like “I am not good enough.” Our system will find confirming training data resonant and disconfirming training data frictional. Bad training data might not feel bad from the inside because false information can still resonate with the existing field. Confirmation bias can orient us away from what is true or good for us while our structural limitations prevent us from seeing the error.

Finally, practice, like solving problems by hand, is important when dealing with training data because it determines what structure is extracted from it. Two people can consume the same training data, like a blog post, and build very different fields or chunks because varying factors like attention and active targets determine what gets conditioned. For this reason, though it is a crucial step, choosing better training data is not enough by itself. It must be paired with active engagement and open-loop correction.

4. Emergence, Thresholds, and Creativity

4.1 Emergence and Failed Gestalts

Emergence is a very relevant concept in this framework because it brings together many of the mechanisms already defined to explain how whole things become available to experience. In the sense being used here, for a thing to be emergent, it must appear whole, not fractured. Emergence, then, is the appearance of properties produced by the organization of the whole rather than by a component taken separately. By this I mean that initially separate components are meaningfully organized, rather than something magically appearing from nothing. The resulting whole depends on conditions like sensory evidence and precision weighting. If you change those conditions, a different whole may appear.

The duck and rabbit illusion provides a simple example:

The markings remain largely the same, but changes in the weighting of possible interpretations allow either a duck or a rabbit to organize the field. One interpretation of the image typically dominates at a time because the same features must be organized through different relations. Neither interpretation about what is appearing exists within the markings themselves. Each appears when the markings and the field’s available structure are organized into a specific gestalt.

Let us take failed higher order gestalts as an example. What I mean by this is that some things can appear to us as almost forming something whole, but do not quite reach a level of higher order organization that satisfies enough of the field’s properties to become whole. Take this image, for example:

Some things within the image can still be named, such as patterns of shapes, colors, edges, textures, lighting, and a general vibe. These are already lower level gestalts, meaning less obvious than higher order ones. The image is therefore not completely without organization. The failure occurs at a higher level because those lower-level things like shapes imply that we are looking at a real scene containing known things, while the possible objects never organize enough to satisfy the field’s threshold for objecthood. Several interpretations may partially activate, but none explains enough of the structure to be dominant.

The unsettling vibe of the image therefore comes from the field attempting to maturate recognizable lower level qualities into higher order things and failing. The lower level cues predict a resolvable scene that should be easy to process, but the actual cost of integrating the cues into real objects is greater than predicted. Since a very default deep target of “this image should be recognizable” is dominant, this failure produces a negative difference in felt tone experienced as friction. If the system did not predict the image to process or had no target that depended on it being fully comprehensible, however, the same failure to produce high level objects could remain closer to baseline rather than producing noticeable friction.

This demonstrates that things are not simply “there” and received in their completed form. They are actively constructed as lower level features and relations are organized by the field. In this image, many of the necessary low level tokens are present, but the whole never comes alive because no available interpretation satisfies enough of the relevant properties the field is holding. It is plausible a differently trained field might possess chunks that allow all objects in the image to be correctly processed if the organization fits neatly.

If the image were gradually morphed into a recognizable object, say through an animation, the external conditions supporting the gestalt would change until the object became valid to the current field. If this resolution happened more easily than the system predicted, it would produce a positive difference in felt tone experienced as resonance. If the resolution of the morph was fully expected, it might simply return the system to baseline. When the viewer takes actions such as changing their perspective to reduce uncertainty, those actions can be understood through active inference because the system is actively using the image in ways expected to produce a valid interpretation.

This same general process appears in a lot more cases than just weirdly designed images like the one I presented. A sentence can contain familiar words while failing to produce a coherent, whole meaning. A math problem can contain individually understood pieces while its organizing structure remains questionable.

The same general process appears in less obviously perceptual cases. A sentence can contain familiar words while failing to produce a coherent meaning. A mathematical problem can contain individually understood pieces while its organizing structure remains unavailable. An emotional experience can contain recognizable sensations like a fast heartbeat while the person remains unable to interpret what they mean as a whole. In every case, lower level recognizable features are available while the relevant higher order gestalt fails to stabilize. And in every case, we have the ability to “morph” the relations of the features and the features themselves so a whole gestalt can be recognized. For instance, in a sentence that has recognizable words but produces friction, we can morph it into a form that registers in our system as valid, and sometimes resonant.

More precisely, this kind of morphing can be understood as a structure-preserving transformation. When rewriting a sentence, the intended meaning and important relations act as the structure we are trying to preserve, while properties like word choice and order remain open to change. Each reformulation becomes a probe generated by the Loop of Refinement which then is tested for resonance or friction. The goal in this instance is to find a notational form that preserves enough of the original embodied or representational structure for a coherent gestalt to be reconstructed.

This is also why we cannot fully instantiate a gestalt for which our system does not yet possess the needed priors or chunks. We may be able to construct an approximation using existing structures, like guessing what we might feel in a novel situation, but the actual organization cannot become available until its prerequisites have been learned and can be in relation together. Failed gestalts therefore show emergence in reverse since the organization has not yet become stable enough for a valid whole to appear.

The next question is why those conditions can develop gradually while the resulting gestalt made from those parts seems to arrive all at once.

4.2 Creativity and Structural Thresholds

The previous section described failed higher order gestalts through lower level priors not being stable enough for the whole to appear. Creativity can be understood as a search process that sometimes resolves this failure. It keeps different chunks active until one becomes sufficiently supported to register as a new gestalt, something novel. Structural thresholds explain why this search can develop gradually while its result feels like a sudden movement from “here” to “there.” A structural threshold is a critical point where enough lower-level chunks become sufficiently stable to abruptly percolate into an available higher-order gestalt. Bear in mind this is descriptive, meaning labels like "enough" and "sufficiently stable" are only knowable after the gestalt emerges.

I’ve long since been fascinated by this tweet because it displays something very useful: the way that moving from “here” to “there” so often feels like a discontinuous jump with no clear progressive path between the two, like his example of making money online being totally unthinkable until you do it, and then totally obvious everyone’s doing it. The discontinuous jump part here in particular makes sense in the light of this framework and how it views creativity. Creativity here is a search process through a possibility space, with a target of stabilizing existing chunks into seemingly novel gestalt(s). You can only operate from the chunks your input history and field have learned, so every new idea is a novel manipulation of existing knowledge the field actively uses to search for answers to targets.

As established in the training data section, the chunks and relations available to the field constrain where creative search can begin and occur. Most people would be better advised to diversify their input in order to train more rich and varied chunks for more combinatorial power. Importantly, this means there is no absolute novelty, since there are only combinations that are new relative to a given individual field, hence why one idea can feel like an obvious next step to someone and a giant leap in innovation to another. The person who made up the novel idea has a field that already stored the needed chunks and the paths between them, so the new combination sits close on their prediction tree and is reachable. To an individual who lacks those needed chunks, the idea isn’t a nearby search at all since the prerequisites aren’t stored. The felt “out-there-ness” of an idea is not an inherent property of the thing but a measurement of the distance between the field that produced it and the field encountering it. This is what we can call creative distance: the amount of reorganization required for a new gestalt to become reachable from the creator’s current field.

LessWrong has modeled something related called ‘inferential distance,’ which is the “amount of steps or concepts a person needs to share before they can successfully communicate the object level point.” This differs from creative distance because it is spelling out the difference in prerequisite structure to result in perception rather than creation.

This also emphasizes the importance of explicit prerequisite maps, since certain chunks are literally needed before learning and using ideas further out on the tree of knowledge. It’s like the dependency tree: some structure of the trunk is needed before branching out.

As a caveat, recombination is not the only source of novelty in the field. Open loops have the ability to import constraints the field could not have generated internally due to lacking prerequisites or deep attractor basins which prevent the correct structure from being reachable. That is why doing the work and practicing produces understanding that just “thinking harder” does not, and why a closed loop can refine structure indefinitely without adding anything novel. So “no absolute novelty” means newness is relative to a given field, not that nothing new is imported. In terms of a starting point within the human system (and other agentic systems), this framework does not attempt to map out where structure originates in the first place, or why any specific pattern shows up in a system at all, which is what people like Michael Levin are attempting to do. This framework’s intentional boundary takes it that our starting point comes from “innate” structure like interoceptive homeostasis, genetics, and evolutionary pressures.

This is ultimately what the tweet is pointing at. Moving from “here” to “there” follows the same general kind of dynamic as gestalt formation, only across a longer time scale. Before the transition, partial prerequisites and candidate organizations may be accumulating without any alternative becoming stable enough to appear. Once enough supporting structure has developed, a thing crosses a threshold and becomes available as a new higher order gestalt. The experienced change can therefore feel discontinuous even though the structural changes supporting it were gradual. The change is not binary at the structural level because what appears abrupt is the resulting change in what the system can perceive.

From the field of the “before you” that was attempting to get into the state of the new thing, the new thing may be unavailable to access because the necessary priors have not been learned, or because existing priors cannot yet be activated and coordinated into the needed organization. After the threshold is crossed, the new gestalt reorganizes what seems obvious, making it difficult to reconstruct why “there” was once so hard to see from “here.”

Importantly, once a new gestalt has crossed the threshold and been used repeatedly through practice, its organization can itself become a reusable chunk. Meaning, the gestalt can form relationships with other gestalts which then form a structure by which input is organized. What was previously the result of creative search then becomes one of the things available for future search. It’s like learning a new math skill that then becomes useable for learning other skills. Creativity therefore expands its own possibility space over time since a current emergent gestalt can become part of the structure supporting a future higher order gestalt.

This is the underlying mechanism Kenneth Stanley and Joel Lehman were getting at with their book ‘Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned.’ Planning an end goal requires the destination to be a branch you can already see and take. The priors and paths that would allow you to see the true end goal at the beginning are built by the journey itself, so the start is simply that: a naive way of beginning a journey that is needed to loop for refining knowledge and action. At the start, the path or paths to the endpoint are genuinely outside your representable space. You reach the “there” by crossing a threshold that allows you to see a new gestalt that was once unimaginable, and you can no longer quite say why it was ever hard to see from “here.”

These core observations about novelty can help us understand the importance of constraints when creating. Moderate, well-chosen constraints boost creativity; too few cause paralysis with infinite possibility, and too many shut the system down due to not having enough slack. This parallels the edge-of-the-field zone that underlies learning and positive differences: too few constraints leave the search space impossibly large, so there is no path cheap enough to resolve into a gestalt (paralysis), while too many minimize the space so much that nothing can be combined into something novel; the sweet spot is where constraints minimize the space to something navigable while leaving enough slack to recombine chunks into a gestalt that wasn’t there before. There is an underlying point here that creativity is search through a possibility space within an ontological framing and the search needs structure to be feasible. Constraints prune certain branches of the dependency tree so enough precision can be used for action so a new combination is interpreted as meaningful.

We can take something like play being helpful for creativity since play is accepting constraints that structure the search space of a situation while leaving enough slack for exploration of meaningful recombinations. A frame of play also enables us to place less precision on branches that would constrain our search too much, like needed validation. If we are in a state of play, we can “override” what we would normally value which can result in creative acts we otherwise would not stumble on. Play is valuable because it lowers the predicted cost of failed output without removing structure from the creative search entirely.

In essence, training data gives us available structures that can be used and organized around, the field’s properties, like constraints, organize the search space, slack and play keep alternative branches in the dependency tree alive, and the Loop of Refinement generates and tests organizations of things. Crucially, a structural threshold is crossed when certain input becomes sufficiently stable to appear as a new gestalt, which is how we get from “here” to “there” in terms of state. As we move to novel states and gestalts, the field can represent its new organization which results in more action policies that are made available through branches of the dependency tree.

5. Conflict, Control, and Suffering

5.1 Emotions

"We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble."

– William James, What Is an Emotion?

What is an emotion? There have been a lot of attempts to answer this question and address emotions' load-bearing downstream effects on our mind-body system. I'd like to offer an account of emotions that works within this framework that may point to a unique direction. Truthfully, any attempt to name an emotion or define it is a failure due to moving up the compression gradient which leaves characteristics left out, but nonetheless attempts are still useful in their own right.

An emotion is a context-sensitive field configuration that structures signals from the body and environment, felt tone, conditioned concepts, targets, and actions into a unifying gestalt like fear or anger. What is felt in experience as an emotion is not a raw signal but the organization the field produced. Emotions are not just properties of the field because their construction is downstream of many properties coming together and forming a coherent generative structure. The purpose of emotions naturally arises out of their definition and placement in this framework. It seems as though emotion concepts are reusable chunks that organize bodily and situational context which would normally be ambiguous. For example, fear is not just a stored object that gets released or expressed; it is more like a context-sensitive organizational tool reconstructed from prior structure. Researchers like to define emotion episodes which are more specifically temporally unfolding configurations that “comprise different components such as appraisal of the situation, action preparation, physiological responses, expressive behavior, and subjective feelings”, but this framework will just use the term emotion across the board. Crucially, emotions are not feelings. An emotion is a whole field configuration, while a feeling is how that configuration becomes known from the inside. Felt tone also is not a feeling because it is narrower, referring only to the configuration’s valence. For example, warmth is a feeling, but we would not consider this an emotion because it is not necessarily a structural category. To clarify terms further, mood must be distinguished as well. Mood might be considered as a slower background configuration that structures and biases how ongoing input gets interpreted. It does not require one absolute clear gestalt, and it can create feedback loops in which current felt tone influences perceived outcomes and how they feed back into mood.

Given that the field contains many properties and mechanisms at play, such as sensory evidence, attention, context, and learned concepts from others, it is clear that bodily signals are not simply read and then labeled as a certain emotion. As the system attempts to infer what is going on with its current state and what to do next, all of these different things brew together, which pulls the system in many different directions. Ultimately, precision weighting helps determine which things, like sensory signals, exert the most influence on the field’s current interpretation and action.

Emotions are indeed gestalts. They compress related configurations rather than exact, formulaic structures. For example, moments of fear share enough properties across time and with enough repetition that fear becomes a deep structure built into the dependency tree, made up of priors leaning on the body and context. The instances of fear are chunked together as the system attempts to save capacity so it does not have to process each component of fear every time they show up together. Crucially, instances of fear can slightly differ in contraction patterns, felt tone, attention, and action, but they are still chunked together as one gestalt, which can result in the "misclassification" of future instances due to wide pruning. And crucially, just as the compression gradient is a continuous unfolding of compression rather than being rigid, boundaries between emotions are not discrete; they are fuzzy. This means that categorical emotions lie within a continuous space, connected by intermediate states rather than separated by hard boundaries. This suggests something like a structural-threshold modeling in the sense that the field gradually climbs through ambiguous configurations until one learned one organizes enough to become a clear structuring gestalt. This means "failed" emotional gestalts can exist, or ambiguous components that don't cohere into a whole. This can be seen at the beginnings of a felt sense about an experience: the feeling is there but it has yet to cohere into a nameable thing.

When it comes to targets, emotions complicate things. Not every emotion has just one target built into its known configuration, though often times a singular target is what is perceived, like a need for safety known through its action policy such as "I need to get away from this situation" in episodes of fear. Emotions can be seen as a sort of modulator that relates and connects differing targets when it comes to current or predicted future input. They organize the field in relation to these targets in such a way that relates to preferred states, needs, and capacity, like how targets of anxiety might get someone to move to a state of avoidance to ensure the system is not overwhelmed by bodily sensations. The most relevant target then in a given moment caused by a clear emotion has multiple ways of being known, such as being implicit, distributed, uncertain, or violated. Because of the overlap and nuance of targets when dealing with emotions, action policies are re-weighted without determining one fixed action across the board. This means an emotion may make a number of actions, like escaping or freezing, more reachable depending on the field, without any singular policy encapsulating the emotion itself. A large contributor to this is context, which is why emotions can be so hard to analyze or know since context is always interpreted and organized in such a way by the field. When it comes to targets and their action policies, precision matters since it can be distributed across a wide net of things across the field like sensory evidence, emotions, threats, and available actions. Because of this, high precision on action policies of an emotion can coexist with the belief that the action will have any meaningful outcome at all. My system can place high precision on the action of running from a situation to avoid the sensations, but in the background the belief that this is not a long-term solution can still exist.

When it comes to emotions, the embodied layer is crucial. Emotions are embodied, meaning bodily changes are not just a result of a thing that was created entirely in the brain first. Sensations like tension and heart rate have the ability to influence the emotional experience and provide interoceptive evidence the system uses to predict and regulate its state, like through homeostatic priors. The body is essentially a part of the field configuration that is producing the emotion itself; they cannot be decoupled.

As stated in the field section, the dependency tree says most deep priors are universally shared because they are innate or heavily conditioned across the human species, while outer ones differ due to experience and training data. Accordingly, it can be said that the human body itself is one of the most universally shared structures, so because of this, processes like interoception and action preparation are constrained in very similar ways before an emotion gestalt can organize them through the field. The body provides universal active constraints that limit our processes that determine perception and action. When it comes to emotional categories themselves, these are things further out on the branches of the dependency tree than these deeper priors and processes, which means they are more malleable to the system. The gestalt depending on the deeper structures can vary much more, which maps cross-culturally. Culture can shape which chunks are available within the emotional configuration, like what actions are socially permitted and who is the type of person to commit the action itself. Taking anger as an example, it very clearly stays at the top of our body so as to activate readiness and the needed actions associated with anger, such as confrontation, which is determined by what we can do with our bodies. This is where conditioning ends up mattering like through culture and environment, since repetition and training data will influence what action and high-level organizations are available when the emotion structures experience. And as it turns out, conditioning is very important in determining what exactly we do with these high-level, malleable organizations. It is also important to point out that increased knowledge through language and concepts can allow the field to more readily distinguish between states rather than compressing nuanced ones. It turns out that increasing knowledge can reduce unhappiness through removing barriers between attractor states, allowing us to move more freely among them.

This makes the somatic connection quite clear: a field configuration that is heavily organized around an emotion is held and enacted in the body like any other, such as tacit knowledge. Sensory evidence that is a result of the high-level organization, such as posture and interoceptive signals, creates a feedback loop in which the system confirms the existing organization to be "correct" in its way of describing experience. This is why habitual emotional responses become available so quickly and feel like how things "just are" rather than being actively constructed. These are conditioned patterns held in the body, the same substrate carrying other knowledge, and these patterns can differ in whether they are held with rigidity or make a wide range of policies available. It's important to mention that contraction, in the phenomenological sense of "closing down", can play many roles such as closing off available actions or maintenance of emotional attractors. For instance, it might be tempting to label grief as purely contractive since it is a thing held in the body, but there can exist expansive grief in which it is not held with rigidity and the emotion moves through the system without blockage.

What's going on with mixed emotions? It seems as though mixed emotions are those in which the high-level structure produces many mixed chunks, like grouping related sadness and happiness properties together. Multiple target relations can remain active at the same time, causing the system to interpret these as singular things being mixed together rather than a whole new structuring gestalt. Just to point back to section 4.1, this exact thing happens when a math problem contains individually understood pieces with no single organizing structure underlying them, which results in several partial readings of the problem instead of a coherent whole (relationships and all), which causes confusion. Mixed emotions of course do not require two fields, since the one field can contain targets that are being satisfied, while some are being violated. As an example, if you are graduating college, there might be satisfaction of targets pertaining to life achievements but violation of targets in relation to identity and friendship; both are valid and result in seemingly contradicting interpretations.

Connecting this to expression and the notational layer of the compression gradient, expression itself can be an "inaccurate" (from the outside) indicator as to what the field is holding. Notation is extremely compressed, so one instance cannot fully capture the vast landscape powering the mind-body system. This becomes especially complicated when it comes to mixed emotions since one behavior can be compatible with many contradicting internal chunks, which we then typically interpret as the behavior being only about one unified thing, when indeed this is usually not the case. There may be "leakage" in the system in which suppressed or mixed states may affect subtle signals like posture or voice, but they are not guaranteed to be known through one external signal.

Regulation of emotions in this framework can be seen as being a part of the field configuration rather than a separate system controlling an emotion object. It can be a tool used to do many different things like changing precision about an interpretation or action, or taking different bodily action that results in some type of attenuation of the emotional state, such as deep breathing when anxious. Reappraisal (changing how you interpret a situation to change how it feels) of a situation that is "causing" an emotion can also be helpful in regulation since it changes how the organization is known and done. This differs from suppression since suppression leaves the existing organization from an emotion alive and only blocks what the expression looks like. This is why reappraisal and suppression have much different downstream effects; the latter is much more harmful in the long run. Suppression is also a somatic act, such as tensing muscles, which eventually forms deep latched contraction which ends up producing suffering as seen later in section 5.3.

5.2 Field Dissonance

There exists a mechanism that allows expression to differ from internal states. We can call this field dissonance, which occurs when conflicting properties or action policies remain active within the current field while the system must still settle on a workable organization or action. The field holds properties from multiple sources at once, like deep priors, the current bodily state, representations, and social constraints, and these can conflict with each other. Field dissonance is therefore broader than cognitive dissonance because the conflict does not need to occur between explicit beliefs. It can occur between bodily predictions, social constraints, representations, targets, and available actions.

For instance, a social constraint, which is downstream of deeper priors, could demand that you smile and say you are fine, while an embodied protective target makes withdrawal more salient. The field can hold both properties, but overt action compresses this complexity. A person may have several conflicting properties active while still having to enact one policy. When two policies require mutually exclusive actions, the system resolves the tension by acting on the more heavily weighted properties, leaving the violated ones unresolved.

The dominant policy may appear as a cached response, such as the cliche statement of “doing good, how about you,” while the “losing” ones continue to influence expressions like posture and tone. Rather than simply disappearing, the violated property can “leak out” through automatic or involuntary behavior, making the dissonance externally observable. It can also remain active internally as somatic contraction and thoughts. This continued activity does not necessarily produce friction. Friction appears when the resulting state is more costly or difficult to integrate than predicted, while an unresolved target may otherwise persist as target error without producing a noticeable difference in felt tone, though friction and negative felt tone are most common here. As another example of “leakage” we can take acts of lying to avoid conflict, where the story you tell stays surface-level while expressions like forced laughter and avoided eye contact expose the unintegrated target.

The weight of a property is a manifestation of precision weighting, which contributes to what gains control before an action occurs rather than just explaining the result after the fact. This is why dissonance phenomenologically feels like being right and wrong at the exact same time: one part of the field was satisfied while another part was violated through action. We can connect this to active inference and say that the field contains competing preferred states and policies, all attempting to coordinate the same limited channels of action. The conflict comes from multiple targets and demands remaining active within one changing field.

Because clear behavior is a compressed output of the field, it does not fully capture the configuration that produced it, since compression leaves out characteristics. One smile could be a result of a rigid social prior outweighing interoceptive evidence, or a temporary reduction in the weight of a target that is meant for self-protection. Field dissonance therefore needs to be understood as a classifier across hierarchical levels, precision weighting, real-world context, and temporal variation rather than known from any single behavior. Dissonance that occurs over small time slices is not necessarily dysfunctional. For instance, it may keep competing interpretations and action policies available long enough for the system to find a "better" organization, like one that ends up reducing suffering in the long run. Dissonance becomes persistent and durable when the field repeatedly selects an organization without satisfying or updating the competing structure. Deep priors may continue influencing action while unsatisfied prediction and target errors remain active, while highly weighted errors may prevent a stable organization from becoming the new default. In either case, updating that is blocked due to these factors can turn an ordinary conflict of properties into a self-reinforcing attractor. For example, a person may repeatedly smile and say they are doing good because priors that are associated with social compliance dominate behavior because of their high weighting, while bodily threat signals remain unsatisfied. Each repetition reinforces the social action policy without updating the other structure, making the contraction and behavior increasingly likely in similar situations.

However, resolution of conflicting properties is not limited to simply acting on the behavioral survivor. Because the system can reduce error either by changing the world through action or by updating its internal state, alternative resolution paths exist. Through weighting, one target can overpower the others. Through suppression, conflicting material can remain active while being kept away from control or awareness. The system can utilize reframing, like changing the narrative, to update the underlying model and reduce or remove the contradiction. It can resort to freezing, stopping action altogether when weights are stuck in uncertainty. Or it can try sequencing, which means separating the conflicting targets temporally, such as allowing ourselves to smile now to satisfy social priors and cry later to satisfy the embodied demand.

Another resolution method is that of integration through a structure-preserving transformation. Instead of choosing to stay within a fantasy of safety, for example, the system identifies the invariant properties inside the fantasy that are load-bearing and develops a policy that can be enacted in the real world that satisfies them alongside practical constraints. This reduces dissonance because conflicting target structures can partially resolve rather than compete. Integration is therefore another kind of structural threshold. Instead of selecting between existing policies to reduce conflict, the field produces a new gestalt of action that preserves important properties protected by the targets. For example, a math student struggling with an abstract proof stops choosing between rote memorization and blind guessing, instead constructing a visual geometric model that satisfies both the strict logical axioms and their need for spatial reasoning.

This can help explain why our words and behavior can diverge. It is a matter of the field reweighting its properties incredibly fast rather than two parallel fields running in the background. One major form of this is a conflict between a local target, like present comfort, and a global target, like maintaining long-term health by going to the gym. A local target organizes action across a narrow horizon, while a global target coordinates outcomes across a wider span of time and context. The distinction is a gradient rather than a binary, ranging from targets that organize the next few seconds to those maintained across years.

A global target is not a property sitting in the field continuously. It is a label applied across time as the field repeatedly reinstantiates a target over a long time. In one moment you may be acting toward that goal because its relevant properties are weighted heavily, but a few moments later your actions can betray your words because the field has reconfigured toward immediate comfort. Failure to reach global targets is therefore not necessarily irrationality. It can be a local target temporarily gaining more motivational precision than the wider target.

Self-control, then, is not simply overpowering parallel desires but keeping the global target alive and weighted in the field long enough to act on it. Discipline is repeatedly making the global target active and weighting it heavily enough to act on before the field defaults to a local target. For nuance, the global target should not always dominate. A long-term target can be poorly calibrated, while a local target may contain information that should update it. Discipline is the ability to reliably reinstate a wider horizon target when short-horizon competitors become salient, without becoming unable to revise targets when conditions change. A key goal here to cultivate discipline is to ensure the attractor states that are relevant for your goal have high reachability. This means designing your environment and routines so that the path from your current state to the intended action requires minimal friction, while blocking off paths toward short-horizon defaults.

Attractors make conflicts asymmetrical. One target may be seriously weighted while the opposing field configuration has a wider or deeper basin. A person can consciously target calm while repeatedly returning to anxiety because the anxiety configuration is easier to reinstate and makes protective policies, like somatic contraction, more reachable.

Field dissonance itself is not identical to suffering. It describes the broader situation in which active properties cannot all be satisfied together. The suffering loop begins when one of those demands becomes a highly weighted need that present experience must be different, while the preferred state remains difficult to reach.

5.3 The Suffering Loop

I want to provide a powerful synthesis of ideas related to suffering to understand the mechanisms going on. I will be explaining the synthesis using both theoretical ideas and examples that tie the concepts together. To make the mechanism clear, we can separate it into three phases: first-order hurt, suffering as a field configuration, and maintenance.

To define suffering, we need a working definition. In this framework, suffering can be thought of as a negatively felt state that "spreads" across the field because a highly weighted target makes experience feel as though it must be different in some way, which includes making it permanent. It persists when the mind-body system cannot find an easily reachable thing to do, such as action, updating the model, or changing the target, that either closes the difference between its current and target state or lets the difference remain without resisting it. To be clear about the terminology here, target error is the difference between the current state and the target, and action policies are the paths that might remove the difference. Technically, ordinary wanting of a different field configuration, like more valence from a material good, is suffering since the target is weighted enough. This is why the state of cessation, or nirodha samāpatti (consciousness briefly ceasing during meditation), is ultimately the only state we can be in with absolutely zero suffering. Practically, suffering in its ordinary usage typically names the inflection point at which the target reorganizes the field enough so it is mostly geared towards that target. In the practical sense, there's also a temporal aspect at play since most people would not consider a craving that can be satisfied in one minute to be suffering. For example, the preference to get rid of sensations of sitting on a chair due to mild restlessness would be suffering, while practically someone might only consider suffering to be when the sensations start hurting and the field organizes enough around moving away from that state (so either getting up or changing the interpretation of the sensations). However, in both instances, suffering is present due to the negative felt tone of not being in a weighted target state. In the case of suffering across the framework, the wise goal is not fully getting rid of it, but reducing as much of its magnitude as practically possible and increasing reachability across field configurations so we avoid getting stuck in states.

First, we start with the basic chain of experiencing some type of input. For every interpreted input, there is a predicted integration cost. Let’s give it a value of 20 here as a metaphor. Then we have to actually integrate the interpreted input into our system, and input can be far away or close to fitting into our field, which is what the fit distance property measures. Let’s say the actual integration cost is 30 instead of the predicted 20. This results in a negative difference in felt tone experienced as friction and, typically, some degree of unpleasantness.

This is first-order hurt, though not the only source of it. With any first-order hurt, there is the immediate negatively valenced aspect of the experience, considered before any interpretation or action. The hurt can come from a threat to a target, violation of an expectation, loss, sensory overload, or wrongly predicted integration cost. In this framework, it is defined as the immediate negatively felt aspect of an experience that is registered before the field produces a target that it needs to be different. In Buddhism, this is known as the first arrow which represents unavoidable painful experience.

First-order hurt can be unpleasant before the system adds any secondary interpretation or control effort. Factors like emotional or physical pain, exhaustion, loss, and sensory overload can be hurtful even when they are not surprising. First-order hurt is not identical to suffering considering the negative felt state has yet to organize the field around a high precision target for experience to be different. Once the mind-body system feels this hurt, taṇhā occurs. Taṇhā literally means thirst and is conventionally translated as craving, though this might be contested in some areas for its technicalities. For example, Romeo Stevens says the term might be better known as 'fused to' or 'welded to,' which seems more like an accurate phenomenological description. Anyways, the important part about taṇhā is that it precedes suffering and is the "grabby" or "pushy" thing that interacts with sensations of experience. So it is not that suffering is directly taṇhā, but taṇhā is the mechanism by which suffering is born through the conditioning of organizing the field around targets. For accuracy, in the Four Noble Truths, Buddhism identifies taṇhā as the principal condition giving rise to dukkha, rather than as dukkha itself: "...the origin of suffering: it is this craving which leads to renewed existence..."

Next is where first-order hurt becomes suffering in the sense defined above because the target is now weighted enough to organize the field around obtaining it. This is known as the second arrow in Buddhism which represents the additional mental second-order stress that comes from reactivity. Targeting can go in two different directions: craving and aversion. In the direction of aversion, the target is something like "this state should not be happening," so the mind-body system attempts to evade the current state. In the craving direction, the target is something like "I need to reach or maintain that state," so the mind-body system attempts to obtain the target. Both directions implicitly contain the notion that there is a difference between the actual state and the target, which is target error. Precision weighting is what organizes the field around closing the difference. The reachability of the closing of the difference is how long the suffering might last and how intense it is. For example, craving for a piece of cake to obtain sensory pleasure is highly reachable if cake is near you, making the magnitude of the suffering not very large. Compare this to something like grief where the target is bringing someone back to life or denial of emotions: the reachability of the target is impossible so the magnitude of suffering will increase.

Roughly, what we have so far is:

First-order hurt + "I don't want this" target → aversion Absence of desired state + "I must have this" target → craving

(Absence of a desired state is still hurt, like a felt lack)

More deeply, craving and aversion can be understood as co-arising signals of the same discrepancy. Co-arising means the thing implicitly signals another thing in experience, like the way 'up' only means anything if 'down' is also available. Every instance of craving will contain an aversion to how things currently are, and every instance of aversion will contain a craving for a different state. When our actual state is not the preferred state, there is craving for the preferred state and aversion to the actual state. Every act of craving can implicitly reject the present configuration, while every aversion can implicitly want something else. They are not always phenomenologically identical in how they feel, nor do they always appear together consciously, but mechanistically they can be understood as co-arising signals of the same process trying to close target error.

Then comes active inference which is the start of the maintenance phase. We take action in some manner when our actual state differs from the target state. The mind-body system can attempt to change the world or body to match the target, change its model of the hurt, or update the target itself. We can take a look at an example of being hurt in some way to understand this better. First, when we are hurt, we can leave the situation, which changes the state of the world. Second, we can suppress the sensations that are downstream of input being processed by the field through contraction, which may change our state temporarily. Lastly, we can reinterpret the input, which changes the model. We can also lower the weighting of the target instead by allowing the sensation without suppression through expansion, which reduces the precision of the demand that the hurt must change. These are all attempts to close the difference between the target state and the current state, or responses to target error.

It is crucial to mention slack and its importance here because without enough, the system cannot hold target error open long enough for competing branches in the dependency tree to stay available, which means hurt will automatically turn into suffering. Without slack, taṇhā becomes destiny.

It’s important to mention somatics here and how the body plays a role. As established earlier, priors may be held and stabilized through recurring patterns of muscular, visceral, vascular, and autonomic tension, which means an active target does not only predict a preferred state but prepares the body to enact it. When input violates that prediction, friction can be felt interoceptively, and the existing bodily pattern may tighten in an attempt to defend the prior. Through active inference, the body then prepares an action to change the experience through the ways listed in the paragraph above.

Haptic contraction is not identical to craving or aversion, but it can be one of their somatic expressions. If sustained, the contraction keeps the target active and highly weighted while also physically reinstantiating it, which helps explain the phrase “what you resist persists.” This is maintenance: the recursive coupling of hurt and taṇhā which keeps the field organized around the target and produces cascading suffering. Maintenance can reinforce itself because the contraction is unpleasant and continues to violate the active target which confirms the prediction that the state needs to change, producing further taṇhā. As a whole, craving and aversion describe the direction of the mind-body system’s control effort, while haptic contraction is a way the associated priors are felt and enacted in the body.

The maintenance loop becomes extra rigid when the wanted target state is highly weighted and has low or impossible reachability. The system continues weighting the target heavily while lacking a viable action policy for obtaining it due to barriers. This leaves the difference in preference active and produces repeated attempts at control that fail to resolve the error.

unpleasant experience → “this must not be happening” → action policy to stop it → contraction → additional unpleasant evidence → stronger belief that the state is intolerable → stronger action to stop it

It is crucial to mention slack and its importance here: without enough slack, the system cannot hold target error open long enough for competing branches to stay alive, which means hurt will automatically turn into suffering. Without slack, taṇhā becomes destiny.

Through repetition, the suffering maintenance loop can become an attractor state itself that repeatedly reinstates the field configuration supporting it. One common starter of this is rumination on memories, which are reconstructed as present representations that bring about unpleasant sensations, so recalling one reinstates the field configuration it came from and the body responds as if the situation is happening in the present moment.

Repeated maintenance of craving or aversion can make the contraction latch in the body, which then holds the suffering configuration in place without ongoing effort. One downstream effect of this is that the latch itself, or contraction pattern, stops needing the original trigger for it to be enacted through active inference. Once the latch can become stable on its own, the configuration reinstates from mundane conditions rather than from anything substantial happening, hence why long-term patterns just feel like the way things work. This is where habitual suppression of unpleasant sensations, like tensing the body to not feel emotions, can do its damage, since suppression is a form of contraction.

Interestingly, this suffering loop is not only phenomenologically relevant, like dealing with bodily sensations, but it is epistemically relevant as well. That is, suffering can occur in the direction of epistemic ignorance, when the thing being defended is a model or concept. Epistemic contraction is just like bodily contraction, in the sense that it is a mechanism to prevent unwanted experience from occurring. Disconfirming input that does not go along with your current model of a thing is registered as friction, which can be both bodily and/or cognitive. This friction can result in aversion, a reaching towards a target of a more pleasant state of the model being "right". Typically this results in suppression, like blocking the input or denial of the ignorance. Just as habitual bodily contraction can bury priors deep into the body, making them the default way of viewing things, so too can the same thing happen to epistemic models: they can be buried deep in the field if contracted around enough. This means revising the epistemic model of a concept or thing means revising everything else that depends on it, which is typically costly to the system, resulting in more defense for deep models. So, phenomenological and epistemic ignorance are the same failure mode pointing in specific directions, since both treat constructions as the way things have to be. In this sense, open and closed loops of refinement to update models must require that the thing needing updating is not contracted around, since defending against change would make refinement very difficult, resulting in more ignorance. For nuance, tacit knowledge might also be seen in the same way, contraction living in the body, but crucially it runs on open loops along with holding diverse action policies so the system does not shut down in the same way it does with patterns related to suffering.

If we lower precision on targets that we once held heavily, through practice like meditation, we can feel a much smaller gap between the actual and preferred state, which means there is less pressure for active inference around those old targets. This can feel like reduced motivation because lots of ordinary motivation is powered by contraction around “this must happen” or “this must stop.”

However, direction and pressure should be separated. Direction comes from the target itself, which can be wholesome like the desire for connection with others, while pressure comes from the added prediction that the target must be reached for whatever reason. This added prediction that the target must be reached is held as contraction, both in the mind and body, and as taṇhā. Crucially, desire and pressure are what produce suffering, while desire by itself without pressure has the ability to be not characterized by suffering, what Buddhism might call wholesome forms of chanda. However, this form of wholesome desire without added pressure is very hard to achieve without some sort of deliberate practice like meditation, so the average person experiencing desire will always be suffering, even if in small amounts. For example, mild wanting of cake is low-grade suffering since the added pressure is usually not that much. If you're starved and haven't had dessert in a while, more pressure will appear and the desire will be more intense, causing more contraction. However, this same desire for the cake can be held without added pressure. This also means the desire can naturally fall away if the person chooses to drop it, since it is not organizing the field in such a way that is load-bearing.

Desires that feel deep and load-bearing, like wanting of love or safety, feel that way because they target priors and branches deep in the dependency tree. Deep priors like a sense of permanence of things and selfhood sit in the trunk of the tree. When a desire somehow routes through these, the target error becomes massive and expensive to deal with, so the pressure itself feels big. Loosen these priors through practice like meditation (what transparency does in section 6 as well), and the same desires stop containing so much pressure due to the priors not being so important to the system. However, if these practices that can help alleviate this type of suffering lower weighting of targets too broadly or become disengagement somehow, they can produce apathy rather than equanimity and motivation.

Underlying all desires, whether they be desires for items, people, or states of being, is desire for a certain field configuration. The thing you think you desire is simply a proxy, a pointer towards that field configuration that is the ultimate target. Ultimately, this is because things are gestalts which organize input; they are not external objects that are neutrally handled. You do not desire the atoms of the thing; you desire the field configuration the object is expected to produce. The cake is not the desired thing; what is desired is the configuration associated with sweetness, pleasure, and comfort that the cake is predicted to instantiate. Wanting of objects is simply a case where a gestalt is an expected route towards the target field configuration. If desire really is solely about individual objects, substitution of objects, like trading cake for another dessert, would be impossible. The fact that a different object can satisfy the same desire proves the target is the associated configuration, not the thing itself. This also helps inform us as to why when we get the thing we wanted, often we are disappointed and the desire does not fully end: the object successfully registered as the wanted gestalt, but not the deeper configuration that the target was aiming for. And the configuration the target aims for often has to do with inaccurate priors that simply cannot be satisfied, like object permanence and selfhood. So the proxy resolves, like through obtaining the item, but the real target error persists. This same mechanism is what often powers the hedonic treadmill: what targets we desire often cannot be satisfied due to ignorance or configurations not showing up how we want. It's important not to completely collapse objects since they are reliable causes of certain field configurations to show up, which is often where deep attractor basins can show up since the object becomes a reliable path towards a desired configuration. Tying this back to the energetic triad, an unmet desire is target error which registers as friction. And because deep desires come from the trunk in the dependency tree like permanence and selfhood, that friction of not satisfying the target is large in magnitude due to many dependent structures having to be recomputed. This is where equanimity comes to be helpful as a tool, since we need to hold target field configurations as reachable without holding pressure while keeping its direction.

Equanimity in this framework leaves targets and actions available while making them less compulsory. Equanimity also acts as prevention of collapse. In section 1.3, it was outlined that a general principle exists that collapsing states from many to one is "easier" than decompressing from one to many, like notation to the embodied layer. Equanimity then becomes a tool to prevent collapse as a result of deep haptic contraction. Mechanically, equanimity means the demand that states of the system be different does not get weighted heavily, so no contraction latches in the long run, and the possibility of a wide range of states is preserved. Equanimity is not simply calmness or low-temper; it is a specific working preventive mechanism that allows us to increase the intensity of experience without producing deep latched contraction.

It might be tempting to believe that to increase wellbeing, we should simply raise our predictions about how costly input will be to integrate so that actual costs repeatedly come in lower, producing resonance and positive felt tone. In theory, this could reduce first-order hurt which would prevent suffering in general. However, the predicted baseline is not a free variable we can easily manipulate in this way. As mentioned in the Energetic Triad section, physiology plays a major role here and can affect our baseline in ways outside immediate conscious control. If we try to “fake” a high expected cost, that estimate is not a genuine prediction, and knowing we inflated it collapses the surprise that would have produced resonance. If we managed to genuinely condition an expectation that everything will be costly, we would be training a prior that the world is difficult or threatening. That could lower background felt tone and increase prediction that things are bad rather than producing genuine wellbeing. Another important point here is that decreasing hurt does not guarantee that craving will go away. In fact, based on the maintenance loop, without the proper skills, decreasing hurt will just increase craving by making wanted states more salient.

Increasing background felt tone, which is one part of wellbeing, can be influenced through our own agency. One objection here is that any increase in the baseline gets absorbed and becomes the new zero all other experience gets compared against. You feel better for a while; it becomes the norm, and you end up at the happiness level you were at beforehand. That mechanism holds for things the mind-body system treats as a stable condition, which it stops tracking once the change integrates into the baseline. Contraction is different, whether dealing with mind contraction or haptic contraction. Mind contraction is held "in the mind" rather than "in the body," like holding a representation about something to be true without budging. Releasing contraction is not acquiring something new that will get absorbed into the baseline like almost everything else. It is ending a cost the system was paying every moment out of its total capacity. Just as you stop noticing when an old pain disappears, like a headache, you stop registering the increase in background felt tone, even as its absence frees up capacity. The rise stays noticeable, but only through comparison to past states or when a change in conditions drops you back into an old configuration with worse felt tone which makes the difference obvious. But, even when this happens, with new skills like equanimity, being dropped into conditions that used to decrease background felt tone can now be handled by not closing the repertoire of the field which increases felt tone. Baseline rise in felt tone also has the ability to decrease craving and aversion by making less demand that current states need to be different. This allows our system to hold more slack for holding intensity and makes more field configurations easily reachable, which results in fluidity. These are structural changes, not just temporary adaptations. Resources are no longer spent maintaining patterns of suffering, and the body stops sending signals that confirm it. This is why advanced practitioners describe these changes as lasting and also as unremarkable, since they are both true about different things.

These processes of suffering lead us to the tool of emptiness as a way to prevent the mechanisms at play. It is important to recognize that first-order hurt when suffering is a perceived solid gestalt, like “anxiety” or a “thing” that should be different. But on closer inspection, the gestalt becomes a part of a greater process and can be decomposed into many different parts. The same thing happens with suffering itself. Remember, a gestalt was defined as “the unified whole that appears in the present moment when parts are organized and glued together.” This means that if we can separate the parts effectively so the gestalt no longer appears as a unified solid thing, our system can reveal many more available policies to enact which do not maintain the state of suffering. Luckily, we have emptiness on our side as a tool to investigate the processes contributing to suffering. Contemplating and realizing emptiness as a tool can liberate us both phenomenologically and epistemically by recognizing the construction of things as an active process rather than a rigid, stuck noun.

6. Transparency

6.1 Emptiness & Ontological Authority

Emptiness, or suññatā, is an extremely load-bearing concept within this framework that leads to insight about time, wonder, suffering, and frankly everything. As a primer, here is what Rob Burbea has to say about emptiness: “We can, at least for now, define emptiness as the absence of this inherent existence that things appear to naturally and undeniably have” and “Emptiness then, is more a quality that we can recognize in something, a way something is, a property it has. A thing is ‘empty’ of its seemingly real, independent existence.” Emptiness means that no “thing” exists in the independent manner it appears to. Its identity and properties depend on causes, conditions, relations, and the field organizing them into a specific gestalt. I like to think of emptiness as seeing through an optical illusion and realizing that multiple interpretations can be valid as perceptual organizations, while none exist inherently within the drawings/lines themselves. The conditions and manner in which you look at the illusion determine what appears. It is empty of being one way or another. It is flexible, just like all other “things.” Emptiness can be a simple tool, like understanding that your perspective depends on your conditioning and mood, but taken to great depths, it is an amazing tool for addressing problems we think we have, like suffering.

To fit emptiness into this framework, it is important to know that it is the negative space of the model, not another component among the others, but a way in which the core components are seen from the other side, like the back of a coin compared to the front. Every component and mechanism so far, like compression, chunking, the field, gestalts, and the dependency tree, has been modeled to understand how “things” get constructed. Emptiness is simply telling us that the construction of “things” is just that: a construction. The constructive process runs roughly as follows:

priors structure sensory experience into features and relations → those relations are compressed into chunks → chunks are packaged as a gestalt → precision makes the gestalt “stable” and useable

Emptiness actually reverses this process:

the solid thing is examined as a gestalt → gestalt is de-chunked into its relation and parts → relations are traced back to the causes and conditions allowing it to appear

To see a thing as empty is to understand how the field is constructing and maintaining it. For instance, a chair is an object gestalt produced from a field configuration, built from co-arising conditions like shapes, relations, context, and prior knowledge. If you change the priors or their weighting, the “thing” of the chair appears differently or not at all, which is exactly what happens with the duck-rabbit illusion and the unreadable image. Emptiness is not nihilism or a denial of realness since the chair remains real and useful at its level from a physical point of view, but it is not “real” as it appears in experience independently of the processes that make it appear and function as what is registered as chair.

Gestalt appearances do not require a thing to receive complete ontological authority. The system needs enough compression and precision for a chair to appear and function as a chair, but it does not need to treat “chair” as an inherent structure that exists independently of the field. Ontological authority is the degree to which the field builds further inference on a gestalt once it has emerged, like how many priors depend on it. To be clear about the difference between precision weighting and ontological authority, we can say precision is about how strongly the system holds a prediction, not about dependent structures. Crucially, you can lower a gestalt's authority while it still appears clearly in experience. For instance, "dog" can appear as a real thing, while very little has to depend on it, like "all dogs are dangerous" which can produce subsequent action like clenching muscles. The gestalt is fully available, but the field has stopped automatically making predictions and actions depend on it.

If “I am unsafe” has high ontological authority, the field still makes predictions about input, but ambiguous input is assimilated into branches associated with “I am unsafe” and lowers the precision assigned to safety evidence. An unsafe attractor state may therefore have a deep or wide basin due to high ontological authority, making alternative configurations difficult to reach. In the attractor state, only certain elements like specific priors have high ontological authority, not necessarily everything within the field configuration. However, high ontological authority does not necessarily always give way to a deep or wide basin. A thing can be authoritative within a narrow context yet disappear outside it, like a certain frame applied by choice. As a more flexible example with lower authority, a chair can appear clearly and guide the act of sitting without its “chairness” governing every interpretation. It can become a step, sculpture, or pile of wood, so its ontological authority is contextual and limited. Ontological authority differs from reification because reification concerns a construction appearing independently as “real” so it is more static, while ontological authority concerns how strongly that construction governs further perception and action. Reification is about how the gestalt appears to the system, while authority is about how the system uses it. This also connects to the process of real-making as laid out in the loop of refinement section as anything that is made real through the loop, such as religion, has high(er) ontological authority.

Ontological authority determines how much of the field a violation has to propagate through, which is what the energetic triad measures as cost. High authority means many other priors rest on the gestalt, so revising it forces recomputation of everything dependent on it. A low authority gestalt can be dropped cheaply because little structure was resting on it. This helps us refine the definition of what depth implies in the dependency tree: a prior’s depth matches the degree to which other gestalts use it as a premise, and updating cost comes partly from how many of the dependent priors must be recomputed. This also means authority is self-maintaining because a high-authority gestalt structures ambiguous input, so that input integrates cheaply in the field and reads as confirming evidence for the active predictions while counter-input is encoded as friction to be explained away. This means resonance and friction end up reinforcing whatever already has authority rather than telling you whether it is accurate. This is why open refinement loops are important so we do not fall into the trap of treating cheap integration of input as evidence that we are right. This also means the two orientations the refinement loops take, real-making and truth-making, matter: real-making raises ontological authority which makes real-made things expensive to update, and truth-making requires exposing it to external feedback the system cannot plan for. Authority also keeps integration of input cheap by pruning the search space of the dependency tree’s branches, so lowering it means holding open alternatives the system had stopped computing, which is why lowering authority requires slack and tends to fail when the system has little.

Connecting this to somatics, it seems as though rigid or latched contraction is ontological authority held in the body. This is why a simple representation of safety typically doesn't work against a highly tense body. Haptic contraction gives a gestalt authority in the embodied substrate, since the contraction a prediction produces becomes interoceptive input that the predicted state is real, and interoceptive priors sit deep in the dependency tree so the signals are weighted heavily over most other input. Bringing slack into this, having alternative branches of input available means not latching or contracting, so there is a beginning point of using a lot of resources in order to hold other branches possible instead of defaulting to what the field is conditioned towards. A latch, meaning a contraction pattern held long enough to become rigid, does the same job as a premise because holding it in the body keeps one interpretation of input “sticky” and the system can depend on it without recomputing the held knowledge.

In terms of the compression gradient, to examine a gestalt for learning or updating, we must first objectify it by making it stable and clear enough to inspect, which means compressing it from the embodied layer, what the dependency tree uses as its physical substrate, into a representation or a piece of notation. A gestalt that holds high ontological authority resists compression because it exists as the structure itself doing the compression rather than as an object obvious to be compressed. This is why the inverse of the constructive process is hard: the field has to make stable and inspect what it is currently using as its guiding structure. For instance, a chair decompresses easily because not much else rests on the “chair” construction, while the sense of self does not because almost everything else depends on the “sense of self” structure. As another instance, the grammar rules of a language of a native speaker stay hidden because those rules are the structure generating the judgment rather than content available. To describe the highly authoritative grammar rules explicitly requires the hidden structure to be objectified, which means moving from the embodied layer into notation which is essentially the loop of refinement.

To sum up the relationships going on here: precision is how much weight or confidence a prediction is given in the current field. Ontological authority is how strongly an emergent gestalt functions as a structure for organizing further perception and action. Depth describes how many other structures depend on it across contexts, while reification describes the construction becoming invisible and appearing inherent or independently real.

precision makes the gestalt stable and useable → other inference starts treating it as structure, which is authority → dependence accumulates across contexts, which is depth → the construction stops being noticed at all, which is reification.

If we question what is or was of high ontological authority, we have the chance to break through our current way of seeing things which is typically the core target of novel discoveries. This is exactly what Grothendieck meant here in this quote:

But let us return to the dream, and to the prohibition that has been imposed on mathematics for thousands of years. This is perhaps the most inveterate of all the preconceptions, often implicit and rooted in habit, which decrees that such and such a thing is “maths” and such other things not. It took millenniums before things as childish and ubiquitous as the groups of symmetries of certain geometric figures, the topological forms of some others, the number zero and the sets found admission into the sanctuary! When I talk to students about the topology of a sphere, and the shapes that can be deduced from a sphere by adding handles - things that don’t surprise young children, but which confuse the students because they think they know what “maths” is - the first spontaneous echo I get is: but this is not maths! Maths, of course, means the Pythagorean theorem, the heights of a triangle and second degree polynomials... These students are no more stupid than you or me; they react as all the mathematicians in the world have reacted from time immemorial until today, except for people like Pythagoras or Riemann and maybe five or six others. Even Poincaré, who was not the first to come along, managed to prove by a well-felt philosophical A plus B that infinite sets were not maths! Surely there must have been a time when triangles and squares were not maths - they were drawings that children or pottery craftsmen traced on the sand or in the clay of vases, so as not to make mistakes...

To be clear, Grothendieck is speaking more so of categories and frames here like “When I talk to students about the topology of a sphere…” but categories can just as easily be gestalts as an item like a chair can be. The core difference is in legibility: categories are not typically well defined in the notational sense while items, like chairs, are obviously “there.” Again this is because in the structure the thing is difficult to see clearly: categories and frames are operating as the structure itself which makes them harder to objectify than an object “out there” like a chair.

Emptiness as a tool lowers ontological authority not by preventing gestalts from appearing, but by revealing that their authority is made from causes and conditions, which are mutable. We can use emptiness wisely to develop a more fluid state of being in which we hold more capacity for moving between states. This reduces suffering and increases the potential for intelligence by widening the range of field configurations that remain reachable. Because objects are pointers towards field configurations (section 5.3), seeing an object as empty makes the configuration it was pointing towards directly addressable. This means you can satisfy the lasting properties of the field through other routes than just the ones organized by the original object, or see that the target was associated with deep priors like permanence or selfhood and release the pressure there.

Constructional transparency, a property of a field configuration in relation to particular gestalts, is the practical capacity to maintain this recognition while keeping structuring gestalts alive, allowing them to guide experience without becoming a rigid structure that everything else has to be recomputed against. To be alive, to survive as a human, we must structure sensory input somehow, so some things need high ontological authority. The key question is not “should we stop giving authority to things in general” but rather “what should we give authority to, how much to give, and in what contexts.”

6.2 Transparency

As mentioned before, transparency is the degree to which the processes producing a gestalt remain apparent in experience, on all three layers of the compression gradient, while the gestalt is still being experienced and used. This is not to be confused with phenomenal transparency, which is the invisibility of a model’s construction. Transparency is hard when ontological authority is high because a gestalt that other structures depend on is what the field is currently running on rather than something the field can perceive as an object. Reification is the “extreme” case of this: construction of a thing is not seen at all and is taken to be inherent to reality. Transparency is not preventing all structure; we still need that to survive and go about our day. It is preventing structure from becoming hidden so we can see through what other interpretations of input can offer. Transparency becomes practical then rather than theoretical because it offers us paths of freedom in perception we would not usually have due to branches in the dependency tree opening up to us.

Transparency is about redistributing precision in helpful ways. We can move precision in the dependency tree from the whole of the gestalt towards its constituent parts that make up its construction. This means the whole loses its high authority status without fully being removed from experience. This leads to ontological authority and organization of experience coming apart. A gestalt can keep organizing experience while still being held as transparent. For example, you can still see a dog and react to danger if it appears, but now the reaction to danger only appears depending on the current instance of dog and its parts, not every time a dog shows up. The gestalt of dog did not disappear since it still organizes certain parts like "4 legs" and "fur"; what went away is the overgeneralization that the original gestalt kept providing which was causing inaccurate modeling across all situations. When we start placing more weight on the parts making up the gestalt in the present moment instead of allowing it to hold top-down power, we start contracting less. To take a deeper example, "I am unsafe" is a gestalt that, if given enough ontological authority, can produce lots of suffering in every moment due to producing unhelpful targets and contraction. When it is given low authority, we can see its parts like a fast heartbeat, which allows us to redirect precision in a way that moves from the top-down label of unsafe to simply "oh this is just a fast heartbeat." Bear in mind simply moving precision to constructing parts is sometimes not enough since deep latched contraction in the body can hold patterns that feed back into the original gestalt's confirming evidence.

Once a gestalt stops being treated as a static structure after enough practice, it still integrates input at the same cost, if not less. This is because the parts now prune the dependency tree instead of the whole gestalt, so you keep the cheap integration while losing authority. Importantly, the integration cost staying the same or reducing occurs after enough practice; when first trying to hold the gestalt as transparent, it can be expensive to compute the individual parts in each moment, and can fail when you don't have enough slack like when tired. We might retreat back into old patterns that are a product of the old gestalt organizing experience. So, transparency is not about simply flipping a switch; it is an active training process done until parts can be seen in detail and be taken as paths in the dependency tree.

If we look back on the constructive process and its inverse, it is clear transparency is the practical version of de-chunking a gestalt into relations and tracing them to conditions. If we want to use this inverse process successfully, reification must drop so the internal workings can start to be seen through rather than as invisible. This leads to the deconstruction process leaving more branches open in the dependency tree due to components becoming separately addressable, so each one suggests paths the original gestalt left out of reach.

Crucially, trying to achieve high transparency can be difficult due to perspective limitations. A high authority gestalt is what the field is running on for structure, so naturally trying to see through it will be like trying to see your own eyes. Transparency then in some cases becomes an achievement of getting around perspectival limitations. Holding alternative organizations of experience costs resource capacity like attention, which means we need a sufficient level of slack. Transparency can fail under conditions like fatigue and threat due to this if it is not conditioned in the field enough.

This is because transparency works by changing whether the field contracts and commits to a certain configuration at all. Transparency does not simply add more capacity, but that can be a side effect. To understand this, "having slack" can mean two different things: the spare capacity you have to spend to get out of a stuck state, and the condition that determines whether the field latches onto a state in the first place. Factors like unclenching and good sleep raise the former, and transparency and equanimity help the latter. A fully rested person with good health can still easily latch onto stuck states which lie in deep attractor basins, like holding onto critique that someone gave them and getting upset about it. On the other hand, someone with high equanimity who can see through the construction of gestalts can let the critique drop quickly even if they are tired or have poor external circumstances. This is why releasing contraction, both bodily and mentally, doesn't simply fade back into the background felt tone like a new material item would—what was removed was capacity uselessly spent that can now be spent elsewhere. This is also why rest and insight are not the same—someone can take a month-long break from a situation, come back, and still latch back onto the same field configuration just as easily as before if they become triggered. You also can't do the opposite: have the insight but be depleted in terms of capacity and expect the change to stay active. Someone with high equanimity might reach extreme exhaustion and not be able to access that skill, and fall back into latched patterns if they are still kept in the system somewhere. Again, the important skill here is equanimity, which allows competing branches in the dependency tree to stay open so passing configurations do not become latched again.

Take for example trying to de-chunk the parts of high anxiety when in a deep attractor basin. You can know the “correct” move in this case, but seeing it as transparent is too costly from the system’s perspective. This also tells us why depth of priors and gestalts predicts difficulty in seeing them as transparent: trunk-level structure resists change because almost everything depends on it. Another problem of gaining transparency is context-sensitivity in which ontological authority can drop in one context while staying high in another, which is one reason why you need multiple passes of the refinement loop. Like with high anxiety states, one context can make the state authoritative enough that transparency does not occur, while in another the same components separate easily.

Another important aspect about cultivating transparency deals with the depth of the gestalt you are attempting to recognize as transparent instead of authoritative. We can view experience across a ladder of perspectives, where each step in either direction reveals a layer of construction that is either easier or harder to see through. For instance, if we take the shallow gestalt of anxiety itself as a thing, on the outer branches the gestalt might be something like “these sensations are scary” and seeing that as transparent just means deconstructing the sensations themselves. Deeper in the tree is something like “these sensations are happening to me,” which is a threat prior that made the interpretation of sensations feel highly authoritative in the first place. Making this gestalt transparent changes every situation in which similar sensations occur, not just this one. We can go all the way to the trunk until we hit something like thing-ness itself or innate priors. Each deeper position is harder to see through as a construction because more depends on it, which makes updating proportionally costly. Depth of the gestalt being targeted predicts both the difficulty and the generality of the change through transparency. Whether repeated practice on the outer branches loosens deeper structures through propagation or simply builds the clarity and slack needed for direct deep work is unclear; either way, working with outer structure matters.

When it comes to somatics, as stated earlier, latched contraction associated with the trunk of the dependency tree is high ontological authority held in the body. Because of this, epistemic transparency without bodily tension release allows the reified gestalt to propagate back through the system, as interoceptive evidence remains structured by the very thing being deconstructed. This means holding transparency and releasing haptic contraction are processes that end up with similar results, since both lower the precision and embodied reinforcement keeping a prior stable. However, this mechanism only applies to contraction that has not been held long. If contraction has been held long enough in the body as to make itself deep, like through habitual repetition of suppression, the range of patterns the mind-body system can produce and manage becomes narrowed and stays that way. At that point, releasing the contraction and restoring the patterns that were closed off become two different processes, not just one. Meaning, releasing deep, latched contraction in one instance that has been in the system for a long time (exact time unknown) does not mean you will automatically regain the patterns that were lost due to that stuck contraction. In speculation, deep haptic contraction sits in a basin and needs extra resources, like energy, supplied from the outside to escape and be released. This is why interventions like psychedelics, heat, and sustained attention are ultimately achieving the same thing and can result in lasting change. An analogous metaphor here is like a joint stuck in a cast: in the short term, you remove the cast and the joint moves again; after enough time spent in the cast, the literal tissue of the body changes, so producing the range of the joint again means having to use it and practice until it becomes viable. This implies that a collapsed dependency tree due to deep, latched haptic contraction produces reachability constraints to other field configurations. So, you might be able to “see” through a deep prior that is “stuck,” but other field configurations will be unreachable because the body cannot supply the state the other configuration needs. This also increases the importance of slack for deep change because we need to inch ourselves closer to the states we want to be in that were once unreachable through repetition in small amounts until the body can handle them. Equanimity then matters even more as its own skill, since holding intensity without the field collapsing prevents deep contraction from forming in the first place, while transparency is what expands possibilities that were once collapsed.

Expanding on the importance of sensory clarity, it seems as though sensory clarity is transparency viewed through an embodied lens: de-chunking a sensation gestalt by resolving it into changing lower-level sensations. This is why sensory clarity reduces suffering according to the energetic triad, because once a sensation stops functioning as an authoritative structure, less of the field has to be organized around it, so the ongoing cost of integrating input lessens and the negative felt tone decreases.

And in general, this is why transparency reduces suffering. The suffering loop as laid out in section 5.2 starts with first-order hurt appearing as a gestalt that carries high authority which organizes the field around it, and its subsequent branches like beliefs that the hurt should not be happening. This then leaves control, craving or aversion as the only available action policy to be enacted to remove that target error. Once the construction of the gestalt is known, less of the field has to be organized around it, so the ongoing integration cost of input falls, and each part of the gestalt branches out separately in the dependency tree, so there are more action policies available. This mechanism can also apply to memories, since recalling one reinstates its relevant field configuration and somatic pattern (contingent on context like slack, mood, and environment). When the memory's gestalts hold high authority, the contraction latches, which then confirms the prediction that produced it. When the contraction is not reinstated, the configuration still reconstructs and sensations may arise, like a fast heartbeat, but they are not tightly bound to being defended against, so the loop that would reinforce the relevant field configuration and contraction does not run.

Making transparency of structure obvious can make certain field configurations more reachable by opening paths the constructed gestalt closed. When a gestalt the configuration depended on loses enough ontological authority, other contending branches in the dependency tree stay active as available paths rather than being pruned. This then weakens the attractor basin holding the current field configuration as being stable by reducing depth and width. The conditions for exiting the basin become easier to meet due to barriers being dropped and more options being open, so the asymmetry between entering and exiting the state is reduced. Transparency does not completely remove the basin, though, since a configuration is also instantiated by other things like bodily states, environment, and attention which can all keep reinstating it.

Importantly, when training transparency, it should be made clear that the level of the thing you are targeting to recognize as being transparent matters a great deal. Meaning, the hierarchical position of the structure you are attempting to see the construction of determines if it will actually have a meaningful impact or not. If we decide to make gestalts transparent that occupy the same position in the dependency tree, we would end up working towards little. Let’s say the gestalt of “this anxiety is dangerous” is seen as constructed and its inner workings are made obvious, which reduces its ontological authority status; no longer will it organize the field to its liking. However, there’s a high chance that the gestalt of “this frustration is unbearable” will simply “take over” that spot because they occupy very similar positions in the dependency tree. By remaining shallow, they both do not have a deep organizational impact on experience, so the chance transparency affects structure that depends on those gestalts is low. Now, that is assuming the transparency insights simply stay on those outer branches, but because transparency targets their construction, it is not uncommon for deeper changes to propagate deeper in the dependency tree; these types of changes, however, will take longer to occur rather than just the shallow gestalt becoming transparent. If we spend enough time holding transparency around these shallow gestalts, there is a chance the insights will propagate toward the trunk and reach deep gestalts like “there are solid sensations.” However, if this happens, and if shallow gestalts are simply “replaced” when they no longer have high ontological authority, then it would make sense to target deep gestalts that have a much more profound impact on organizing the field. This way, we can make bigger changes faster through targeting generative structures, which has the potential to allow us to move between states with more freedom. Intuitively, the system would need to be holding a sufficient amount of slack here for these changes to be optimal instead of destabilizing, which is why moving toward the trunk at a steady pace can be helpful instead of simply only targeting the deepest thing we can.

It seems as though “thing-ness” is the deepest gestalt we can practically target to hold as transparent within our system without becoming completely destabilized. Crucially, thing-ness is simply a construction like all other gestalts, meaning its parts and interactions matter in the way we target it to hold it as transparent. Its construction seems to be made up of processes like input being segmented into bounded units, those units cohering as single things, and their persistence across a seemingly continuous temporal slice. If we can lower the ontological authority of thing-ness, we have a chance at reducing a massive amount of suffering due to almost every other gestalt depending on it, so the core action policy of craving and aversion loses the structure it was using as a substrate, and that policy stops being the only available response.

In terms of the energetic triad, holding thing-ness as transparent means the field no longer has to hold it as a high-authority structure, so the ongoing cost of integrating input falls, and an update pertaining to any particular gestalt that depends on thing-ness propagates less through the system, so negative differences in felt tone shrink. This also changes what resonance and friction are signals of, since they now keep track of integrating input into a field with more available branches, which makes the surprise of resonance or friction propagate through less structure in the system so the difference in experience is smaller. In terms of the baseline of experience, the background felt tone rises because fewer resources go to holding reified things, and because fewer of the dependency tree’s targets are ones that the world cannot fulfill, like permanence, so there is less error and hurt in experience. A situation is then held in the body as something with moving parts rather than as one solid thing that has to be enacted, which is why the felt shift becomes available more often due to more pathways being opened up by differentiating the solid thing.

(Credit to Roger Thisdell’s A Very Streamlined Enlightenment Protocol for first introducing me to the concept of targeting deep priors)

To be clear, holding transparency around gestalts does not mean the way you model things is now more truthful or corresponds with reality. Something can be seen as constructed yet still be wrong, which is why open loops of refinement are necessary. However, transparency does make the refinement loop easier as it clears barriers that would normally prevent updating, like defense mechanisms. As laid out in the suffering loop section, epistemic suffering is when we hold craving or aversion around epistemic models or concepts and habitually contract around them so they do not update. Seeing a model as constructed out of conditions rather than as reified reality means input that would usually create friction is no longer a threat that needs to be avoided. Objectification is a step in the refinement loop, since we have to clearly isolate the thing first to inspect it, and high ontological authority blocks this by making the thing the structure itself instead of an object. Once we can successfully inspect the parts a gestalt depends on, we can run the loop on those, which means resonance and friction can pertain to deeper, more fundamental priors, which means more dependent things get updated. This helps undercut the self-maintenance problem of gestalts confirming themselves through regeneration of contraction. This is analogous to the sensory clarity skill in meditation: seeing conditions of a high authority gestalt is like seeing how bodily sensations are made up, through input becoming more differentiated over time. This is why blurriness, the opposite of sensory clarity and transparency, causes suffering: it regenerates the conditions of the substrate giving way to the suffering in the first place.

As for failure modes like depersonalization and derealization, the conditions leading to these states are complex and I’m not sure if there is any one algorithmic formula to understand how they work, but what is obvious is that these types of states are not about holding transparency, but about over-reifying things. The structures organizing experience are still given high authority; there is just a disconnection between certain ones that matter to the system like “real-ness” and “self-hood” through the loss of interoceptive evidence. I believe states like DP/DR are configurations we enter into when our system does not hold enough slack to handle the way it is organizing experience. This is why releasing contraction and “surrendering” to the state can be helpful: it stops the system from spending resources on the policy that the state be different, which is the contraction keeping the state up in the first place.

Lastly, developing transparency should not lead to paralysis. A system holding wise and genuine transparency can still compress things and commit to singular interpretations. Nothing about recognizing construction of gestalts should require holding every alternative branch open, which would be costly and useless. The difference is that the system can update when context changes, rather than being stuck with whatever field configuration it started with. Acting firmly while holding a gestalt with low ontological authority is not in tension, since compression is what makes action possible and transparency makes updating possible. Think of someone who holds a model so close and tightly that nothing can penetrate it, like a flat-earther. Then action may be firm, but in no way can they enter the loop of refinement oriented towards truth-making which would allow them to see the belief as transparent. This is often what happens as we age and continue to hold rigid models about how things work.

This points to what the important target is in this framework: ontological authority is not the problem itself, it is authority that the system cannot see clearly and drop. Committing to a gestalt fully while keeping its authority low is what kids do when they play, what adults do when thinking within specific frames, and what phenomenological practices can do when propagating feelings of safety down into the mind-body system. All of these moves depend on the system’s ability to treat a thing as given, then stop on command without contraction. The core question then becomes what to grant authority to and when. This is how sanity and wisdom come to matter.

7. Sanity, Wisdom, and Practice

7.1 Sanity & Wisdom

Now that we have established that any gestalt can in principle be held transparently and that it is made of causes and conditions no different from optical illusions, it is now a question of how to use this insight wisely. What exactly should we be moving towards? This of course is a highly debated topic already, for example what ethics argues about, but I want to give the answer that best fits this framework.

First we must differentiate between sanity and wisdom as they work together to provide maximal wellbeing and flourishing. Sanity is defined as holding gestalts with enough compression to take action and enough transparency to update, while having the ability to move between levels of abstraction when the situation calls for it. Wisdom is that same skill oriented towards targets that are worth weighting and whose subsequent actions matter. Sanity is a prerequisite for wisdom rather than an independent property, as you cannot select targets freely from inside a field you have no clear view of.

Here are two quotes that capture the essence of these tools:

“[Consciousness of abstracting can be defined as] awareness that in our process of abstracting we have left out characteristics” — Alfred Korzybski

Sanity = Lucidly climbing up & down the branches of abstraction

A big source of confusion I spot in people is when they get stuck attending to reality at stifling levels of abstraction; either by going too complex or too simple, and not being able to scale to a different level when that would get them out of a confuddlement.

“Everything is Yin and Yang! No, it’s all One! No, it’s neither, it’s non-dual! But my five family members are coming over for dinner and I need to remember to buy 14 items at the supermarket, and calculate my taxes. And my brother is probably going to bring up politics which is so overwhelmingly complicated! But it’s all One...”

So many minds get stuck trying to figure out the forest, when maybe what they really need to do is look at the individual trees. Or sometimes they need to get down to ground level and pick through the dirt, or sometimes take a bird’s eye view of the canopy, except they’re stuck or don’t realise everything else has moved on.

The mind chunks and fuses reality all the time - you may have noticed. But how much have you really noticed, and how much flexible control do you have over this process? At least become lucid of it.

I have found that by recognizing this feature of mind, and becoming better at navigating strata of abstraction, my sense of clarity and sanity has gone way up.

Great spiritual development is to be able to nimbly both reach lower on the levels of abstractions - all the way to zero* - AND be able to hold onto higher and higher levels of abstraction, extending our consciousness capacity, before we project to infinity (aka ‘more than I can make sense of’).

— Roger Thisdell

The abstraction ladder operates as a continuous hierarchical spectrum across experience, acting as an interface for different levels of the dependency tree. Moving up the ladder is an act of compression by dropping details into high-level patterns which collapse a dense data set. This is seeing the entire forest out of many trees or concluding that “it’s all one.” The inverse is decompression; moving down the ladder. This is decompressing a summary into its working parts and details, like seeing the individual branches of all the trees in a forest. Neither end of the ladder is superior since they both come with trade-offs depending on the context. Compression gives you strategic orientation, while decompression gives you specific available action. You can see this pattern in goals: the goal is the high-level global goal-post that orients attention and the details of the goal are what set the actions to get there. The main failure mode here is getting stuck at a point in the ladder. When the mind-body system loses the fluid capacity to move between levels, confusion and suffering set in and we try to navigate certain contexts with the wrong level of compression in mind, like getting buried in details when all we need is a high-level overview.

However, flexibility by itself does not guarantee sanity, since a mind-body system can be infinitely flexible within a closed or limited field. When a gestalt becomes large enough to structure every piece of input, it creates a rigid loop where every piece of counter-evidence is integrated as confirmation instead of error. Inside this closed loop, we receive lots of internal resonance because our interpretations feel right due to rigid structuring. However, resonance is a signal of unexpected lower integration cost; it says nothing of the system’s level of corresponding to reality. This is why sanity depends on open loops. Without constraints that the field could not have produced on its own, there is no way to tell a model that corresponds with reality from one that has simply become good at integrating everything. Sanity then fails in two ways: through rigid contraction around a single level of abstraction, or through too much flexibility with no external feedback that only provides resonance.

You can see these mechanics play out in how a child integrates something they lack the accurate priors for. Without the average adult-filtered gestalt to compress input into, children reach for whatever priors they do have, often animate and relational, and build a representation out of them.

Take this monologue from a child in this short-form video:

Why do humans skip rocks?

I think that the river likes games. Not big games, just tiny ones.

You throw a bad rock, it eats it. You throw a good one, it lets it skip a few times… like it’s deciding like no, noo, maybe, no, yes!

You think you’re throwing rocks but really you’re asking the river a question and every time it answers.

The first time I heard this I was amazed, because what adult is ever going to come up with that kind of metaphor? Adults have the compressed and factually correct dead gestalts that explain rock skipping like angles and spin. The child’s version does not correspond with reality, but it is genuinely “alive” and an act of construction reaching across a large fit-distance to build a vivid gestalt, mapping the felt sense of the event onto the embodied, animate priors they have stored. This is the same metaphor mechanism described earlier in the essay, but it is coming from a less-pruned, less conditioned field so it takes a different orientation.

"I think the optimal state of maturity for an adult is to be like a Wise Old Child," Vacha states on Twitter. This is the mature form of childlike wonder. A child sees things as more empty without trying, because their field has not yet solidified into "solid" reifications, which is why so many things feel fresh and vivid and new. The average adult's world feels solid and dull because heavy conditioning has compressed things into stable, cheap to integrate, reified chunks. Interestingly, psychedelics have similar results through loosening rigid deep priors, just as seeing emptiness and releasing contraction does. As Michael Pollan puts it, "My own gut is there is something childlike about the consciousness at certain stages in the trip. Just judging from the experience of having had a kid, that there are levels of sensitivity to environment that children have that we lose as adults because of our desire to focus. We shut things out." Contemplative practice is partly a deliberate return to that newness, not by having fewer priors like the child, but by holding them in expansion rather than contraction: keeping them as light as a feather but as useful as a sharp knife, seeing their emptiness while keeping the competence.

However, this sanity is not yet wisdom. We may describe children as whimsical and full of life, but not wise. So what are they missing? Orientation towards targets the moment calls for.

Take for example a conman. A conman that scams people can be sane; they can read a situation well, move between states flexibly, and update when they need to. But we would not call this wise. Flexibility alone does not choose the right targets we should be oriented towards. Wisdom adds this orientation, like which targets are actually worth weighting, and what outcomes matter to the field.

What makes this hard to fully state is that wisdom does not seem to be algorithmic. When we find someone, an action, or notation wise, we do not apply that characteristic because they are enacting some set of rules that follows the right guidelines. Wisdom seems closer to something known through negation, like good health, which is not something added on top of experience, but what remains once contraction preventing flexibility is released. Here, you do not acquire wisdom by just adopting the correct model; you understand it by releasing the contraction and reified structure that were blurring what you could see and do when in other states. Wittgenstein's point applies here too, since the target(s) of wisdom cannot be fully compressed into a statement of what it is and what it is aiming towards. This makes wisdom easier to describe through negation because it is side-stepping the direct compression needed to put its felt sense into words. This is because holding gestalts that we must use for structure as transparent is a difficult task due to an apparent “invisible” quality. It might be said wisdom is reflexive, only known after the fact through examination of the person-environment system, but distortion is still needed to be dropped for it to be cultivated and used in the first place. However, just because some contraction is dropped does not mean wisdom is guaranteed. This is the case of the spiritual leader who has ethical scandals or acts in such a way that it is obvious some amount of blockage is still present in their field preventing their dependency tree from containing what is needed.

Lastly, the targets of wisdom matter. Removing targets from the field is impossible and not the goal of wisdom. From the suffering loop, direction comes from the target while pressure comes from the second-order control we believe we must enact to prevent something intolerable. Wisdom here has the ability to keep the direction but drop the pressure, which makes action decisive without a single action policy feeling necessary. Equanimity here comes into play as a side effect by leaving targets and actions available without making them feel necessary. Apathy contrasts this by dropping salience across the field so nothing feels worth targeting. Wisdom and equanimity work together by leaving more options alive for the mind-body system to enact well-calibrated policies by dropping pressure.

Practically, the most useful model here is running the loop of refinement with transparency underlying its process. When a gestalt forms, you can recognize it as a construction and recognize the level of abstraction it sits at, such as whether it is too detailed or too broad. You can attempt to check how much of the field depends on it and what alternatives it is preventing you from seeing. You can act from a level of compression knowing the advantages and disadvantages for climbing the layers. And you can question what in the field needs revising like precision, the model itself, the targets, or the action policies. Then all of it can run again, iterating on itself. Crucially, there is no global endpoint here; there never is an end to insight, both in terms of wisdom and intellectual capacity. This is because holding a structure as transparent eventually ends up as the default in the field, which then eventually becomes transparent in its own turn. Each structure you see through becomes available to be objectified for the next round, so each iteration of refinement updates what is reachable in the field by expanding the space of what is available as interpretation and action rather than shrinking availabilities. The priors underlying all of this are not thrown away, just reinstantiated moment-to-moment. This is why holding different levels of the dependency tree as transparent matters, as it enables you to change your relationship to the structure rather than just sitting on the same insights.

All of this is driven by a recognition that felt senses can be wrong, both friction and resonance. Resonance only deals with the field you are working with, so a field that can integrate everything without question will feel right even when it has stopped corresponding to reality and complexity. Sanity and wisdom are not about arriving at a singular, correct representation or field. They are about understanding that flexibility and feedback are crucial to increase well-being and our intellectual capacity, and knowing that maps are never the territory, but they can still be accurate.

I’d like to leave this off with an Alexander Grothendieck quote that eloquently captures the essence of this conclusion and why it matters.

It is surely no coincidence that the natural process of any real research never appears in texts or talks that are supposed to communicate and transmit the substance of what has been “found”. Texts and talks most often are limited to recording the “results”, which must be made in a form that appears to the common people as austere and immutable laws, inscribed from all eternity in granite tables of a sort of giant library, and dictated by some omniscient God to insider-scriber-scholars and assimilated; to those who write scholarly books and no less scholarly articles, those who transmit knowledge from a pulpit, or in the smaller circle of a seminar. Is there a single textbook, a single manual for the use of schoolchildren, high school students, university students, even “our researchers”, which can give the unfortunate reader the slightest idea of what research is - if not just the universally accepted idea that research is when you are very knowledgeable, when you have passed lots of exams and even competitions, big heads, Pasteur and Curie and the Nobel prizes and all that... We as readers or listeners, ingesting the best we can the Knowledge that these great men were kind enough to record for the good of humanity, we are barely good enough (if we work hard) to pass our exam at the end of the year, and then over and over again...

How many are there, including among the unfortunate “researchers” themselves, in need of theses or articles, including even among the most “scholarly”, most prestigious among us - those who have the naivety to see that “research” is neither more nor less than questioning things, passionately - like a child who wants to know how he or his little sister came into the world. To search and to find, that is to say: to question and to listen is the simplest, most natural thing in the world, in which no one in the world has a privilege. It is a “gift” that we all have received since the cradle - made to express and flourish with an infinity of faces, from one moment to another and from one person to another...

— Alexander Grothendieck

7.2 Practice

"For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g., men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts."

– Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics

Through this statement, Aristotle asserts that practice is of utmost importance, for without practice, we cannot obtain higher virtues. We cannot think our way through getting better at a skill, just as we cannot become just by thinking about justice. The thoughts and reasoning themselves can contribute but only if practice is enacted, for without practice, no demands are made on the world, and our field cannot cultivate those results.

This framework gives a clear and intuitive picture as to why practice matters so much: the field can be trained by cumulative experience. The current field our system holds and uses has been shaped and handled by a compressed history of what we ourselves have repeatedly perceived, attended to, believed, enacted, and received feedback about. Practice matters because it molds the field in a way that will determine how later input is reasoned about, interpreted, and acted on. It is one of the most controllable mechanisms we have over the field, which is crucially what gives humans a unique edge over other living systems. We have the ability to use tools like reasoning and representations, whether internal or external, to enlarge what can be called the 'cognitive light cone' of living systems. Practice then is important not only for pragmatic applications of theoretical thought, but also for inspecting and using the field itself to result in self-authorship, an ability to reshape the substrate experience relies on. We need to practice wisdom to be wise, to practice seeing construction of gestalts to hold transparency, to practice not habitually contracting so we don't suffer.

Practice then is repeated engagement that directs attention and awareness across varying spatial and temporal levels, using feedback to retune the field's dispositions and dynamics to match our targets. Practice can vary across differing levels of precision weighting, not just repetition. Experiences can be weighted differently due to factors like mood and situational context, so a few highly weighted practice moves can be more influential than many weak ones. Two people can practice the same thing for the same amount, but come away with different results. Thus attention plays a role: what we weight and attend to when practicing affects field retunement. Practice is selective training of the field because of this, not just passive absorption. Temporally, practice can work across multiple timescales through attention and awareness. Moment-to-moment attention changes what is available right now; dwelling on something retunes the current field; repetition changes parameters and deeper priors, and "offline" periods like consolidation while sleeping support structural and implicit changes. These varying levels offer a reason why insight by itself without practice is not enough: insight may alter the moment-to-moment field, but not the deeper underlying properties of our default field that regenerates future states.

Tying this to the compression gradient, different depths of knowledge change at different speeds, which matters for the amount of practice you must do to reach a target. Notation can change quickly like rewriting a sentence; updating an internal model can be harder due to interacting parts, and embodied knowledge or deep priors can take the longest since the body is where our deepest compression lives. A single piece of feedback containing a contradiction you must update towards will not instantly update your tacit knowledge that lives in the body, but it can update your notational expression. This is field dissonance coming into play, since different levels of the compression gradient update differently, so they may seemingly be at odds.

When it comes to slack and practice, holding enough of it is of utmost importance so updating and action can be done as effectively as possible. Slack is a prerequisite itself for corrective practice, since it allows the field to hold error which is competing branches across the dependency tree. If we cannot hold error without immediately resolving towards habitual interpretations and active inference, we will not be able to gradually refine them. Without enough slack, repetition tends to reenact existing habits instead of producing exploration or updating. And as stated in the slack section, it is specific to the task at hand; slack is not just one out-of-context thing. Someone can access abilities in one field configuration while being unable to access them under states that reduce slack, like anxiety or social pressure. Thus, the needed capacity for practice to be effective needs to be reachable during the practice itself. This emphasizes the importance of mediums that increase channels of slack like routines, rituals, external notation, and supportive friends, because these things reduce background uncertainty and preserve more capacity for ideal updating. On the inverse, a volatile environment requires more slack in certain areas for the same updating to occur. We can also talk about slack in terms of the domain and problem space the practice is being done in. Too few constraints on the practice create a very large search space of possibilities that leaves too many branches open in the field; too many constraints and you force premature collapse, preventing diverse practice or solutions that explore other branches. Good practice provides enough structure to make search navigable while preserving enough capacity to explore dynamic possibilities you otherwise would not.

From the loop of refinement section, a natural cycle or path for practice looks like this:

begin with a felt sense of understanding → turn that into a probe you can test → test the probe against the field or external structure → notice resonance, friction, or a baseline felt sense → locate what causes the response → update the representation or notation → begin again with the new felt sense of understanding

Crucially, you cannot force an embodied recognition of the updating of new knowledge. The embodied layer only comes from practice that is weighted enough. This is why notation and representations are crucial, because without them, our range of knowledge would be very limited in terms of the embodied layer. Because of this, making implicit material stable enough through notation and representations is crucial. A vague felt sense cannot easily be revised until it is made objectified. That said, not all practice necessarily involves using this loop exactly as stated, but the general mechanisms still drive change. For example, take wanting to practice a conversational skill: this is still stabilizing a felt sense through words. The words, the notation, are making the implicit stable so you can understand your current positioning. Without this, we may assume we can just enact the skill at any moment without practice, but when we try in a real situation, we come to realize our habitual interpretations and action take over without deliberate weighting, marking the importance of practice in general, and more specifically testing probes so you can really see where your knowledge breaks down.

Regarding resonance and friction when it comes to practice, these two signals simply measure surprise of integration cost, not truth or rightness. These two signals are useful guides, but taken to their extreme we get delusion and gradual habituation of branches in the dependency tree that may take us away from our long-term targets. A "false" idea can resonate through familiarity, and "true" information can produce friction, all the more reason to not let these things hold high ontological authority. Just as these things cannot give us accuracy about truth, they do not identify what is wrong or right. Resonance or friction may arise from the embodied, representational, or notational layer. It may also come from external evidence or be manipulated by lack of slack, so it is up to us to identify the source. Effective practice must diagnose the thing that was violated or satisfied by input before deciding what exactly to update. This is where open loops come into play because without them, repetition simply becomes self-reinforcement. Open loops introduce external feedback that otherwise could not be generated from the field doing the practice, meaning we get to explore branches of the dependency tree that would otherwise not be available for us to use.

The mechanics of practice and what it structurally changes are crucial to understand. Repetition with enough weighting turns recurring relations into chunks. When several priors activate together repeatedly, form a stable relation, and support successful action, the system compresses them into a single unit. The parts no longer need to be integrated separately and can be used together automatically. What this does is compound into chunks reducing more and more future search and integration costs. A chunk that has been used a good deal in practice shrinks the search space and frees attention, assuming targets remain stable. Of course, often this isn't the case as chunks allow us to explore targets that increase in their temporal and spatial qualities. For instance, learning new chunks in math now allows us to explore harder problems that otherwise would not be possible; naturally our system gravitates towards these problems to satisfy underlying targets. This also means practice makes chunks return to our baseline perception. As an organization becomes predictable and cheap to integrate, it stops producing obvious resonance or friction and becomes a part of our default field. This frees capacity, but it also means fluency is not a signal that new learning is occurring. It is also not a signal the chunks that are being integrated are not accurate, which is a danger. Every repetition of using a chunk makes it cheaper, but it also makes more depend on it. Practice deepens ontological authority, making the gestalt have more and more organizational power. If lots of things depend on inaccurate chunks, then relearning the thing will be costly to the system, making it much harder to pursue.

This is why ease alone is not enough for learning and effective practice: integrating input that is not costly in some way means we already have stored chunks related to that thing that are stored in the baseline. Slack is meaningful here when talking about deliberate practice, "mindful repetition on performance tasks just beyond the edge of one's capabilities." Difficulty by itself does not produce learning. Repeated perceived failure may update conditions towards threat detection, helplessness, or a freezing of committing to action policies. On the inverse, frictionless repetition of practice may deepen automaticity but cease producing refinement on one's edge of capabilities. Of course there are varying levels of worry here based on targets, like maybe some knowledge is needed to be conditioned deeply into the embodied layer so it's automatic, while some only needs to be brought up in a representation when needed.

It is crucial to understand the role of memory here because practice changes memory, and memory changes what can be perceived, constructed, and done. Thus, practice changes the problems the field can effectively understand and solve. Long-term memory, an outcome of practice, is not a simple stored thing then. Learning is a durable change in the dispositions from which future fields are constructed, influencing the configurations themselves. If we were to hold an account for the act of remembering something, we could say it is the moment-to-moment reconstruction of a gestalt from those dispositions under current cues like targets and constraints. Because of this, properties of the field like reachability matter because they determine if you can access a field configuration you need under certain cues. Something can be retained but inaccessible due to lack of slack, or unfamiliar framing. A configuration can be reachable under the present cues, but be inaccessible when those cues disappear. Therefore, understanding invariants, factors that stay the same across problems, is important so as not to be reliant on specific cues. Practice should remove support that will not be there later, like reference material, though this depends on the thing you are practicing. Spaced repetition or notational generation can test whether the field can reconstruct the correct structure after the temporary context has disappeared. This highlights the utmost importance of prerequisites when it comes to learning and therefore practice, because without the proper ones in place, the field configuration we need to access is literally unreachable based on the current priors we hold. As Justin Skycak puts it, "Having your prerequisites in place is the difference between something seeming confusing and inaccessible versus 'wait… that’s all it is?'"

The novice and expert therefore do not merely apply different amounts of reasoning to the same represented problem; their fields reconstruct the problem entirely differently. As Terence Tao says, "You’re so certain that a certain approach is going to work or a certain answer is going to be what you expect. So, sometimes asking the right question is key to actually getting a satisfactory answer." The expert will see relations, invariants, constraints, and available actions that the novice literally doesn't perceive, not because of magic, but because practice puts into place the proper dependency tree pathways. This highlights the importance of having fundamentals in a domain heavily conditioned, since all advanced and more specific chunks will depend on these fundamentals. Having fundamentals in place means lowering integration costs across the board against a wide variety of input, making later learning and integration easier. This leaves more slack for our system to handle uncertainty and higher-order possibilities. Based on the dependency tree model of how priors work, it can be said a great deal of learning is a matter of settling and connecting prerequisite priors into unified gestalts, each being a step towards greater understanding. Additionally, experts compared to novices do not merely perceive larger patterns than the novice with more nodes, but they can also distinguish differences within those patterns that the novice's field collapses together. This resembles the skill of sensory clarity discussed earlier: sensory clarity acts in the embodied layer to de-chunk a unified gestalt of sensations into its changing smaller parts, while expertise in a domain does the same thing with knowledge. The expert can notice fine details the novice might blackbox away and notice which parts of the chunks they are using are causing error.

When it comes to training data, learning, and practice, meaningful abstractions can be built from concrete examples, which makes them valuable. Encountering an abstraction, like a broad mathematical definition, gives the field little input to compress and work into itself. Practice with concrete examples therefore makes the compression wider, which has a higher chance of settling knowledge into the embodied layer. For the compression gradient, this is turning notation into representations, then folding this into the embodied. We can fold concrete examples into representations and properties of the field like constraints and variables, and by doing so we chunk these things together into increasingly efficient gestalts that organize experience. This knowledge can then be expressed back through notation, allowing us to share it for feedback. This is why memorizing a definition without true understanding can occur: the symbol, the notation, exists but is not held up by the lower layers through relational dynamics which therefore cannot be applied to new problems that have not been practiced. Without a representation behind a definition, how can we conserve the properties the definition holds and apply it elsewhere? High-quality training data allows us to practice holding structure and underlying chunks which can be applied to new input. This is the skill of structure-preserving transformations – comparing several things and detecting which properties hold across them. Practice of a thing will change context, cues, and examples, and using structure-preserving transformations trains the field to preserve and reinstantiate the relations and variables that hold, allowing us to generalize across situations. Abstractions can remain connected to intuition, hold structure across varying context, and connect in novel ways if we so choose to practice.

Practice therefore is not merely a means to an end, like becoming competent. It is how the field participates in its own construction and perception. Every repetition gives weight to a way of seeing and acting, making some configurations easier to reach while making others difficult to even see. Thus, practice is never entirely neutral as it is training us to have new dispositions that influence our consequences onto the world. We can practice towards ethical behavior, compassion, or wisdom. Or we can practice towards harm, contraction, greed, or delusion. It can make either of these categories fluid and easy to use. The important question alongside how we can effectively practice is what we are practicing towards, which is what the section on wisdom is getting at. As we return to wisdom, it's important to consider how we can translate practice into its domain. Sanity gives us the transparency and flexibility to revise the field, so as not to hold current models so rigidly. Wisdom orients that flexibility towards targets worth pursuing. Yet wisdom cannot be described here as a finished model we install and work towards with practice. It takes reorientation and refinement on its path. We must practice acting decisively without contractive defense and not holding gestalts with high ontological authority so as to remain open to feedback without losing direction. If, as Aristotle states, we become builders by building, then we become wise by doing the things that construct wisdom, and thus it becomes an orientation the field holds.
