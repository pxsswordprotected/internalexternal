The Problem of Expression



“There is indeed the inexpressible. This shows itself; it is the mystical.”

— Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 6.522

This one proposition by Wittgenstein shows us the ineffable. Some things, ethics, aesthetics, love, are only fully comprehensible when not put to language. For Wittgenstein, the mystical here is the boundary where language breaks down and cannot express what the one who speaks is attempting to convey. There remains a mystery, a sort of unexplainable frustration that the most important things, the things we dedicate our lives to, cannot be expressed without losing their immense meaning. This problem of the inexpressible remains a frustration we have to deal with. As long as we have to communicate in a finite, compressible manner, we will never be able to share what hangs in our bodies. Fortunately, not all is lost.

Language here is not a complete failure as it allows us to transport seemingly fleeting experiences into symbols that others can understand themselves. However, it fails in the sense that the fleeting experience is never fully captured because to turn it into tokens of interpretable symbols, we must use compression. Compression in this framework means preserving contextually important structure while leaving other characteristics out. As Wittgenstein put it, the inexpressible can only show itself, not tell itself. Lots of experience then is ineffable since a compressed expression that attempts to capture it cannot preserve the full richness of what it proceeds.

Knowledge (which I am defining here as information encoded in a form that can shape perception) shows up in the world through many forms such as a book, a television show, a conversation, or a diagram. But these are all expressions of something deeper. Below the obvious surface of notation are two more layers, each so dense with information that it is puzzling why they stay hidden. The paradox of these layers being hidden resolves in a single mechanism known as compression. Compression of knowledge loses richness but gains portability, stability, and shareability. To externalize deeper layers beyond surface level notation is to compress them, and most of what we know, what we feel, cannot be compressed in totality.

1.1 The Compression Gradient

To externalize deeper layers of knowledge is to compress them. This is the limit Wittgenstein points to when he says there are things we cannot put into words, only show. So the obvious layer of knowledge, like writing, is only the top of a stack that looks like this:

At the bottom of the stack is the embodied layer. This is knowledge that lives in the body below immediate conscious awareness such as gut feelings and tacit knowledge. The embodied layer shapes experience before explicit description through deep, implicit structuring because aspects like physiological baselines sit here in this layer as well. Next in the stack is the representational layer. In this layer sits images, simulations, metaphors, concepts, and models that can be internally inspected or manipulated through mental activity, whether implicit or explicit. Lastly, the layer that sits at the top is the notational layer which holds explicit symbolic forms, such as language, diagrams, equations, and labels, that can be stabilized and shared.

As knowledge travels across these three layers, we need a way of effectively moving through them which is called compression gradience. As we move up the stack of the three layers, we are compressing knowledge into more legible forms which naturally leaves out richness the bottom layers contain. For example, if I have a feeling of connection to what I call God and I attempt to put that connection into words, a massive amount of data will be lost in that process. As we move down the stack, we are in decompression which is reconstructing a richer representation or embodied understanding from a compressed notional expression. To move from the embodied to the notational layer is a sacrifice, an acknowledgment that you will lose out on the vastness and richness of the thing, but also an acknowledgment of practicality. Moving upward generally increases legibility while losing characteristics and moving downward reconstructs richness, but cannot perfectly recover the original state of the notation. This means one belief can traverse the stack while still just being one belief, it is simply expressed differently. 

Importantly, compression and decompression within the stack is bidirectional. An embodied state can give way to a metaphor, while a metaphor can change posture and action (which count as the embodied layer). Similarly, notation like an english sentence can change the modeled metaphor you currently hold and that can change your notational expression. As a whole example, think of this process as moving from the notational layer of writing to the representational layer with models and simulations, then to the embodied layer with tacit knowledge. It should also be said that there is a temporal factor here: compressing is usually quicker than decompressing. Moving from notation to the embodied layer takes longer than the reverse, as you must reconstruct the internal richness from which the externalization originally came.

The representational layer cannot be skipped when understanding is occurring, but it can be bypassed when the system is producing cached outputs. Here we can turn to Lawrence Barsalou, an American psychologist and cognitive scientist, to see why this layer is so important. He argues that comprehension is often scaffolded by simulation; it is the re-enactment of perceptual and motor states. In “Perceptual Symbol Systems,” he rejects the view that “amodal symbols represent knowledge in semantic memory” and says that cognition runs on simulation: “Simulation is the reenactment of perceptual, motor, and introspective states acquired during experience with the world, body, and mind.” This means that if cognition runs on active simulation, an abstraction cannot be understood without first running an inner model of the thing itself. Philip Johnson-Laird arrives at a very similar conclusion regarding how we reason.2 His mental-models theory argues that we do not think by applying static, formal logical rules to language. Instead, we reason by actively constructing and manipulating internal representations whose structure mirrors the real-world structure of the situation we are trying to navigate. The key point here is that the representational layer is most active whenever any type of “attempt to understand” is occurring. It does not have to be activated at all times, such as when moving from the embodied layer to the notational layer by merely expressing a cached phrase without a live internal model. Of course, when expressing an automatic output, such as “Hi, how are you?”, it may activate an internal representation, which means the phrase stops merely being cached and becomes an act of comprehension, but this does not always happen.

To expand on how the representational layer functions, language must be addressed, since it is tempting to assume thought is just words running in the head. This is not entirely true, since internal representations are not necessarily made of words. A wordless model, like an image, can be held in mind, and it can be inspected, run, and understood without a word. Language and thought are separable, meaning a good bit of thinking runs on visual, spatial, and motor representations rather than words or sentences. Language is just one medium among several, and a representation is simply anything that can be run internally, whether an image, a metaphor, a simulation, or words. Language counts as a representation only when it evokes a model that can actually be run. For example, the phrase “invariance under rotation” might unfold into images and simulations for someone who has developed the corresponding knowledge in the representational and embodied layers, while for someone who has not, it remains dead notation with no meaning. While an image carries structure directly, language does not, which makes it more lossy. What language is specifically good at is labeling parts of a representation so they are easier to hold in mind; it parses big structures into smaller handles like “feedback loop”; it makes an intuitive representation explicit and easier to inspect; it shapes attention; and it supports abstraction through compression. Ultimately, the representation is the actual object of understanding, while language becomes a connecting interface.

1.2 The Field

(This section deals a lot with predictive processing. If you are unfamiliar with the theory, it would held to know the basics)

The field of experience is the active, moment-to-moment configuration of our active priors, bodily state, context, attention, targets, and available actions which functions as a deep mobile frame that constantly reconfigures what is salient to the mind-body system. Priors are the stored generative structures that organize experience, and the field is what they manifest as when instantiated in a moment of time. The field’s properties, like variables and constraints, are the active criteria used to test input for fit. By input, I mean both raw sensory experience, such as seeing or hearing, and the things that experience is organized into, like a dog, a cat, or a mathematical object.

The field is always active; we are always using a sliding window through which to experience things, whether implicity or explicitly. At any given moment, the field uses a set of properties, which are downstream of deeper factors like active priors and bodily state, to determine if input fits within its current configuration. The most important properties seem to be constraints, variables, and fit distance. Variables are the parts that can change and alter the prediction of the system, constraints are the binding rules or limits that shape what can fit within it, and fit distance tells you whether input feels closer to or farther from the field’s properties, like a puzzle piece fitting into place. These are not the only three properties active within a field at once; others include actions that feel possible, feeling tone, and targets. Felt tone is valence, meaning the positive, negative, or neutral quality of experience.

A crucial property helping the field is precision weighting. It dictates how much attention or trust is given to specific things over others. This weighting comes from conditioning through past experiences. Like the priors it acts on, precision is shaped by past input and inferred from current context, so the system learns which kinds of signals have been reliable in which contexts and weights them accordingly. This means precision weighting can be conditioned over time (a person trained to treat their environment as dangerous will keep threat signals weighted high by default), but it can also shift rapidly in the moment as context changes. When a threat is detected, the system heavily weights survival variables and constraints and turns down the importance of logical constraints, forcing input to pass a very different test for fit. This rapid reconfiguration of the field is how the system instantly shifts what is salient without needing to build an entirely new one from scratch which is called sensory precision.

Another key job of precision weighting is that it can alter the current relative influence of competing prior preferences whose context-sensitive weighting helps determine the currently prioritized target. Prior preferences are prior probability distributions about which future outcomes the system prefers. In this framework, a target is the currently prioritized preferred outcome of a situation, while policies are possible action sequences selected partly by how well they are expected to reach the target. Simply, targets organize coordinated action by giving more or less weight to certain policy selections across short and long time horizons. For example, if conditioned enough, a survival target can gain control over social or long-term targets, but those other targets can still be “left over” if the survival target is fulfilled, and the left over targets still have the ability to influence things. This precision weighting among prior preferences helps instantiate momentary intention by deciding what gets chosen, when, and with how much weight. This process is called motivational precision. This means our current experience, which contains context (variables, interactions, etc.), changes both what evidence is trusted and which outcome is currently preferred.

For nuance, nested subsystems such as cells, tissue, organs, clusters of priors, and our nervous system can pursue different targets at once which is why target relevance matters. Target relevance here refers to the dominant target or synthesis of targets currently organizing attention and action. Other targets can remain active beneath the relevant target, producing mixed responses when their preferred paths to fulfillment conflict. A conscious target could therefore dominate knowable attention while an embodied protective target constrains what feels possible in its current state. This explains why someone can explicitly want a relationship with someone else while their field still makes avoidance more available than connection. For this framework, when I am speaking of targets I am generally meaning the most relevant to the current context of a situation. 

So to be clear, there exists two type of precision in this framework: ‘sensory precision’ which determines what evidence is trusted, and ‘motivational precision’ which determines what targets most strongly organize perception and action. Typically I like to just use ‘precision’ since both versions are pointing to the process of giving weight to factors within the field. 

In every moment, our “default” field is active and influencing things. This default field is incredibly deep and structures our experience in the most load-bearing ways. It is primarily instantiated and felt through the embodied layer, without needing to become explicit. It is a result of deep priors, which structure abstractions like “thing-ness,” “center reference point of experience,” or “that is a person.” These deep priors make the field feel like reality itself because they shape the way in which we constantly experience things. They do the most work to compress sensory totality, by which I mean theoretical sensory input before perceptual organization. In his streamlined enlightenment protocol, Roger Thisdell proposes that priors act like a reverse house of cards in which our lowest-level ones sit at the bottom, making up the foundation for the things built on top of them. For example, the concept of “thing,” a low-level prior for objecthood or thingness, sits almost at the very bottom level, while the concept of “water bottle” sits much higher, being made up of not just “thing,” but many other priors like “resource for survival,” each of which survive on a hierarchical model. There are also deep bodily priors called homeostatic priors, which are innate and evolutionarily ingrained and provide information about the body’s internal physiological state, such as temperature. These homeostatic priors allow us to continuously generate predictions that anticipate bodily needs and keep us within a survivable range through bodily functions and actions. Because they track survival directly, the system gives them very high precision, so they carry strong top-down authority and can be difficult to override. These priors give the system a physiological floor from which it makes predictions to ensure we do not bottom out at a biological level. Built on top of these priors is what researchers Micah Allen and Manos Tsakiris call the “first prior” which is a stable sense of self-awareness grounded in the body’s regulation. As shown, the default field is like the reference against which everything else is compared or on top of which it is built, so it cannot be bypassed easily. In a way, the default lives in the embodied layer to “act out” its predictions and reduce the error between what is happening and what it predicts will happen. This is active inference, a process in which the system takes action to make input match its model. Incoming sensory experience is structured by default priors, and behavior expresses or fulfills predictions shaped by them. The default field is deeply conditioned, while additional field properties are situationally activated by context, such as representations, and are generally recognizable as an explicit lens. The transition from the default field to something “extra” is like a gradient, not a binary either-or phase change. Repetition makes the extra properties imported from situations and objects sink deeper into the embodied layer, into the field. Additional field properties that are activated and used long enough may eventually become the new default way of seeing things, hence why experts pick up on small things so quickly. The expert has default load-bearing priors running in the embodied layer as a way of perceiving and acting.

I say the field constantly reconfigures based on moment-to-moment experience because ever-changing sensory input is always activating different priors. If I am talking to a friend one moment, then switch to using my phone in the middle of the conversation, the context and object of the phone bring new properties into the field. Talking to a friend activates priors like “I need to listen,” “I need to sit a certain way,” “conversation moves like this,” and “my face and tone matter,” while using the phone activates different priors like “I look down,” “my fingers move like this,” “awareness collapses,” and “my posture changes.” Action and the nervous system then come to matter a great deal, since the field shapes the body for what it prepares to do. Changing input has the ability to affect posture, muscle tension, awareness, attention, breathing, and what actions can be taken. This shows us that the field itself is embodied, as it is not just a way of structuring input but also a way of preparing, responding, moving, and regulating. Most of this stays implicit and low-level. 

Another way to picture what the field is doing is through prediction trees, or “a probability distribution over competing hypotheses and possible future trajectories.” A prediction tree is the space of potential interpretations of input and the branches the field could resolve into, weighted by precision, which also come with their own action policies modulated by targets. Conscious perception usually settles on one dominant branch in moment-to-moment experience, while competing branches remain represented with lower probability and can still influence inference. Under active inference, one branch gaining enough posterior probability, meaning it becomes the most likely interpretation after taking current evidence into account, increases confidence in its associated action sequences. A dominant branch therefore organizes how the input is interpreted while increasing the weight of the action policies associated with that predicted state. Simply, the system selects actions expected to move it toward its preferred state. Through active inference, it minimizes prediction error, the gap between expected input and actual input, either by acting to make the world match the prediction or by updating its model. At the same time, the system’s actions can reduce target error, a term specific to this framework for the gap between the system’s current and preferred state. Prediction error and target error often interact, but they describe different discrepancies. Because the system cannot effectively explore every branch, it must narrow the tree through pruning methods like compression. Compression groups many lower-level prior structures into a single higher-level unit, such as “water bottle,” allowing the system to treat the package as one thing rather than evaluating each part separately. In doing so, a good deal of the lower-level richness is left out, but the search space shrinks and attention is freed for other input. 

Prediction trees and the reverse house of cards therefore connect in what we can call the load-bearing tree, which is the dependency structure that makes the field’s current configuration possible. The house of cards is the vertical structure, with deep priors at the bottom holding up higher-level ones, while the branches represent competing predicted states made possible by that foundation. The perception that an object is a “water bottle” can only become a branch because “thing” is part of the foundation at the bottom. There exists the trunk of the load-bearing tree which is not something you can simply choose from; it is the foundational set of priors that makes almost any branching or action possible at all. Branching happens mostly at the higher levels, where multiple interpretations are in competition, such as whether a shape is a water bottle or a thermos, while the lower-level prior that renders both as “a thing” remains relatively stable. It makes sense, then, that changing a high-level branch is much easier than changing something near the bottom. Altering a deep foundational prior would disrupt much of what is built on top of it. Deep priors are load-bearing and slow to update for good reason, since changing them can present a massive threat to the system as a whole.

A short list of deep/foundational priors that make up the trunk of the load-bearing includes things like causality, time, tribal belonging, anthropomorphism, status and hierarchy, reputation, conformity, threat bias, disgust, body ownership, and a continuous self, which are all made up of even more low-level priors like thing-ness and agency. All of these are not necessarily at the same levels, and some may sit more downstream than others so they can be modulated more, but they are still foundational in the way the average human structures experience.

An internal representation, such as an image or metaphor, is not itself a field. It is an object we “place onto” the field that has its own implicit or explicit properties, and in doing so, it reshapes the landscape of our current field’s properties. Take the metaphor “argument is war.” This metaphor introduces new high-level branches and reweights existing ones, changing which interpretation becomes dominant and, through that shift, altering the field’s active properties towards war-like things, such as attacking and defending. These updated variables and constraints were not alive before the moment the “argument is war” metaphor became active; they only appeared once we took that metaphor as a way to look at things. In terms of the load-bearing tree, a representation mostly affects the upper branches rather than the trunk because it reweights high-level interpretations through precision, which is why it can restructure how you interpret input in a moment without changing the deep priors underneath. Because of this, representations are typically cheap and fast to apply and remove, since they are not changing foundational structures. It’s important to note that a representation begins as a test case against the current field, and when it fits the field’s properties, it is integrated and reshapes the field. Over time, repeated use of a representation can train its structure into deeper parts of the load-bearing tree, reshaping the parts of the tree built on top of them.

For instance, if I am on the roof of a building and have intrusive thoughts of falling off, the image of myself falling matches the spatial awareness of being at a high elevation (variable), the physical laws of gravity (constraint), and the survival prior of “threat detection.” Crucially, a representation that fits your current field does not have to produce a positive feeling tone, as in the intrusive-thought example. This is because two properties of the field can come apart: valence and target. In the falling-off-the-roof example, the currently prioritized preferred outcome, the target, might be something like “survival by not falling off,” so representations that are coherent with these threat-related properties of the field will fit. However, the representation of falling off the building still produces negative valence because it is a signal that influences action selection to satisfy the survival-related properties of the field (ie generated unpleasant images of falling off the building causes you to avoid such a thing); this is active inference doing its thing. If the image were pleasant, we would be more motivated to fall off, but since it feels very bad, there is less incentive to give high precision to branches that lead to violation of survival.

Interestingly, representations do not only model present reality or intellectual knowledge, they can also preserve or simulate the properties of a target, a preferred state, that the current environment has made unavailable through processes like suppression. This is related to a function of fantasies in which they keep a preferred state active internally when the system does not believe that state can be realized externally due to conflicting reasons. By comparing several fantasies, or representations, the system can detect which properties remain stable across them, such as safety, agency, belonging, or adventure. Learning then consists of separating those deeper properties from the original form, like a fantasy, and finding new representations or actions that preserve the same properties. This is called a structure-preserving transformation. For example, a fantasy of living on another planet may contain targets like freedom, exploration, belonging, and wonder, which can then be separated from the fantasy setting and expressed through new actions or environments in ordinary life.

Phenomenologically, the field is like a thing we see through that can never itself be seen. For instance, to look at things with a representation active is to deploy a kind of map in front of us, like putting on goggles that highlight some things and dim others in the latent space of knowing. Often, it is so implicit that we cannot get behind its curtain to watch how it applies structure to experience. As a distinction, raw experience, or sensory totality, is a theoretical, chaotic, high-entropy landscape that our systems never meet directly. Deep, low-level priors filter and reduce this entropy into the embodied field which will be explained later on through the mechanism of contraction. While the embodied layer is rich and feels like unfiltered reality, it is always structured when we perceive it. The field’s job is to select what is and is not salient, leaving us with a workable set of options. Some might recall Aldous Huxley’s ‘reducing valve’ mechanism: “To make biological survival possible, Mind at Large has to be funnelled through the reducing valve of the brain and nervous system. What comes out at the other end is a measly trickle of the kind of consciousness which will help us to stay alive on the surface of this particular planet. To formulate and express the contents of this reduced awareness, man has invented and endlessly elaborated those symbol-systems and implicit philosophies which we call languages.” In other words, we must compress as we move up the three layers of the compression gradient because the richness of the bottom layers is not something we can act on directly. The field compresses sensory totality into a workable structure for us to live within, and as we move through the world, the field changes. Even when the embodied layer is perceived as a vast and rich landscape, that perception is itself a theoretical limited model.

Importantly, sensory totality itself is still a model, something that cannot be experienced directly so any mention of it is theoretical. However, there is substantial evidence to support the claim that, even though we cannot experience this totality, overwhelming, uncompressed sensory input outside of normal structure can still occur. The first being the entropic brain hypothesis by Carhart-Harris & Friston. Their REBUS paper says psychedelics act “via their entropic effect on spontaneous cortical activity” and “relax the precision of high-level priors or beliefs, thereby liberating bottom-up information flow.” This shows psychedelics increase neural entropy and reduce top-down constraint from the default mode network which predicts flooding, dissolvement of boundaries, and overwhelm. Interestingly, some Jhana and 5-MeO-DMT appear to increase brain entropy while producing less complex experience, like whiteouts or cessation, which adds more nuance to a simple “more entropy = more flooding” reading of sensory totality. Secondly, we can look at filtering pathologies like schizophrenia: “Sensory gating refers to the central nervous system’s ability to filter sensory inputs,” and poor gating may cause “sensory inundation and ‘flooding.’” We can also look at migraines as another example: migraines show “altered sensory processing” with “hypersensitivity to visual stimuli” and “a lack of habituation.” Third, we can look at the neuroscience of gating in general: “The TRN [thalamic reticular nucleus] gates the relay of sensory information to cortex,” and high gain can “disconnect” the relay from cortex. Information theoretic estimates put human behavioral throughput at about “10 bits/s” against sensory inflow of “no less than 1 gigabits/s.” And lastly, meditation progress seemingly points to the phenomenology of this reduction in compressing sensory totality as Laukkonen & Slagter points out that meditation acts as something like predictive deconstruction: “deconstructive meditation brings one closer to the here and now by disengaging anticipatory processes.” So even though sensory totality in itself as whole cannot be directly pointed to, there is evidence our systems are doing some type of filtering of rich entropy which leads to moment-to-moment experience. 

The mechanism of attention should be addressed. Attention is a process of that directions precision across the field, which includes the load-bearing tree, whether done automatic or manually. It is the control mechanism that determines which properties are weighted more or less in the moment. Attention has three core roles in this framework: moment-to-moment weighting, dwelling on layers, and sculpting.

Moment-to-moment weighting determines which properties and parts of experience count as being salient, like placing attention on bodily signals rather than signals from other people. Dwelling on layers is the act of resting attention on a specific layer in the stack, which can result in downstream retuning of the field when the proper mechanisms are in place, such as safety and time. For example, staying with a sensation at the embodied layer long enough retunes the field to the sensation’s properties. This is a part of how “aha” moments happen and why background compression and decompression occur. Lastly, there is sculpting. Whatever receives repeated attention gets reinforced and eventually sinks into the trunk of the load-bearing tree, which is why years of practice develop an expert’s deep tacit knowledge which is a part of the embodied layer.

It’s important to note that there is a map of different ways the field can be instantiated through richness. On one level of the map, we can have an explicit “lens” in which we view experience through, on another level there is amodal perception which is the field affecting things so implicitly that it is hard to even recognize. An example of this would be the prediction that the back of your hand exists if you are looking at it from the front, or that when you turn a corner on the street you are walking on you will see a different side of a building you’re looking at. Awareness (which is less “obvious” than attention) can hold a wider but already structured configuration, while more amodal or abstract cognition compresses experience into highly integrated representations that are easier to manipulate but carry less of the original sensory richness. The present moment is therefore not instantiated in one fixed form but rather, the system can move between diffuse embodied richness and highly compressed abstract structure depending on its targets, precision weighting, bodily state, and context.

Because deep priors are load-bearing and often tied to survival, the system can treat threats to them as a crisis. However, safety alone does not seem sufficient for updating them. A better requirement is sufficient slack, meaning the system has enough regulatory, cognitive, temporal, social, and representational capacity to encounter prediction error without immediately resolving it through defensive active inference. Safety contributes to this slack by reducing immediate threat, but controllability, bodily resources, social support, available attention, time, and the presence of a viable alternative model also matter. Research suggests that learning often occurs within a kind of Goldilocks zone in which a mismatch must be large enough to destabilize the existing model, but not so complex that the system becomes overwhelmed and falls back on rigid policies.

In terms of this framework, slack allows the system to keep both old priors and the contradicting input active at the same time without prematurely pruning one of them. The slack provides enough room to delay habitual action, tolerate the richness that is lost when compressing, search alternative branches, and allow a new representation to form. Controllability matters because a system that expects its actions to have no effect has less reason to explore new policies, while social support can share some of the regulatory burden required to remain in contact with destabilizing material. Without sufficient slack, the system is more likely to increase the precision of its existing model and return to its predicted state. With sufficient slack, it can leave the prediction error unresolved long enough for the deeper structure to reorganize. This seems like a core underlying driver of deliberate practice, which “consists of individualized training activities specially chosen to improve specific aspects of a student’s performance through repetition and successive refinement. It’s mindful repetition on performance tasks just beyond the edge of one’s capabilities” according to Justin Skycak. 

Deep change, then, becomes most likely when there is enough destabilization to loosen the relevant parts of the load-bearing tree, enough stability to avoid complete overwhelm, and enough time and alternative structure for a new field to become embodied.

1.3 Chunking, Gestalts, and Attractors

Chunking can be seen as a way we save energy by compressing and encoding information so that its structure is preserved while its full richness only needs to be revealed when necessary. It is the process by which parts that once had to be integrated separately get compressed into a single unit that the field then treats as one thing. The process of chunking forms chunks which are learned, reusable compressed units that reduce future processing or search cost. The mechanism of precision helps here in which we can prune many branches of the load-bearing tree into one usable unit, which shrinks the search space of interpretations and frees attention for other input. The current experiential result we perceive is a gestalt: the unified whole that appears in the present moment when priors are organized together and instantiated. A novel image can suddenly become a gestalt even though you have never seen it before which requires existing priors. 

If you encounter and use that organization of the novel image repeatedly, the system may store its relational structure as a chunk. This results in feedback loops of repetition, stable relational structure, and successful action that confirms our predictions.

To put this in terms of the load-bearing tree, lower-level priors predict separate characteristics and relational dynamics, and when input provides evidence for several of them to activate at the same time, such as shape, expected use, interactions, and weight, chunking compresses those priors into a higher-level one that can be used as one unit. When that higher-level prior successfully organizes current input, meaning the underlying lower-level priors make sense together, the field experiences the result as a gestalt. For example, if we take a water bottle, the high-level prior of “water bottle” is a gestalt formed from lower level priors that are chunked like shape and usage that interact together successfully, 

Repetition builds the priors that let a bundle of parts resolve cheaply as a single gestalt, which is why mathematicians can parse a problem and have several pieces of knowledge quickly come online. Experts do not simply perceive richer chunks; they possess better-calibrated chunks and greater flexibility in moving between the whole and the details it leaves out when needed. Chunking also occurs across time, not just between the parts of an object in a single moment. The sense of a stable “now” is the field binding a stream of changing sensory input into one continuous gestalt, the same compression that pulls spatial parts into one thing applied to temporal change. This is what it means for deep priors to be re-instantiated moment to moment: a prior is not literally a static object sitting in storage, but a learned pattern that must be continuously reproduced to remain active. The trunk of the load-bearing tree feels permanent because it is regenerated so reliably and quickly that it appears smooth and independent, while actually depending on the conditions reproducing it. This also means the trunk can change, since a truly permanent foundation could never shift. Practices like sensory clarity can de-chunk temporal gestalts, like explicit slices of time, allowing experience to appear as changing sensations and events rather than one continuous gestalt. Because chunks make experience workable, the goal is not to eliminate them but to recognize what characteristics they omit and regain access to their components when the current gestalt stops serving the context.

Repetition of field configurations and their reinforcing loops produces attractors. These are configurations the system repeatedly settles into because its components reinforce one another, whether or not the system consciously prefers it. The load-bearing tree explains which structures support a given field configuration, while attractors explain why that configuration persists against competing options. When the same chunks repeatedly activate together, the field begins treating their relations as one even larger gestalt, so activating one part helps reinstate the rest. As this gestalt becomes easier to reproduce than competing configurations, it gains stability and increasingly pulls the field back toward itself. For example, an anxiety attractor might contain bodily arousal, threat-focused attention, catastrophic interpretation, and less precision on corrective evidence. This differs from targets because the person’s current target may actually be safety or calm, but the policies used to reach safety can reinforce the anxiety attractor. An attractor is also not one singular prior. It is a recurrently stabilized field configuration produced by interacting priors, precision weights, bodily states, attention patterns, environmental cues, and policies.

Attractors then have their own attractor basins which are ranges of states from which the system still returns to the same configuration. A person does not have to be fully anxious to remain inside an “anxiety basin” because if certain co-arising conditions come alive in relation to each other, like bodily arousal and threat signals from environment, they can pull the rest of the field back online. Basins can differ in both width and depth. A wide basin can capture the field configuration from many different starting states, while a deep basin strongly resists disruption once held. Repetition, bodily reinforcement, environmental regularity, and enactment of associated policies can widen or deepen a basin, while corrective evidence, new chunks, alternative policies, and increased slack can make competing configurations more available for usage. The boundary between basins acts like a threshold because gradual changes may accumulate without an obvious shift until the current pattern stops reinforcing itself strongly enough and another pattern takes over. Since active field configurations and mind-body states select for biased evidence and action to reduce target and prediction error, entry into and exit from an attractor may also require different conditions like environment. A small threat signal may trigger an anxiety attractor, while leaving it may require strong safety signals which can be much harder to perceive and maintain than the conditions that led to the attractor itself. This asymmetry of entry and exit is a form of path dependence called hysteresis.

Field configurations also come with a property of reachability. This describes whether a viable path to the attractor exists under the field’s current available actions. Two attractors could have the same width and depth while differing in reachability because one is separated from the current state by lacking sufficient actionable moves from where the system is currently at. Reachability is load-bearing for configurations because changing it means changing what actions are available by the system to move from one state to another. If a property, like felt tone, of a field makes it so the reachability of a state is difficult to climb to, the property is load-bearing for that configuration. This becomes especially important when considering psychological changes in which barriers block different pathways that would usually be open if not for load-bearing properties that have been conditioned which result in persistent attractors. 

When many possible trajectories depend on one such constraint, reachability becomes load-bearing, since changing that constraint reorganizes what the field treats as possible.

Field configurations also differ in what they make reachable: basin depth describes how difficult an attractor is to leave and basin width the range of states it captures, while reachability concerns whether the current constraints and available actions permit a viable path to it at all. When many possible trajectories depend on one such constraint, reachability becomes load-bearing, since changing that constraint reorganizes what the field treats as possible.

Because our perception and actions depends on the priors and chunks currently available, it is impossible to fully instantiate and realize a gestalt that we do not have the prerequisites for. We can imagine an approximation, a proxy gestalt, using existing structural dependencies, but the actual organization can only become available after new priors have been established and are available for precision. More on the importance of this later. 

Lastly, it is important touch on the idea of memory as a construct and what is going on there. Priors in the load-bearing tree are something like persistent dispositions, which are real structural changes left by past experience and conditioning. However, there is a core difference in what actually “shows up” moment-to-moment as memory instead of it just being priors interpreting what’s going on. It is more like the field constructing a gestalt in the present moment, using what resources are available and what the person-environment context gives you. This means access of memory can vary independently of what is being recalled or retained, since context can influence the field’s ability to hold certain things and structure. So a disposition can be present and shaping behavior and representations while remaining unavailable to explicitly access. This is why context like mood and state change what can be reached in the moment by the mind-body system. Episodic recall is therefore the same construction process as all other gestalts using past experience. The sense that a thing is a categorically different type of thing called a memory, rather than an internal representation, is itself a gestalt formed from features like temporality and familiarity rather than a separate, unrelated thing. This is why a memory can can be the cause of reactivity that would occur as if a thing were happening, while being wrong, and why rumination gives way to attractor states rather than describing one. As Michael Levin frames it, because our mind-body-environment system changes over time with context and targets, a trace is closer to a message that has to be remapped to the current system rather than a solid, unchanging thing, which internal representations are not. 

2. The Dynamics of Fit

This section explains why the architecture moves, what felt tone reports, and how bodily states stabilize it.

2.1 The Energetic Triad

This framework’s ontology rests heavily on how the mind-body system manages metabolic and computational cost. I use “cost” here in a relative sense, not as a measure of calories burned. At any moment, the brain has limited processing capacity and attention to go around, and different possible interpretations of what you’re perceiving compete for resources. “Cost” means how much of that limited capacity an input demands and how much additional capacity is used to process it.

Our system runs on a finite budget of processing capacity and spends much of it maintaining a stable, workable interpretation of ongoing input. For every input, the system estimates how much processing it will require and then registers how much capacity it actually demands. This is always happening in experience because sensory input is constant. Here, processing refers broadly to the resources used to handle an input, while integrating it means attempting to “place it into” the field, a process that subsequently produces a felt sense if deviations are consciously noticeable.

One main source of an input’s obvious feeling tone is the system reporting the difference between the estimated cost and the registered cost of processing it. Put simply, this is integration-cost prediction error pertaining to the predicted versus actual integration cost of an input. Successful compression here can reduce the cost of integrating an input by allowing the field to organize previously separate, unchunked elements into a single workable structure.

Some practical proxies for whether an input is high cost to integrate are time (rereading a passage), effort (using “extra focus”), and bodily tension (like a furrowed brow). For an input that is low cost to integrate, some proxies include immediacy (clicks fast), freed attention (gained capacity for more input), and tension release (relaxation).

However, cost difference is not the only source of feeling tone; a second source is an input’s relation to the current field’s target. When facing threatening input, say if you saw a lion, the field’s most heavily weighted target would be something like “survival at all costs.” The input can integrate cleanly and quickly, as when you immediately recognize the lion, but because the situation threatens the heavily weighted target of being in a state of remaining alive, the felt tone is negative. These two sources of feeling tone can come apart at the edges, which is why an intrusive thought, like being eaten by the lion, can integrate easily but still feel bad. It should also be noted that these two sources can contribute simultaneously to the felt tone of an experience and with different strengths. For example, if the lion is hidden in some grass and making noise, the input may both cost more to integrate than expected when trying to disambiguate hearing from seeing, and threaten the target of remaining alive, causing the two sources to reinforce each other.

Implicitly, to “remove” the target-relation contribution to valence, we can remove a second-order rigid action policy on experience like “this thing must be different.” The integration cost of input will wind up staying the same, so a sense of negative feeling tone can drop to its minimum integration cost without going further. Here, there is no analogous move on the other side since a positive difference is just an actual cost that fell below prediction, there is no second-order thing to be removed. The negative different has two contributors here and one is “removable.”

This pattern of a baseline with positive and negative spikes shows up across vast situations in experience. The baseline is where integration cost roughly matches the estimate, while spikes in either direction occur when processing demand is unexpectedly greater or lower than predicted. Because sensory input is constant and most inputs integrate near their estimated cost, these baseline-level integrations result in a cumulative embodied background tone of fit. This baseline is what the spikes are compared against, which is why they are noticeable. Note that this low-level baseline is not the same as a positive difference, since the positive difference is a noticeable deviation. You can see this three-level pattern across aesthetic experiences, a math proof, a painting, or sensations in the body, since part of an input’s feeling tone comes from the system registering these deviations.

For the three levels, we can categorize them as positive, baseline, and negative, with positive and negative representing differences in opposing directions. Baseline is where registered cost roughly matches predicted cost, with the trunk of the load-bearing tree structuring incoming input without major surprises in either direction. A negative difference occurs when an input costs more to integrate than predicted, often because it cannot be compressed into a single gestalt, so the system spends extra resources parsing it. A fully predictable input that is also predicted to be cheap to integrate, like elevator music, produces little or no difference and sinks into the baseline as mundane rather than producing an obvious positive difference. A positive difference occurs when an input expected to be costly, perhaps because of its perceived richness or complexity, instead fits neatly into the active field as a valid gestalt and integrates more cheaply than predicted, requiring fewer processing resources than expected. In fast cases, this can happen within fractions of a second, though slower forms exist, such as a math proof that unfolds over minutes.

This helps explain why internal representations matter so much. Representations can retune the field’s properties at a given moment, lowering the cost of integrating an input by making fit more substantial. This is why an already well-modeled input can integrate cheaply, while a poorly modeled one produces an obvious negative difference. A poor model will have you attempting to integrate the input, like a question, but there will be lots of struggle because of increased processing cost. A good model lowers the integration cost of inputs relative to encountering them without it, and it does so across many input types. For instance, a math problem is much easier to parse when the necessary prerequisites are already in place, making a model with those prerequisites better. Remember, the claim is that a good model lowers the cost of integrating future and novel inputs, not that low cost in the present necessarily means learning is happening, since our models need to integrate a range of varied and conflicting inputs. A flat-earther can update their model by watching YouTube videos and easily integrate a great deal of input, but only within a restricted subset, such as an echo chamber. The key is that good representations continue reducing integration costs for inputs you cannot plan for and across long time horizons.

This triad connects back to the load-bearing tree in important ways. The size of any spike compared to the baseline depends on where in the tree the affected priors sit. A branch that sits high in the tree (i.e., not dealing with foundational priors) is cheap to change and hardly registers as an event, while violations that occur deeper in the tree affect load-bearing priors and force changes to everything else built on top of them, resulting in a much larger and more obvious felt tone difference. Hence why some surprises or changes in experience feel trivial while others feel like deep fragmentation: the same core mechanism occurs, but the depth and magnitude of the priors being affected differ greatly in cost. Because the tree rests on deep priors, updating them is incredibly expensive for our system to integrate, so the negative felt tone produced when deep priors are violated reflects the system accounting for that cost. This is why shame can hurt so badly, since it affects deep priors dealing with the sense of self and security, such as “I am bad,” which produces many discrepancies in the integration of input, even if low-level. The foundational trunk of the load-bearing tree is therefore slow to update because, according to the triad, such integration is extremely costly, while branches that sit higher are much cheaper to change. But keep in mind that higher branches are where most updating happens (which can have cascading effects downward), whereas deep priors, which are active in nearly every moment and rarely violated, contribute to much of our ongoing feeling tone, mostly through steady baseline integration with occasional obvious spikes of felt tone.

It’s important to mention the temporal variation that comes with these functions. This section so far has focused on one-shot differences tracking fast integration time slices, but what about the way we track feeling tone across longer slices of time? The main measurement we can use here is compression progress, the rate at which integration cost is reduced relative to a target. We can model this in three broad ways: too easy, productive struggle, and too hard. Of course, these slopes do not always appear in such simple forms; they are just practical proxies for measuring feeling tone over time. The “too easy” signal tracks experiences over slices of time in which there is little integration cost left to reduce, which typically results in feelings like boredom. The “productive struggle” signal tracks experiences in which integration cost decreases over time with input that starts as costly. If cost falls steadily, this can result in feelings like enjoyment or flow. Interesingly, there seems to be a “Goldilocks Effect” in cognitive science and learning which people disengage from sequences that are either too predictable or too surprising. The “Eighty Five Percent Rule” rule demonstrates something very similar in which “there is a sweet spot in which training is neither too easy nor too hard, and where learning progresses most quickly.” Lastly, the “too hard” signal tracks experiences in which integration cost does not decrease over time or even increases, which commonly results in feelings like frustration or overwhelm.

It is also possible to extrapolate this cost-slop function into a a temporal property of phenomenology, meaning the rate at which an ongoing experience becomes more structured relative to what the system is trying to do. For instance, “too easy” means the field is producing dominant, low-cost gestalts, so experience feels mundane. “Productive unfolding” means uncertainty remains, but the field is gradually finding structure, producing curiosity, absorption, insight, aesthetic pleasure, or flow. “Too hard” means that no workable structure emerges in experience, so unresolved input accumulates into tension and overwhelm. This is not a complete theory of feeling tone but  one proposed contributor to phenomenological experience. 

Crucially, compression progress is always relative to a target, like finishing a problem set today or becoming a mathematician over years, which operate across different time slices. An activity is not by itself too easy or too hard; it is evaluated relative to what the system is currently trying to accomplish through its heavily weighted targets. So the same activity, such as solving a problem set, can show different rates of progress relative to each target. For example, solving a problem set in a math textbook may feel like little immediate progress while still feeling meaningful based on the long-term target of becoming a mathematician. Likewise, a more difficult problem may feel locally productive while seeming irrelevant to a larger target, like learning side branches in math that aren’t necessary for becoming a mathematician. Several targets can remain active at once, and precision weighting determines which of their progress signals contributes most strongly to the feeling tone of the moment. When these targets align, short-term work can inherit meaning from longer-term targets. When they conflict, the same activity can produce mixed feelings. Felt tone in any moment is therefore shaped by compression-rate signals across several nested targets, such as short-term targets within long-term ones, with deeper, more heavily precision-weighted targets carrying greater weight and tending to dominate feeling tone. In this way, felt tone of experience over time is a signal of whether integration cost is falling as quickly as the field predicts relative to its active targets.

This model is not meant to claim that the brain literally reports exact metabolic cost in experience. It is more like a synthesis of predictive processing, interoception, allostasis, processing fluency, and aesthetic prediction error. The Free Energy Principle models perception and action as processes organized around reducing uncertainty or prediction error. Active inference and allostatic models then help inform this synthesis through bodily regulation, where we predict and manage our physiological needs rather than reacting after the fact. Dealing with aesthetics, processing fluency theory says that “easier” processing tends to produce more positive aesthetic response, and predictive models of art say that pleasure often comes from the reintroduction of order after prediction has been disrupted due to complex input. All this supports the fact that felt tone of experience is a result of the difference between expected cost and actual integration cost.

Lastly, I want to touch on the importance of biological/physiological factors at play here. A big part of the baseline of estimating and integrating costs of input is not fixed but is set by the body’s overall state, which is where physiology comes to matter. The felt tone of this triad is modulated by allostasis, which is the body’s proactive regulation of its own resources: “Efficient regulation [of the body] requires anticipating needs and preparing to satisfy them before they arise.” Other modulators at play include the state of the nervous system, stress hormones like cortisol, and inflammation, which are all processed into the field through interoception, which is “the process by which the nervous system senses, interprets, and integrates signals originating from within the body, providing a moment-by-moment mapping of the body’s internal landscape across conscious and unconscious levels.” Another well-supported finding is that inflammation can produce sickness-like behavior such as fatigue, minimal activity, social withdrawal, and a depressed mood. Relatedly, heavy stress can bias perception and attention toward potential threat, which causes feedback loops of threat-detection. This means a drained or threatened body with little slack has lower capacity to handle complexity and interprets more inputs as costly to integrate before any specific input is received. 

What is being described here is not the cost-integration baseline that positive or negative spikes are compared against, which lies wherever the system is currently at, but the background felt tone the whole system uses. What modulates that background is how much capacity is already uses before input is integrated, which includes capacity spent holding contraction and running second-order targets on experience. By releasing held contraction that is not globally useful and lowering the precision on second-order targets, we can gain capacity and raise the background felt tone, the way chronic stress and bad health lowers it. Put simply, baseline mood (which is a force multiplier) partly reflects the brain’s expectations about bodily state and available resources.

Importantly, these expectations can include long horizon targets, not just short-term bodily needs. A future target is held in the current field as an expectation that present actions are moving the system toward it, and the body can regulate differently depending on whether that target feels achieved, achievable, or threatened. This helps explain why someone can feel a sense of deep okay-ness while doing difficult work if the system trusts the current actions to reach the long-term target, or remain tense when feeling pleasure if a deeper target still feels unachieved. In this sense, feeling tone is partly about whether the system models its highly weighted targets as sufficiently handled, not just pleasure. Handled here can mean either changing the world to reach the target, changing the action(s) that are supposed to reach the target, or simply changing the target itself. To be clear, the general claim that bodily state sets a background influence on affect is well supported, but the more specific predictive-processing mappings (like tying neuromodulators like dopamine or serotonin to specific prediction roles) are debated, which I treat more loosely but still consider meaningful. To be clear about the three layer stack of thecompression gradienr, all of these things, the physiological factors, are a part of the embodied layer, not something separate.

Luckily, we have agency over a portion of this physiological aspect of the baseline since it is not exactly fixed. The things that are so far best studied to help are exercise, sleep, morning light, slow breathing, humming, and dancing, but I suspect the literature is far behind what is possible by an individual, so I would recommend not constraining yourself to what the current “science says.” For instance, much of the literature on meditation pertains to novices or relatively inexperienced practitioners, and advanced practitioners/states are understudied, which is a limitation when trying to understand the upper limits of human agency in this area. If you were to experiment, finding ways to reduce background processing demand or increase the system’s available capacity, whether by releasing held contraction (more on this below), loosening rigid priors, or supporting the body so it has greater physiological and psychological capacity, would be a helpful guiding principle.

2.2 Embodiment and Somatic Stabilization

I want to expand on the importance of the body in this framework and explain in more detail how it connects to the load-bearing tree, the Energetic Triad, and the three layers by showing how the field is physically held and enacted in the body.

First, a definition: a haptic contraction is a felt tightening or clenching in bodily sensation, whether muscular, visceral, or vascular. It is the somatic form that a defended or violated prediction arising from prior structures takes in direct experience. Its counterpart, expansion, is simply the release of this kind of contraction. In this sense, contraction refers more specifically to tension that participates in maintaining or responding to an active field configuration. It is unclear if ALL bodily tension can be mapped to some type of priors-relationship. 

(One possible mechanistic account is Michael Edward Johnson’s “vasocomputation” hypothesis, which proposes that active-inference predictions may be physically instantiated through vascular tension. In this account, a contraction first collapses an ambiguous neural pattern into a more definite one, holds it as a form of medium-term memory, and, if sustained, latches into a durable prior that resists updating until the prediction resolves or the latch is released. Through this frame, taṇhā, or craving, is this compression-clench, which is why craving and the somatic contraction that holds it can be understood as the same process expressed through different channels.)

These haptic contractions may match the depth of the priors being held, meaning that deeper priors produce stronger, more persistent, and “physically deeper” contractions. For example, a deep, load-bearing prior held as tension is trunk-level, which is one reason chronic contractions can be so hard to release: releasing them means changing a foundational structure that experience relies on. Tacit knowledge becomes an obvious example here. Deeply conditioned priors may be partly expressed and stabilized through recurring haptic contraction patterns so they can be enacted. These patterns do not instantiate only single priors, but instead help instantiate whole field configurations, each of which may come with a characteristic pattern of haptic contraction and release that supports its properties like targets and constraints. These patterns may also keep the targets associated with those priors active after the original situation has passed so they can be resolved. By holding unresolved prediction or target error in the embodied layer, they keep attention and action weighted toward signals that might reduce the discrepancy or satisfy the target. In this sense, haptic contraction can function like an embodied bookmark, keeping later perception and action organized around an unfinished target so the error can be reduced. Importantly, a bidirectional feedback loop is running here. Active priors can generate contraction, while the resulting interoceptive signals provide new evidence that the predicted state is real and meaningful. This develops a feedback loop in which the body helps maintain the field that originally organized the body. Episodic recall here is doing something similar in which reinstating the configuration dealing with the memory, the internal representations, reinstates its somatic pattern, since this is how the configuration is enacted. 



If we take a look at a mathematician in the middle of solving a problem, their tacit knowledge, embodied through conditioned patterns of haptic contraction, is activated by the current problem and target to form a specific field configuration that comes with its own unique properties. Each new representation, such as a mental image, is then tested against the embodied felt sense of that field, with a negative difference in felt tone signaling that it does not fit and a positive difference signaling fit.

(Again I turn to the vasocomputation hypothesis as well as this tweet by Michael Johnson himself that proposes something very similar, which I did not see until after I put together the above paragraph. He describes these recurring patterns of muscular and vascular tension as “stances,” which closely parallels the claim here that field configurations have characteristic somatic patterns that stabilize predictions and make certain actions available.)

Noticeable bodily contraction is the somatic expression of a negative difference in felt tone because it arises when the system registers input that conflicts with a held prior or attempts to prevent that prior from being updated. The magnitude and persistence of the contraction may scale with how deep and load-bearing the prior is. Taken together, these three accounts describe the same process from different sides: the load-bearing tree gives the structure its depth, the Energetic Triad explains the magnitude of the resulting spikes, and haptic contraction provides a physical substrate through which the structure is stabilized and enacted. Depth in the tree predicts how costly a prior is to update and how firmly its associated tension is latched. This means the meditation/contemplative work of releasing contraction and the epistemic work of updating knowledge may be closely related mechanistically, since both loosen the precision and embodied reinforcement that keep a load-bearing prior stable, making the underlying model more available for updating.

As stated earlier, single releases of contraction usually affect higher branches of the load-bearing tree, but repeated releases can gradually propagate downward as the field learns that the contraction is no longer necessary. With greater sensory clarity and regulatory slack, meditation allows the system to remain with prediction error without immediately restoring the old contraction through active inference. Over time, this can reduce the precision and embodied reinforcement of deeper priors, making increasingly load-bearing patterns available for updating. This is the same general process through which tacit knowledge develops, but applied in the opposite direction: repeated experience can both embody useful structures and loosen embodied structures that no longer serve their original function. Indeed, this mechanism helps explain why meditation can occasionally produce psychedelic-like moments through the release of deep tension, as psychedelics similarly loosen these rigid, load-bearing priors.

Connecting this to the representational layer, all representations bring their own properties into our field and reweight active priors. A representation imports properties into the field, and the body enacts them via active inference, so the somatic change is downstream of the field re-tuning, not the representation mysteriously touching muscle. It’s important to remember that these changes that the representation brings to the field are typically more surface level, affecting prediction branches that sit higher up, so the somatic changes it produces are usually the fast, unlatched kind (you tense or relax in the moment, and it releases when the representation drops), whereas the deep latched contractions that come from deep priors don’t change just because you adopted a new representation. For example, a representation of safety won’t do much if the body remains braced since interoceptive evidence still signals danger. Reframing can shift your state of being momentarily but doesn’t dissolve deep somatic holding unless that framing is held until it can sink deeper into the load-bearing tree. Now, nuance here is needed since there are instances in which representations, over time, can indeed affect the system deeply, like the practice of ideal parent figure protocol developed by Dr. Daniel P. Brown and David Elliott. In this instance, repeated imagery of attuned caregivers gives the field a stable representation of secure attachment that can be reinstantiated across sessions. In the terms of this framework, repeatedly using that representation while it produces an embodied sense of safety may gradually reduce the precision of older attachment predictions and train the new pattern(s) into the embodied layer, allowing an initially temporary representation to influence deeper priors and their coupled contractions. This results in a new field configuration and destabilizes the old attractor state and its basin, allowing for more flexibility. Importantly, time and repetition alone are not sufficient for a representation to reach deeper priors. The representation must generate trusted evidence and support actions that repeatedly produce outcomes the system can use to override its older predictions. So, representations re-tune the embodied layer when they are used, the body enacts the imported field via active inference, and this typically operates at the higher levels of branches in the load-bearing tree unless done so at a large enough time scale. 

Then moving to the notational layer, we must first remember anything in this layer requires compression of at least the embodied layer. Insight originates as an embodied felt sense and gets compressed into representation or notation, such as Einstein’s “muscular type” thoughts: “the above-mentioned elements are, in my case, of visual and some of muscular type.” Now, since the embodied layer is so involved with this process of bringing forth notation, and we have established that priors are physically held as tension/contraction, then the physical intuition behind these insights is the body’s stored prior structure coming alive as a felt sense. So embodiment-in-the-layer-sense and embodiment-in-the-somatic-sense are the same substrate doing the same thing. We can come to an implied conclusion that pure symbol interaction with no grounded intuition produces no genuine insight. Because notation is always a lossy translation of the embodied, with no felt sense driving the interaction, there’s nothing being compressed and no positive difference in felt tone guiding which interactions are meaningful. We’ve all felt this before: we are reading something, and five minutes later we realize we have not retained almost a single thing because no loop is running to get feedback from our embodied layer; it’s just pure symbol input. This does not mean notional manipulation is useless without the embodied layer, since notation can function as external scaffolding and generate structures that can become embodied later. It’s that notation become meaningful to the system through feedback with the representational and embodied layers rather than through notation alone.

Just as an example, we can take the names OpenAI vs ClosedAI. Both these words import different properties into the field, like open being expansive and safe and closed being contracted and guarded, along with all the implications associated with each that propagate through the system. The priors associated with “open” and “closed” run deep and are activated “automatically” here, since the notation is so common that its implications have been trained into the field over lots of repetition. These properties re-tune the field, which is felt somatically by using different patterns of tension to lean closer to open or closed. The field is embodied, so a representation that imports branches associated with “closed” produces actual haptic contraction, and one that imports branches associated with “open” produces a sense of release, even if very subtly. That said, the notation of “open” or “closed” can bring about representations that affect the field, but not always, since the priors associated with open and closed are deep enough to instantiate on their own without a deliberate representation being placed, which is why the embodied orientation can happen before you have consciously pictured anything. Keep in mind all of this is related to the individual and dependent on context and conditioning. “Open” can imply freedom in one field and vulnerability in another, while “closed” can imply restriction or protection depending on the active target.

3. Learning and Expertise

3.1 Metaphors

One of the main ways an abstract thing becomes meaningful to the mind-body system is through metaphor. Metaphor is a translation mechanism that dates back to the origins of language and is a part of the representational layer. Philosophers have long argued that early humans did not invent literal words first and embellish them later; rather, they used the physical world around them as an immediate landscape to grasp abstract realities. This marks the importance of metaphor: it is one of the clearest forms of content within the representational layer.

In Metaphors We Live By, George Lakoff and Mark Johnson establish an important thesis: “Metaphor is pervasive in everyday life, not just in language but in thought and action. Our ordinary conceptual system, in terms of which we both think and act, is fundamentally metaphorical in nature.” What they mean is that metaphors are not merely poetic devices or devices used in language, but they are unconscious structures that shape how we perceive reality, interact with others, and make decisions. We use language because we conceive of things that way, and we act according to how we conceive of them.

Returning to the example of “argument is war,” when we are having a disagreement, we can experience the argument as a battle. This is the act of conceiving a thing a certain way, which changes our actions due to changing field configurations. Using the war metaphor, our actions jump to winning and defending, the same way we would when fighting an enemy. If we were to instead use the metaphor “argument is a dance” on a societal level, our behavior might change to a more balanced and aesthetically pleasing interaction. The main point stands: metaphor in the representational layer shapes both the notational and embodied layers through language and behavior.

The embodied layer here plays a sneaky but important role: it is where metaphor earns its place. “Argument is war” works because you already know, in your body, what war-like conflict feels like, such as the bracing and the charge. “Time is money” works because you have dealt with scarce resources and felt the weight of using and running out. The metaphor makes the abstract felt sense intelligible by mapping it onto embodied structure; it is not arbitrary. This is what Johnson calls an image schema in The Body in the Mind: a recurring bodily pattern like containment, balance, or moving along a path, laid down by physical experience before any words. A felt sense that has no internal representation of its own can be made explicit through metaphor by using the embodied layer as crucial feedback. So the chain runs bottom to top, where the embodied layer supplies the felt sense, metaphor maps that embodied structure onto an abstract target to build a representation, and that representation is expressed through notation as language and enacted through the embodied layer as behavior. Without the embodied layer grounding it, the metaphor would not mean anything to the system; it would be hollow. The reason “argument is war” can move us to defend and attack is that the body already knows what defending and attacking feel like. We can also trace the stack back down as we process the notation, moving into the image of war, which activates the embodied characteristics associated with war. To give one more example, “feeling down” or “being on top of the world” relies on your physical experience of gravity, where standing upright can feel powerful and collapsing can feel disempowering.

Going back to mathematics to demonstrate metaphor’s importance for understanding, mathematics is a domain in which many of its objects are not directly sensory (you can’t touch a number), which is why understanding them often depends on grounding through metaphor and the embodied layer. This is why mathematicians reach for spatial and physical language constantly. As William Thurston puts it, “People have amazing facilities for sensing something without knowing where it comes from (intuition); for sensing that some phenomenon or situation or object is like something else (association); and for building and testing connections and comparisons, holding two things in mind at the same time (metaphor). These facilities are quite important for mathematics. Personally, I put a lot of effort into ‘listening’ to my intuitions and associations, and building them into metaphors and connections. This involves a kind of simultaneous quieting and focusing of my mind. Words, logic, and detailed pictures rattling around can inhibit intuitions and associations.” This illustrates why the notational layer is the “weakest” of the three in terms of richness: it can never capture the full felt sense of the underlying internal representation.

As a reminder, the representational layer is not only composed of metaphor. What matters is the general principle shared across the entire layer, which is that understanding is built by constructing a representation that fits the field. This could be a metaphor taken from the body, an image, or a mental simulation. Metaphor is simply the clearest usage of representation because it maps bodily structure onto the abstract, but it is just one method among several for developing representations within the layer where understanding actually resides.

3.2 Intuition {check whole for ai}

Intuition is your priors, your implicit structure, speaking directly. It is the field generating a salient representation or feeling before any explicit derivation. It is salient because the field gives it enough precision to make it worth attending to. Intuition is not experienced as algorithmic, but is a valuable signal about experience. The result is fast and pre-verbal, and it does not land in the notational layer, but in either the representational or embodied layer. Intuition is not itself a fit signal: a fit signal reports whether something satisfies the current field, while intuition is the rapid process by which something salient is generated. Results of intuition, like having a feeling that two things connect, are handed to you by the field, which is why an externalization or representation generated from intuition can reveal information about experience.

Intuition reaches toward possibilities through positive differences in felt tone and pulls back through negative differences. This is why a feeling of something being “off” is still a matter of intuition: the field is handing you a pre-verbal embodied assessment of fit before you have derived it step by step.

Neuroscientist Patrick House recounts a story about a young military driver taking an officer down a dangerous road in Iraq who suddenly slammed the brakes and turned around, saying, “I don’t have a good feeling.” In this framework, his trained field may have detected a violation: the road was unusually silent. This violation then surfaced as an embodied “off” feeling through a negative difference in felt tone, produced by the external input conflicting with the field. Hours later, the driver compressed that embodied feeling into notation, suggesting that the mothers may have kept their children inside because they expected danger. This is exactly what intuition is: the trained field delivering an embodied, pre-verbal feeling before notation can name the properties of the field being violated. This is the same mechanism that can contribute to “aha” moments. It is background field processing generating a result, just occurring on different time horizons.

There exists an optional step after this pre-verbal intuition surfaces, which is explicitly naming what properties in the field have been satisfied or violated. Sometimes this is easy, as in, “I have a feeling that these two things connect because of X and Y”; at other times, we must derive the properties slowly, by hand. When this explicit step occurs, we are compressing that intuition up into notation, which will not capture the entirety of it. Articulability of an intuition measures how compressible it is, not whether it is an intuition in the first place. If we do not have the proper notational knowledge in place, it will be harder to name that intuition since we don’t have the proper ways into which to compress it.

This also explains why intuition can be right or wrong. The answer lies in our field, and luckily our field can be fine-tuned through how we shape it. Untrained intuition, like that of a novice, is a rapid output from default priors that are not well tuned to the current context. Trained intuition, however, like that of an expert, is a rapid output from a field that is well tuned to what the context calls for, hence why the tacit knowledge of expertise is so valuable: it is the actionable expression of these intuitions that have been conditioned substantially. As David Bessis puts it, “Mathematical intuition is the same intuition we use every day, but developed and solidified by its confrontation with language and logic.” This confrontation has a name that I like to call “the loop of refinement.” Without this loop, one may confidently believe that an intuition from a miscalibrated field is correct, since it can feel identical to an intuition coming from a calibrated field. Intuition is only as trustworthy as the field it comes from, which means a refinement loop is needed to calibrate it well.

Mathematics makes this process especially visible because it forces pre-verbal intuition to be translated into explicit notation and then tested through formal proof.

3.3 Mathematics

To develop a deeper picture of The Compression Gradient, we can turn to mathematics, which can reveal what the phenomenology of traversing these layers is like. Fortunately, many great mathematicians and mathematical thinkers have given us subjective reports of what it feels like to use these layers in practice. Ironically, they are reporting on their own tacit knowledge, so these reports will be lossy.

Let’s start with one of the most well-known figures in this space, Einstein. In his response to Jacques Hadamard’s survey of mathematicians’ mental processes, he wrote: “The words or the language, as they are written or spoken, do not seem to play any role in my mechanism of thought. The psychical entities which seem to serve as elements in thought are certain signs and more or less clear images which can be ‘voluntarily’ reproduced and combined.” He continues: “this combinatory play seems to be the essential feature in productive thought, before there is any connection with logical construction in words or other kinds of signs which can be communicated to others.” On the secondary status of notation, he wrote: “The above-mentioned elements are, in my case, of visual and some of muscular type. Conventional words or other signs have to be sought for laboriously only in a secondary stage.” Jacques Hadamard framed the survey around this question: “It would be very helpful for the purpose of psychological investigation to know what internal or mental images, what kind of ‘internal words’ mathematicians make use of; whether they are motor, auditory, visual, or mixed.” Taken together, these reports suggest that at least some epistemic invention is largely pre-verbal, with notation and language coming at a later stage in the process.

Next, let us turn to Alexander Grothendieck. In Récoltes et Semailles, Grothendieck gives us a vivid account of the representational layer: “What drives and dominates my work, its soul and reason for being, are the mental images formed during the course of the work to apprehend the reality of mathematical things... All my life I’ve been unable to read a mathematical text, however trivial or simple it may be, unless I’m able to give this text a ‘meaning’ in terms of my experience of mathematical things, that is unless the text arouses in me mental images, intuitions that will give it life.” Later, he says, “Interrogating things, listening to the voice of things, means trying to imagine them, examining the mental images that form within you, seeking to solidify these images and make them clearer, working at unveiling more and more details, as when you try to recall a dream.” And: “[…] my fundamental difficulty in following the thought of others, without first translating it into my own images, and rethinking it in my own style.” What Grothendieck is most obviously trying to communicate, just like Einstein, is that the representational layer is being manipulated more directly than the notational layer. The notation may be changed, such as by writing or deleting parts of a formula, but it is the representational knowledge that is being expressed and simultaneously updated. This is one example of the bidirectional feedback between representation and notation described earlier.

A more practical example comes from Terence Tao, a leading contemporary mathematician, who shows how a shared internal representation lets two people communicate with far less notation while maintaining richness: “I find there is a world of difference between explaining things to a colleague, and explaining things to a close collaborator. With the latter, one really can communicate at the intuitive level, because one already has a reasonable idea of what the other person’s mental model of the problem is. In some ways, I find that throwing out things to a collaborator is closer to the mathematical thought process than just thinking about maths on one’s own, if that makes any sense.” What he’s trying to say is that if two collaborators already share a vivid internal representation of the thing they are communicating about, their communication can be compressed more heavily because each can reconstruct structure that the other leaves unstated. It is possible to share a rough gesture, and the other person can decompress it correctly using the shared internal representation they already carry. With a peer or someone you are just conversing with, you may lack that shared internal representation, so more of the structure must be made explicit in notation, and the other person must reconstruct a model from that still-lossy expression. Shared context lets you transmit the representational and embodied layers more easily through notation, since the structure of the content does not need to be rebuilt from scratch. The more shared models two people have, the more heavily they can compress their communication without losing usable structure.

Lastly, there is David Bessis and his book Mathematica. He says that “the magic power of mathematicians isn’t logic but intuition,” and that the written proofs we see are downstream products, while the central activity is developing inner intuition through mental imagery, just as Grothendieck describes. Bessis places great stock in intuition; it is the actionable component of layers coming alive within the present context. He argues, “Secret math, also known as mathematical intuition, can be found in the heads of mathematicians. It consists of mental representations and abstract sensations, often visual, that are for them quite obvious, and that give them a great deal of pleasure. But when it comes to sharing these sensations with the rest of the world, mathematicians are often at a loss. What had seemed so evident to them is suddenly less so. To transcribe their ideas, mathematicians have had to invent that esoteric language and those indecipherable symbols, just as musicians had to invent a complex musical notation in order to transcribe their compositions.” That sounds a lot like traversing the Compression Gradient! Mathematicians must, as Bessis puts it, transcribe their representations and intuitions in a legible way so others can understand what they are holding internally. Without this compression, collaboration would not be possible and progress would be lost, so it is a matter of pragmatism. In terms of this framework, Bessis gives the embodied and representational layers a central role when he states, “mathematicians’ intuition is their masterpiece, their lifetime accomplishment.” The vast and rich bottom two layers are so important to this account that the trained intuition they support becomes the mathematician’s deeper accomplishment. I highly recommend Mathematica if you want to learn more about intuition and the representational layer and how they apply to doing mathematics, especially at a high level.

Based on these reports, it is not unreasonable to say that many great mathematicians agree on an implicit truth: notation is not the whole difficult part of the process; it is also knowing when and how the internal representation corresponds with mathematical reality or truth. We see this in Grothendieck’s work to “solidify these images and make them clearer,” Bessis developing intuition until it becomes obvious and provides “a great deal of pleasure,” and Einstein combining images in “combinatory play.” These reports do not provide a complete formal rule or system for generating and evaluating those images. In the pre-formal stage, there is not always an explicit methodology for checking whether a mental image has captured the thing accurately; there is often first an internal sense of direction, like a compass. Formal verification, like proof writing, becomes possible as the intuition is compressed into notation, though feedback from notation can then revise the intuition. This compass lies below the notational layer, most of the time below articulation. Its signal does not guarantee truth; as the intuition section argued, it is only as reliable as the field that produces it. This leads us to a signal the compass gives, something we might call resonance.

3.4 Resonance & Friction

To talk about the importance of resonance, we must turn to Eugene Gendlin. Gendlin trained as a philosopher and later became widely known for his work in psychotherapy and Focusing. In the 1950s, he worked with Carl Rogers’s group at the University of Chicago, using a clinical setting to study how people symbolize experience and how felt experience becomes words, concepts, and change. Gendlin himself thought many therapy theories were too focused on content, such as “the client has hostility.” He did not declare the content wrong; instead, he believed that descriptions alone do not explain how change actually happens. In “A Theory of Personality Change,” he argued that knowing what is wrong is different from the felt process of changing it: “Yet it is clear that there is a major difference between knowing the conceptual explanation of personality (which one can devise in a few hours) and the actual feeling process of changing (which often requires years).” His research moved from what the client talks about to how the client talks and senses while talking. A client can express the same thing in two completely different ways depending on their bodily connection and presence. Experiencing is about the client’s manner of relating to experience, not only the content of speech. Skipping ahead, Gendlin and his colleagues arrived at a central finding in their research: clients who improved paused, turned their attention inward, sensed something unclear but bodily present, searched for words that fit it, and then felt some kind of movement or shift. This is the core of his process known as Focusing.

Next, we should turn to the felt sense, a term coined by Gendlin. The felt sense is not just an emotion; it is a vague, holistic bodily sense of a situation. A common example of the felt sense is when, while talking to someone, a murky feeling comes up in the body, somewhere like the stomach. It’s not yet a clear thought; it’s more like a bodily holding of the situation before language finds a way to compress it. This sense might make you feel like something is off or that you want to exit the conversation, but the feeling is not yet entirely clear to you. This should be differentiated from felt tone since the felt sense is the implicit bodily whole of a situation, while felt tone is its positive, negative, or neutral quality.

Working from the felt sense, Gendlin then named the felt shift. This is when you find a word, image, or sentence, which he calls a handle, that fits the felt sense, and something in you changes physically or emotionally. This feels like a release of contraction resulting in expansion, a sort of lightness. This, for Gendlin, is where the real change happens. The person experiences a whole new way of relating to the thing they were holding onto. We can extrapolate this beyond a clinical setting to a broader pair of phenomena called resonance and friction. Resonance is a noticeable positive felt response that occurs when an input fits the field and integrates more cheaply than expected. Friction is the corresponding noticeable negative felt response that occurs when an input costs more to integrate than expected. Because an input’s relation to a target can also produce positive or negative felt tone, not every pleasant response is resonance and not every unpleasant response is friction.

Moreover, friction can also signal that a constraint is blocking movement toward a valued attractor or that targets at different levels of the system are in conflict. This goes back to the field section of this framework. If friction persists after adjustments of a current state, then the reachability of the state you are trying to reach needs to be resurrected in a manner that makes more viable pathways available. Keep in mind that these two generators of friction, barriers to attractor states and conflicting targets, still contain the underlying principle that the attempted transition through the field costs more than the system predicted.

Resonance is that feeling when a word or name is on the tip of your tongue, and once it is found and said out loud, it feels like a relief. There is a shift like, “Oh yeah, I got it, so I can move on now.” Before finding the right name, someone else might say other options out loud, like “Is it X, Y, or Z?” while the response is, “No, no, not that one.” Finally, once the other person names it, it feels like a matching puzzle piece for your current understanding. During the initial attempt to think of the name, there was a holding onto something, an attempt to search through a vast internal landscape of intuition, as Bessis would put it. Then, when the correct name comes out, there is an obvious felt sense of correctness. To quote Grothendieck, “It is the naming that makes the awareness that has appeared irreversible, ineffaceable… [it is] a silent welcoming of a knowledge appearing in deeper layers of our being, where thought has no access.”

(In a related manner, the Bio-Emotive Framework by Dr. Douglas Tataryn uses what it calls Resonance Testing to identify and express feelings, sometimes producing expansion or crying.)

Crucially, resonance is only a felt sense of fit with our current field, not an objective truth-seeking mechanism. What one finds resonant is highly individualized and dependent on conditioning. Of course, in some ways, we are all conditioned by many of the same things, especially if we are part of the same culture. The felt sense of rightness is partly driven by how fluently an answer comes to mind, not by whether it is factually correct. As the common saying goes, “repetition builds intuition.” If we are constantly repeating something that is factually wrong but fits according to our embodied layer, an image can still produce resonance even if it is not based on “truth.” This is visible in trauma-related hypervigilance, where a threat-oriented field may make safe cues unexpectedly costly to integrate while making threat interpretations unusually fluent. In the same way, we might carry an incorrect representation of a math formula, but that image still remains resonant because we have yet to update it accordingly. This updating occurs through the loop of refinement which will be described later. Friction is equally dependent on the current field because it indicates that something was unexpectedly costly to integrate, not that the input was objectively false. A true but unfamiliar idea may produce friction, while a false but condioned one may produce resonance. Similarly, an incorrect representation may remain fluent and easy to integrate because it fits a field confirugation, but it produces resonance only when it integrates more cheaply than expected.

Returning to the field ontology of this framework, resonance and friction are pre-verbal embodied responses to input being tested against the field’s current configuration. They are changes in felt tone, not the same thing as Gendlin’s general felt sense. Resonance and friction are the two opposing directions an integration-cost difference can take, with the former signaling input that integrates more cheaply than expected and the latter signaling input that costs more to integrate than expected. When an input satisfies the field’s active properties, it fits; if it also integrates more cheaply than expected, we feel resonance. When an input violates or strains a sufficient property and consequently costs more to integrate than expected, we feel friction, even before we can name what was violated. Because there is always a default field active through low-level, fundamental priors like “thing-ness,” there is a background feeling of fit in experience, in which things are constantly tested for fit and happen to satisfy the field. Because of this, an obvious sense of “clicking” or feeling “off” reflects how far an input’s felt response deviates from the constant background baseline, with deeper priors producing stronger resonance or friction. As the Energetic Triad established earlier, resonance and friction can be understood as opposing differences between predicted and actual integration cost. 

Importantly within the field, resonance and friction also can change the field’s configuration. Under active inference, these two properties can change what happens next, which therefore has feedback loops of action that can reinforce interpretations and behavior. Resonance has the ability to increase the precision of the successful branch in the load-bearing tree taken pertaining to the input, which then reinforces precision and capacity allocated. Friction can redirect precision away from the branch taken, which can result in updating models or taking action in another direction. Both of these things reinforce a loop of integration and action that retune the field in the direction of where precision is steered.

To put this in the frame of The Compression Gradient, resonance and friction can become especially useful signals when moving between layers. Each time you compress a felt sense up into words or decompress someone’s words back down into your own embodied layer, resonance or friction can tell you whether the translation integrates more or less successfully than expected. The felt shift can signal a successful move between layers, where the sentence corresponds with your field and fits the embodied material more successfully than expected. When a move costs more to complete than expected, friction is introduced. Friction means the system sensed something that violates a constraint of the field; the attempt to find fit with input in the layer below or above the one you are moving from does not produce a correspondence that works without additional processing. Because the two lower layers are not fully legible from the outside, resonance and friction act as internal compasses for figuring out what works and what does not. They guide exploration, but they do not replace external testing or formal verification.

This is where the field’s “fit distance” property matters. This property tells you whether an input sits closer to or farther from the field’s current configuration. Take the tip-of-the-tongue example. There might be active variables and constraints like “the name starts with a certain letter” or “the name is of a celebrity.” If an externalization is presented that violates these properties, it will not resonate with the current field. The presented name sits at the far end of the fit-distance spectrum, so it fails as an input for the current search even if it remains salient. If processing that mismatch costs more than expected, the candidate may also produce friction. Fit distance affects whether an input can integrate, but closeness alone does not guarantee resonance and distance alone does not guarantee friction; the cost must differ noticeably from what the field predicted.

This also explains the experience of feeling that something is “off” about an externalization. In this case, the system detects friction but has not yet built an explicit representation of the cause of that friction. The embodied layer registers this negative difference in felt tone before the representational layer can identify which variable or constraint was violated. There is noticeable friction but no located cause yet. Having no explanation for the friction does not mean the signal is a failure; it simply means an explanation has not yet been constructed. A clear example of this is demonstrated in the TV show Mad Men, season 3, episode 2. In one scene, the team produces a commercial and shows it to a client. Both the client and, later, one of the ad men, Harry Crane, agree that it is a good commercial but that something about it is off. None of them, however, can say what makes it feel off. Ultimately, the client rejects the commercial even though it is almost exactly what they asked for and regards it as a failure. Both the client and Harry are describing the embodied layer registering a mismatch between the ad and their felt sense of what it should be, so the system registers friction. However, the key point is that no one has activated an explicit representation that can locate which property is producing the friction. They can feel that something was violated without first having to name it or put it into a representation. It could be said that this is part of the point of hiring experts like these in the first place: they can detect that variables or constraints were violated in a field before they can necessarily determine which ones. They can turn the vague felt sense of something being off into concrete work that identifies and reduces that friction. The power of a well-calibrated field is that it can register subtle contextual violations quickly, though locating their cause may require further work.

Importantly, checking for fit internally does not always happen slowly or consciously. A representation does not need to show up as a vivid image or a live simulation for resonance or friction to occur. However, implicit or rapid fit does not automatically count as resonance, and an ordinary expected mismatch does not automatically count as friction. In speech especially, which is a form of notation, the checking process often runs implicitly and quickly. Deep priors and repeated use matter because using a cached expression like “how are you doing” means the system does not need to actively search for whether it fits. The representation is either very “small,” like a quick flash, or bypassed because the expression has become a cached output. Because this expression is expected to integrate cheaply, it will usually sink into the baseline rather than produce resonance. However, when someone is speaking from a felt sense, the process changes. When speaking, successive words or sentences may be checked against the field in real time. When a person pauses and says something like, “What I’m trying to say is…,” they are feeling friction between the words they just expressed and the active field. When a later phrase lands unexpectedly well, the resulting positive difference becomes noticeable as resonance. Fluent externalizations such as cached speech can appear to skip activating an explicit representation, but repeated use and conditioning make them fit the field so reliably that the checking happens quickly and implicitly, without becoming noticeable.

To hit on a point made earlier about background compression and decompression, it seems as though most “aha!” moments of insight are resonant. This is because, as our system works in the background without conscious direction, it implicitly reorganizes the field and the representations active within it. When the embodied layer registers this update as an unexpectedly successful fit, it releases the small cognitive tension it was holding, just as a well-fitting handle does when it lands. A sudden moment of clarity in the shower is the system updating itself, which feels resonant, and because it is going on in the background rather than through our steering, it is a surprise when it happens. This is why the process is something like this: you’re not really thinking about the thing → a sudden insight comes that feels resonant → you say something out loud like “finally,” which is not the insight itself but the notational expression of the cognitive tension being released, resulting in a sense of expansion. As a reminder, resonance means an unexpectedly successful fit, not factual correctness. A moment of insight may fit your system at the moment, but that does not mean the insight cannot later be updated.

An interesting thing about resonance and friction is that they fluctuate temporally. This means the same object can be resonant one day and less so the next or produce friction in one context but not another. I can cry when I watch a film because something in it resolves or fits unexpectedly well, and when I rewatch it the next month, I can feel close to nothing. As our field is changing moment to moment on varying scales based on context, it makes sense that different properties will be salient at different points in time, which determines whether we feel resonance or friction. A wide variety of things seem to influence this, like sleep, mood, recent input, physical state, current worries, the environment and physical context, and novelty or repetition. Considering the field is a deployment of our priors that are weighted moment to moment, it makes sense that salient properties will change based on the specific context. It also seems like the integration of a thing into our field, say a film, influences the intensity of felt senses, since the differences lose strength. Watching the same film over and over can generally cause the stream of input to be integrated into the baseline tone, which lessens the spike in felt sense and makes the film feel dull because it is predicted to be easy to integrate due to repetition. Potential for resonance can also return once lost if the properties of the current field are ones that make the input resonate. Maybe a year later, I watch the film again, and a recent life event allows old disconnected material to organize through it in a new way, producing resonance. 

3.5 The Loop of Refinement



One must start with error and lead it over into truth.
That is, one must uncover the source of the error; otherwise hearing the truth is useless to us. It cannot enter as long as something else occupies its place.
To convince someone of the truth, it is not enough to state the truth; one must find the path from error to truth.
I must again and again dive beneath the water of doubt.

Ludwig Wittgenstein, Remarks on Frazer’s Golden Bough

We have two facts that make refinement necessary: resonance does not guarantee factual correctness, and notation is always lossy because it compresses knowledge as it moves up the layers. An internal representation can feel right but still not correspond with reality. Understanding therefore cannot be a single act of finding the right expression; it must be part of a greater whole, something like a loop of refinement.

The felt sense of “I understand this” is not enough. Take educational YouTube videos, which have become a tragedy of modern learning. A viewer may have a sense of understanding the thing, yet if they were to write an essay on what they learned or understood, the paper might remain mostly blank. This is why the testing effect is so important. Because of cognitive limitations, we must find a loop to ensure our understanding is being correctly iterated upon and tested for correspondence with reality. This active iteration is one central mechanism of learning.

This loop tests three things that are important for understanding: fit, coherence, and truth. Fit is how well a probe satisfies the current field, coherence is how well your model holds together internally, and truth is correspondence with reality. Accurate correspondence means something like the way you model the problem being in accordance with the actual shape of the thing, which gives intended/wanted feedback from reality. This requires keeping standard nuances in mind like framing effects and the fact that the map is never the territory, etc. 

There are two ways of running the loop, either open or closed. A closed loop tests a representation using properties already available within the system, like when thinking about an idea on a walk by yourself. An open loop introduces information or constraints from outside the current model, like working with a tutor to learn a subject. To note, just because you are running an open loop does not mean you are inviting correspondence with reality. Someone like a flat-earther could indeed be running an open loop but arrive at obviously false claims. Open loops make external correction possible, but do not guarantee it. What improves the chance of moving toward correspondence with reality is a complex combination of accurate feedback, falsifiable probes, beliefs favoring truth, and enough slack for contradictory evidence to be integrated rather than defended against.

In totality, this loop looks something like this:

felt sense → internal or external probe → test against the field  → resonance, friction, or baseline → locate the source of the response → update representation → new felt sense → ↻

As established earlier, intuition needs this loop to become well calibrated and attuned to the relevant context you are working within. However, the loop also needs intuition to run, it is a contingent relationship. Feeling resonance or friction is not the external feedback itself, but the change in felt tone produced once the field interprets that feedback. Locating the source of friction may involve intuition catching which properties were violated or satisfied, deliberate analysis, or using both (which is common). Intuition and the loop work together as you use intuition to generate and interpret each pass, and each pass tunes the field which then reshapes intuition.

It is important to note that we do not only use this loop when we deliberately sit down to learn or think. Lower-level re-ordering happens constantly and mostly below immediate awareness, since sensory input is continuously being tested against the field and small updates occur as we move through the world. Most passes of the loop are quick and implicit and never come to be recognized as an explicit turn, like the micro-adjustments we make when interacting with others. Deliberate usage of this refinement loop is a more specific version of this process where a representation is kept stable and scrutinized enough to be interacted with, like when writing an essay or solving a math problem. 

Relatedly, closed and open loops also run in parallel rather than only one being ran at individually. You can be changing a representation internally by imagining how something works, and in the next moment external feedback can affect your modeling of the thing through contradiction. The field integrates both channels nearly in parallel. The loop is therefore better modeled as many overlapping cycles happening continuously at different temporal variations and differing levels of awareness. The clean process laid out here is a simplification of one pass compared to the more dense and mostly automatic version that exists '“in the real world.” Still, understanding its core mechanics allows us to use it deliberately and wisely. 

Felt sense

We must begin with our felt sense of understanding, our current model of how a thing works, which is experienced first as an embodied sense. This is how the field sits in the body at a given moment; it’s our most immediate access to where the layers currently stand, although it can still be incomplete. The felt sense includes the default field and any additional properties introduced by representations, like using a metaphor to describe your current embodied sense of what you know. This is Gendlin’s findings at play.

Internal or external probe

A probe is an expression of what your felt sense is “saying.” It is a candidate expression of what the field currently holds, like “I think this works like this…” or “A metaphor I would use to describe this is…” It does not matter yet if this probe is factually correct; what matters is that it expresses your current understanding clearly enough to be tested. As Wittgenstein puts it, “One must start with error and lead it over into truth.”

There are two ways this probe can manifest: internal or external. An internal probe is run inside the mind using a representation like a simulation or an image. An external probe compresses the felt sense of understanding into notation, like drawing a diagram or writing about it.

To present a probe is to objectify your current model. You are making it a stable object you can inspect and manipulate. We do this internally by turning attention toward the representation we are holding or externally by translating it into notation. Externalization is especially powerful because it makes the probe legible enough for other people and systems.

Roger Thisdell offers a useful structural parallel in his streamlined enlightenment protocol. He argues that before a phenomenon can be deobjectified, it must first be objectified, isolated clearly enough that the mind can recognize it as an object of experience. Only then can it be seen through as empty, contingent, and not ultimately solid. In non-meditation jargon, before we can deconstruct our perception of something, we must first bring it into immediate experience and make sure our system registers it as an inspectable object.

The epistemic loop uses an analogous move. To refine embodied or representational knowledge, we must first objectify it by making it stable enough for the mind to inspect. Attention can do this internally by sustaining a representation like an image, while notation can do it externally through a fixed sentence or diagram. Thisdell’s deobjectification and epistemic testing are not identical operations since his concerns contemplative action which is geared towards phenomenological changes, while this loop tests where a model breaks against internal or external properties. The shared principle is that implicit material must first be isolated before its construction can be examined.

Remember that externalizing is lossy because of compression, so forming a probe does not capture the full essence of understanding, but makes part of it usable and inspectable. If your understanding is not rich enough yet, the externalization will be especially imprecise because the knowledge has not yet developed deeply within the bottom two layers.

Working memory places an additional limit on the internal loop because, in simple unchunked tasks, we can typically hold only around three or four items or chunks in focal attention at once. This limitation is one reason a vague felt sense of understanding can be misleading: the system may be holding only a highly compressed outline rather than the whole structure. To work around this constraint, we can offload items into external notation or develop organized chunks in long-term memory. As Justin Skycack puts it, “Whereas a beginner perceives individual isolated pieces of information, an expert perceives ‘chunks’ of information organized into meaningful patterns and structures. These chunks are physically encoded as wiring in the expert’s long-term memory, and they are the building blocks that make up the expert’s representation of what they’re looking at in working memory.” Externalization and expertise therefore extend the loop in complementary ways by adding structure into the environment, while the other compresses it into retrievable internal structure.

Test the probe: resonance, friction, or baseline

This part covers how the field responds when the probe is tested. If the probe produces resonance, this does not mean the probe is entirely truthful; the field itself may not correspond with reality. It simply means that the probe integrated more cheaply or satisfyingly than the system expected, producing a positive difference in felt tone. If we hit friction, we are encountering input that is more costly or difficult to integrate than expected, producing a negative difference in felt tone. If the probe fits about as expected, the response may remain at baseline and provide little noticeable signal at all.

Because resonance and friction are differences from the background baseline, neither is guaranteed to appear every time a probe fits or fails to fit. A baseline response may mean that the probe is unsurprising which does not produce a noticeable spike.

There are four places where a mismatch can become detectable. The first is the embodied layer: you might write a sentence explaining something and, when you do, your body disagrees. There’s a slight twinge that tells you to try again. Second is the representational layer: through externalizing the model, it becomes clear what is missing, how your representation was incomplete, or how your memory is not up to date. Third is the notational layer: the sentence has implications or contradictions you could not have registered while it was abstract. Lastly, external evidence can produce friction after being interpreted by the field. This can happen by sending a probe out and having the world push back, such as a tutor saying it is wrong, or by taking in new information that contradicts your model. In this final case, external reality gives the constraint, while friction is the internal felt response to it.

It is important to parse out these four locations and categorize the first three as closed, meaning the model is tested using internally available constraints, and the fourth as open, meaning a constraint enters from outside the current model. Closed loops primarily test fit and coherence, while open loops can test correspondence with reality. Even then, the outside source may itself be mistaken, so open feedback must also be evaluated.

This testing can be fast and implicit, like saying a phrase out loud, such as “How are you?” and seeing how it lands in the system, or receiving feedback from others and immediately wondering, “Why did they say that?”

Locate the source of friction or resonance

Because friction simply tells you something is off, not what is off, we need a diagnostic step to understand how we can precisely update our understanding. With this step, we can know that something has failed to integrate and then investigate whether our model is actually what needs to change.

We first must locate where the response arose. Is it coming from the embodied, representational, or notational layer, or was it triggered by external evidence? Is the friction coming from some new information I just read or from my internal representation not being complete? We can also locate which field property is being violated, like a variable or constraint. For example, “This variable cannot be this, so that is where the friction is coming from.”

As a caveat, just because friction occurs does not mean updating needs to happen. The response could be coming from mistaken notation, a body in fight-or-flight mode, or a misreading of the question. With a diagnosis, we can better determine whether our model is incorrect and ensure that we are updating in light of accuracy.

This step is one place where expertise matters: within a well-calibrated domain, experts can locate the source of friction much faster and more accurately than a novice. Because they understand their field so well, the violated properties become clearer, and they can surgically manipulate the things causing the friction. Expertise does not make the response truthful, however, especially outside the domain and context in which that intuition was trained.

The other side of locating the source of friction is locating where resonance is coming from. I would argue this part is not as needed to train understanding, but it is helpful in conditioning what your system finds good. For example, if I generate a metaphor in my head that I then explain and it checks out as being accurate, it might be helpful to assess why this is. Is it the feedback loops that made it a good model? What about its variables? This can then let us in on different principles that could be extrapolated elsewhere, like, “Okay, this shape takes this form when X or Y, so I can apply this to this other problem.” This is training intuition on the resonant side.

If the response stays at baseline a new question comes to light that asks was the probe already expected or was it too weak to test the relevant structure? In that case, the next step may be to construct a more specific probe rather than just update the model. 

Update representation

After encountering friction and locating its source, we can update our representation of the thing to be a better fit, either externally or internally. When open, we are updating it toward better correspondence with the available input we believe to be of higher order; when closed, we are updating it toward fit and coherence abiding by the internal substrate. In any scenario, revising the representation retunes the field and its properties, like variables and constraints, which is why the embodied felt sense may eventually shift after an explicit update.

It must be said that there is generally an update-speed hierarchy. Notation updating is fast: we can quickly erase a sentence we just wrote and change it to match what is better supported. A representation update is slower: we must change its interactions, what it looks like, its feedback loops, or its general shape in our mind. Surface-level somatic retuning can also happen quickly when the representation changes, but deeply embodied updates are usually the slowest because they involve load-bearing priors that structure perception in the first place. One contradiction won’t rewrite a prior repeated thousands of times, just as doing a task one time won’t generate true tacit knowledge.

To address the field of experience, this loop makes the landscape clearer, like making the edges of a thing more visible so you know where you stand. The refinement loop makes the field more navigable, like a foreigner becoming accustomed to new land. By iterating on how we model the thing, we redraw the boundaries, slopes, and paths of the field to better align with reality. Externalizations help us redraw the landscape, while feedback reveals where the new map still fails. Friction can make violated variables and constraints visible enough that the internal representation can be updated accordingly. The mistakes created by each lossy externalization reveal hidden constraints, potentially making the next expression less wrong and the field itself more accurate and differentiated. Over time, territory that was once foreign becomes familiar as it is slowly untangled, like a messy bundle of yarn being cleanly unwound.

This act of making representations and the field clearer seems similar to the skill of sensory clarity in meditation. Sensory clarity involves learning to distinguish phenomena in experience without immediately becoming lost in their movement or story. It’s like tracking the different sensations occurring in one specific area of your left foot; it’s a skill that takes time to develop. This parallels the process of making internal knowledge clearer by untangling confusion. Sensory clarity is resolution-building at the embodied layer, while refinement is resolution-building at the representational layer. Both skills use a similar move of taking an abstract blob and differentiating it over time to see what is actually there. In this limited sense, sensory clarity can reduce suffering while representational clarity can reduce ignorance.

One caveat here is that updating toward resonance alone without an independent constraint can make wrongness feel true, even if it has no correspondence with reality. A field can become internally coherent while remaining detached from the world. Empirical models therefore require empirical feedback, while formal models require constraints like proof. Other people and “factual” information can provide additional checks. Some independent source of constraint is needed to keep refinement from becoming only the production of increasingly satisfying representations.

New felt sense → loop

For an update to be “successful,” it must eventually become integrated into the field, which may generate a new felt sense. This new felt sense does not need to be deep, like a core belief; it can just be a new way of looking at things. For example, maybe you updated a metaphor to be more accurate, and now the update has generated new constraints you did not have in mind before. If the notation changes but the felt sense does not, this may indicate that the update has not yet propagated into the representational or embodied layers.

Now that this new felt sense exists, we can restart the loop with our new understanding and form another probe. Each new probe can become better calibrated as long as reliable feedback is incorporated, allowing the field to become more accurate and differentiated.

This loop is not a one-and-done thing. Many passes are needed for deep change, especially for embodied knowledge, which is where expertise lives. Repeated passes can also propagate surface-level representational changes into lower, more load-bearing branches of the tree, much like repeated practice gradually turns explicit knowledge into tacit knowledge.

This active iteration is a central mechanism of learning: it uses the mechanisms of knowledge and their different layers to loop on updating toward truth. Passive input, like educational YouTube videos, pales in comparison to actively participating in this loop of refining internal states, although passive input can still supply material for later testing and refinement.

The end state of this loop is not to reach perfect truth, but to progressively improve calibration. Through fine-tuning our field, we are orienting ourselves toward experience in a manner that is better aligned with available feedback while remaining useful for our targets.

I believe Grothendieck almost perfectly captures the essence of refinement:



When I am curious about something, mathematical or otherwise, I question/[interroge] it. I question it, without caring if my question is perhaps stupid or if it will appear so, without it being carefully weighed. Often the question takes the form of an assertion - an assertion which, in truth, is a knocking probe. I will then believe more or believe less in the assertion, which depends of course on where I stand in the comprehension of the things I’m looking at. Often, especially at the beginning of a research, the assertion is completely false - but this still had to be done to convince yourself. Often, it suffices to write it down for it to become obvious that it is false, whereas before writing it down there was a vagueness/[flou], like an uneasiness/[malaise], instead of obviousness. It then enables us to return to the probing with less ignorance, with a question-assertion that is perhaps a little less “off the mark”. More often still, the affirmation taken literally turns out to be false, but the intuition which, still clumsily, tries to convey itself through this is correct, though remaining vague. This intuition will, little by little, be decanted from an equally shapeless gangue of false or inadequate ideas, it will gradually emerge from the limbo of the misunderstood which only asks to be understood, of the unknown which only asks to be known, to take a form which is her own, to refine and sharpen its contours, as the questions I ask of these things in front of me become more precise or more pertinent, to surround them more and more closely.

Alexander Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles

3.6 Real-making vs Truth-making

In this loop, there exists two main orientations that it can take, which I am calling real-making and truth-making. Just as the open and closed versions of the loop can be ran at parallel, these two orientations of either version can be ran simultaneously to reinforce each other.  

Real-making, in general, is the process by which something becomes embodied and actionable within the field. Repetition, attention, aesthetics, emotion, social reinforcement, and practice can all deepen its priors and make it a more reachable field configuration from a global point-of-view. It is making a thing more “real” in the sense of it mattering more to the system. To real-make something is to make it meaningful within a present configuration of the field. This process is moreso correlated with closed loops because what is being repeatedly tested is simply internal with no meaningful external feedback. Of course it is possible we can detect logical errors and misonerms in our models, but this is much harder to do closed because it is relying on your current field configuration to supply you with the necessary working properties. 

Resonance helps push real-making further because the feeling can make a representation easier to embody and take seriously as a concept rather than simply discarding it. When a thing is resonant, there’s like a hook attached to it that is hard to let go of, this of course can make things seem a lot more meaningful than without resonance. However, other orthogonal feelings can real-make a thing to like fear and authority so it should be made clear resonance is not necessarily needed for the process. Real-making is about integration and stabilization, not necessarily positive felt tone, so any mechanism that contribute to those two multipliers is what matters here. 

Truth-making on the other hand is the process of testing and updating a model so that it better corresponds with reality. The process depends on constraints, like evidence, argument, and other people, that push back against the current field so naturally the process is closely correlated with open loops. The process of truth-making is essential to making things reliable so external feedback gives consistent and useful results. This dynamic shifts when viewed across different levels of abstraction. For example, someone may be “truth-making” within a frame of religion, like debating about ancient texts, but from a higher-order outside perspective of a non-believer they are simply real-making false claims. What the outsider does not understand is that the debaters are indeed truth-making within the ontological constraints the frame they are operating in, and because we are always operating in some type of environment that places structure onto what is possible, all truth-making is ultimately relative to the boundaries and rules set by that environment. Of course resonance is not required for truth-making since accurate information may initially produce friction because it conflicts with existing priors.

To be clear, the loop of refinement itself is a neutral process; it contains no inherent orientation. The loop does not move towards truth on its own, it just modulates whatever its targets and feedback environment select for. An echo chamber contains genuine refinement loops, but they may refine a belief for a target that is not in favor of external correspondence, such as social acceptance or emotional resonance. 

Real-making can be useful before truth-making because an idea may be initially be too vague or fragile to test. If we “incubate” the idea long enough through real-making, it can allow us to stabilize and scrutinize the idea instead of us instantly discarding it when we encounter friction. Michael Nielson speaks of something similar by using the concept of “creative cocoons”: “To be creative, you need to recognize those barely formed thoughts, thoughts which are usually wrong and poorly formed in many ways, but which have some kernel of originality and importance and
truth. And if they seem important enough to be worth pursuing, you construct a creative cocoon around them, a set of stories you tell yourself to protect the idea not just from others, but from your own self doubts. The purpose of those stories isn’t to be an air tight defence. It’s to give you the confidence to nurture the idea, possibly for years, to find out if there’s something really there.” Those set of stories are there precisely to real-make the thing so it can be protected and registered with priors as meaningful. The danger here occurs when the protective process seals the cluster off to outside feedback due to sealing itself off. 

Crucially, the main things driving the truth-making orientation on a global scale, meaning an orientation set outside the current frame and operating at a higher level of abstraction, are higher order targets for accuracy and correspondence with reality (targets that stay stabilized long-term), alongside a willingness to expose local frames to constraints they cannot control. It means having the ability to ensure field configurations reach a stable attractor state in which targets aiming for truth exist and matter to the system. Ironically, we must “real-make” goals of truth-seeking and error-correction so that correspondence with reality becomes an active priority within the system. This is long since why I have believed that learning and “reach towards” truth is more of a spiritual endeavor than an intellectual pursuit as it requires sources from within that must summon the courage to surrender control and look outside established priors that exist only to protect the current model. This is why a certain amount of slack, like safety, is needed for quality truth-making: without it, the system will simply prioritize protecting load-bearing priors which would be very costly to update. 

3.7 Training Data

I want to touch on the importance of training data and why high quality training data is so important. By training data, I mean the books, articles, art, conversations, social situations, and other experiences that train how our system perceives and acts. In one sense, all sensory input is training data, but I am using the term more narrowly here to emphasize what we consume or repeatedly engage with in ways that shape perception. Your field, which determines what resonates, carries a compressed history of what you have been trained on, in a sense. Because of this, the quality and diversity of your training data strongly shape the loaded distinctions, chunks, relations, and models available to perception, which in turn constrains what you can parse and externalize.

Importantly, training data helps build the field that the Loop of Refinement uses as its substrate while also supplying many of the open-loop constraints through which that field can be corrected. The new information you take in or the feedback you get affects how your model is updated, so bad training data has the potential to erode the corrections the loop tries to make. Put simply, training data gives the evidence from which priors can be learned, while feedback helps determine which of those remain calibrated to reality, or the frame you are working in.

Our fields are not arbitrary, of course. Their structure emerges from the interaction between the trunk of the load-bearing tree and a lifetime of training data and action. Different instances of training data are weighted very differently depending on their precision, context, repetition, emotional importance, source credibility, and relation to active targets. For example, an “I love you” coming from your mom is going to be weighted much more heavily than a stranger on the internet saying the same thing. And, a fact coming from someone you respect and admire will matter much more to the system than the same fact coming from someone you find un-trustworthy. 

Importantly, training data matters not only because it tunes your field but also because it constrains the structures that the field can generate and recombine. What we can think or express is constrained by the priors and chunks already available to us. Novelty can then arise by recombining, decomposing, abstracting, and reorganizing those structures into configurations that have never previously been instantiated. But then this begs the question: how do new things come to be that seem novel? My answer is that existing chunks and properties can be reoganized while the field makes a new thing out of the configuration, even if in full is has never appeared in training data before. We may also be able to describe a possible state using our current chunks without being able to instantiate the gestalt itself because the priors necessary to organize experience in that way have not yet been learned or cannot be activated on command. For example, a student can understand the formal definition of a math proof, but still struggle to construct one because they lack the underlying processes to know which path to take for construction. 

So your field, which is “the frame” from which we act, is trained through this accumulated history. This gives us a principle of garbage in, garbage out: a field built on narrow or inaccurate training data can produce a resonant, but wrong understanding and the actions that follow from it. Of course, what counts as a bad action also depends on the active field’s targets and predictions. A field trained to anticipate rejection may experience acceptance as friction because the “good” outcome of acceptance violates what the field expected.

This is also why the distribution of training data matters. Remaining inside an echo chamber can produce bad overgeneralization because the field never integrates the variation that would force its properties to update toward a more “complete” model. This results in a type of expert who might be in a domain for a long time but only sees one way to handle things because their training data has been too narrow, resulting in a field with rigid variables and constraints. Broader and well-chosen training data can reduce this kind of overfitting by making the field to distinguish what generalizes from what works only under a narrow set of conditions. A field can therefore become internally coherent and highly resonant while remaining poorly calibrated the training data it has used never forces wise revision. 

Repeated, sufficiently weighted training data can gradually train its interpreted properties into the default field, particularly when those properties are repeatedly reinforced through feedback. What you repeatedly consume and engage with can become part of your baseline perception, felt as reality itself, so repeated training data should be chosen carefully. Because we have some choice in this matter, choosing your training data is partly choosing what your future default field may become if it is repeated and weighted for long enough. As an example, my aesthetic taste has undoubtedly been refined through years of scrolling Pinterest and Tumblr, slowly but surely training the variables and constraints my field uses. Over time, repeated exposure and selection have trained an increasingly high-dimensional aesthetic target into my embodied layer. I do not need to explicitly state all of its properties when evaluating a new image. The accumulated structure first appears as an immediate intuition about what fits, and language comes afterwards to compress that intuition into words.

Quality of training data is important as well. Think of the difference between content optimized to get clicks vs an expert compressing decades of experience into a careful explanation. The higher quality training data can tune the field to finer variables and constraints, sharpening your internal compass for resonance. In this instance, you are not directly downloading the expert’s embodied knowledge. Instead, you are using their externalization to reconstruct some of the chunks and relations that took them years to learn. This can shorten the path, but those structures must still be rebuilt and tested within your own field to reach into the representational and embodied layers. However, low quality training data can also shape the field, only toward inaccurate, shallow, or poorly calibrated structure. Of course, low and high quality depend partly on your target. When the target is truth-making, quality depends on whether the training data helps the field form chunks and heuristics that remain calibrated across different ontological systems. 

This matters especially when the system is attempting to confirm harmful priors like “I am not good enough.” Our system will find confirming training data resonant and disconfirming training data frictional. Bad training data might not feel bad from the inside because false information can still resonate with the existing field. Confirmation bias can orient us away from what is true or good for us while our structural limitations prevent us from seeing the error.

Finally, practice, like solving problems by hand, is important when dealing with training data because it determines what structure is extracted from it. Two people can consume the same training data, like a blog post, and build very different fields or chunks because varying factors like attention and active targets determine what gets conditioned. For this reason, though it is a crucial step, choosing better training data is not enough by itself. It must be paired with active engagement and open-loop correction.

4. Emergence, Thresholds, and Creativity

4.1 Emergence and Failed Gestalts

Emergence is a very relevant concept in this framework because it brings together many of the mechanisms already defined to explain how whole things become available to experience. In the sense being used here, for a thing to be emergent, it must appear whole, not fractured. Emergence, then, is the appearance of properties produced by the organization of the whole rather than by a component taken separately. By this I mean that initially separate components are meaningfully organized, rather than something magically appearing from nothing. The resulting whole depends on conditions like sensory evidence and precision weighting. If you change those conditions, a different whole may appear.

The duck and rabbit illusion provides a simple example:





The markings remain largely the same, but changes in the weighting of possible interpretations allow either a duck or a rabbit to organize the field. One interpretation of the image typically dominates at a time because the same features must be organized through different relations. Neither interpretation about what is appearing exists within the markings themselves. Each appears when the markings and the field’s available structure are organized into a specific gestalt.

Let us take failed higher order gestalts as an example. What I mean by this is that some things can appear to us as almost forming something whole, but do not quite reach a level of higher order organization that satisfies enough of the field’s properties to become whole. Take this image, for example:





Some things within the image can still be named, such as patterns of shapes, colors, edges, textures, lighting, and a general vibe. These are already lower level gestalts, meaning less obvious than higher order ones. The image is therefore not completely without organization. The failure occurs at a higher level because those lower-level things like shapes imply that we are looking at a real scene containing known things, while the possible objects never organize enough to satisfy the field’s threshold for objecthood. Several interpretations may partially activate, but none explains enough of the structure to be dominant.

The unsettling vibe of the image therefore comes from the field attempting to maturate recognizable lower level qualityies into higher order things and failing. The lower level cues predict a resolvable scene that should be easy to process, but the actual cost of integrating the cues into real objects is greater than predicted. Since a very default low-level target of “this image should be recognizable” is dominant, this failure produces a negative difference in felt tone experienced as friction. If the system did not predict the image to process or had no target that depended on it being fully comprehensible, however, the same failure to produce high level objects could remain closer to baseline rather than producing noticeable friction.

This demonstrates that things are not simply “there” and received in their completed form. They are actively constructed as lower level features and relations are organized by the field. In this image, many of the necessary low level tokens are present, but the whole never comes alive because no available interpretation satisfies enough of the relevant properties the field is holding. It is plausible a differently trained field might possess chunks that allow all objects in the image to correctly process if the organization fits neatly.

If the image were gradually morphed into a recognizable object, say through an animation, the external conditions supporting the gestalt would change until the object became valid to the current field. If this resolution happened more easily than the system predicted, it would produce a positive difference in felt tone experienced as resonance. If the resolution of the morph was fully expected, it might simply return the system to baseline.  When the viewer takes actions such as changing their perspective to reduce uncertainty, those actions can be understood through active inference because the system is actively using the image in ways expected to produce a valid interpretation.

This same general process appears in a lot more cases than just weirdly designed images like the one I presented. A sentence can contain familiar words while failing to produce a coherent, whole meaning. A math problem can contain individually understood pieces while its organizing structure remains questionable. 

The same general process appears in less obviously perceptual cases. A sentence can contain familiar words while failing to produce a coherent meaning. A mathematical problem can contain individually understood pieces while its organizing structure remains unavailable. An emotional experience can contain recognizable sensations like a fast heart beat while the person remains unable to interpret what they mean as a whole. In every case, lower level recognizable features are available while the relevant higher order gestalt fails to stabilize. And in every case, we have the ability to “morph” the relations of the features, and the features themselves so a whole gestalt can be recognized. For instance in a sentence that has recognizable words but produces friction, we can morph it into a form that registers in our system as valid, and sometimes resonant.

More precisely, this kind of morphing can be understood as a structure-preserving transformation. When rewriting a sentence, the intended meaning and important relations act as the structure we are trying to preserve, while properties like word choice and order remain open to change. Each reformulation becomes a probe generated by the Loop of Refinement which then is tested for resonance or friction. The goal in this instance is to find a notational form that preserves enough of the original embodied or representational structure for a coherent gestalt to be reconstructed.

This is also why we cannot fully instantiate a gestalt for which our system does not yet possess the needed priors or chunks. We may be able to construct an approximation using existing structures, like guessing what we might feel in a novel situation, but the actual organization cannot become available until its prerequisites have been learned and can be in relation together. Failed gestalts therefore show emergence in reverse since the organization has not yet become stable enough for a valid whole to appear. 

The next question is why those conditions can develop gradually while the resulting gestalt made from those parts seems to arrive all at once.

4.2 Creativity and Structural Thresholds

The previous section described failed higher order gestalts through lower level priors not being stable enough for the whole to appear. Creativity can be understood as a search process that sometimes resolves this failure. It keeps different chunks active until one becomes sufficiently supported to register as a new gestalt, something novel. Structural thresholds explain why this search can develop gradually while its result feels like a sudden movement from “here” to “there.” A structural threshold is the critical point where enough lower-level chunks become sufficiently stable to abruptly percolate into an available higher-order gestalt.





I’ve long since been fascinated by this tweet by  because it displays something very useful: the way that moving from “here” to “there” so often feels like a discontinuous jump with no clear progressive path between the two, like his example of making money online being totally unthinkable until you do it, and then totally obvious everyone’s doing it. The discontinous jump part here in particular makes sense in the light of this framework and how it views creativity. Creativity here is a search process through a possibility space, with a target of stabilizing existing chunks into seemingly novel gestalt(s). You can only operate from the chunks your input history and field has learned, so every new idea is a novel manipulation of existing knowledge the field actively uses to search for answers to targets. 

As established in the training data section, the chunks and relations available to the field constrain where creative search can begin and occur. Most people would be better advised to diversify their input in order to train more rich and varied chunks for more combinatorial power. Importantly, this means there is no absolute novelty, since there are only combinations that are new relative to a given individual field, hence why one idea can feel like an obvious next step to someone and a giant leap in innovation to another. The person who made up the novel idea has a field that already stored the needed chunks and the paths between them, so the new combination sits close on their prediction tree and is reachable. To an individual who lacks those needed chunks, the idea isn’t a nearby search at all since the prerequisites aren’t stored. The felt “out-there-ness” of an idea is not an inherent property of the thing but a measurement of the distance between the field that produced it and the field encountering it. This is what we can call creative distance: the amount of reorganization required for a new gestalt to become reachable from the creator’s current field. 

Lesswrong has modeled something related called ‘inferential distance,’ which is the “amount of steps or concepts a person needs to share before they can successfully communicate the object level point.” This differs from creative distance because it is spelling out the difference in prerequisite structure to result in perception rather than creation. 

This also emphasizes the importance of knowledge graphs and mapping out prerequisites, since certain chunks are literally needed before learning and using ideas that sit higher on the tree of knowledge. It’s like the load-bearing tree: some structure of the trunk is needed before branching out. 

As a caveat, recombination is not the only source of novelty in the field. Open loops have the ability to import constraints the field could not have generated internally due to lacking prerequisites or deep attractor basins which prevent the correct structure from being reachable. That is why doing the work and practicing produces understanding that just “thinking harder” does not, and why a closed loop can refine structure indefinitely without adding anything novel. So “no absolute novelty” means newness is relative to a given field, not that nothing new is imported. In terms of a starting point within the human system (and other agentic systems), this framework does not attempt to map out where structure originates in the first place, or why any specific pattern shows up in a system at all, which is what people like Michael Levin are attempting to do. This framework’s intentional boundary takes it that our starting point comes from “innate” structure like interoceptive homeostasis, genetics, and evolutionary pressures. 

This is ultimately what the tweet is pointing at. Moving from “here” to “there” follows the same general kind of dynamic as gestalt formation, only across a longer time scale. Before the transition, partial prerequisites and candidate organizations may be accumulating without any alternative becoming stable enough to appear. Once enough supporting structure has developed, a thing crosses a threshold and becomes available as a new higher order gestalt. The experienced change can therefore feel discontinuous even though the structural changes supporting it were gradual. The change is not binary at the structural level because what appears abrupt is the resulting change in what the system can perceive.

From the field of the “before you” that was attempting to get into the state of the new thing, the new thing maybe be unavailable to access because the necessary priors have not been learned, or because existing priors cannot yet be activated and coordinated into the needed organization. After the threshold is crossed, the new gestalt reorganizes what seems obvious, making it difficult to reconstruct why “there” was once so hard to see from “here.”

Importantly, once a new gestalt has crossed the threshold and been used repeatedly through practice, its organization can itself become a reusable chunk. Meaning, the gestalt can form relationships with other gestalts which then form a structure by which input is organized. What was previously the result of creative search then becomes one of the things available for future search. It’s like learning a new math skill that then becomes useable for learning other skills. Creativity therefore expands its own possibility space over time since a current emergent gestalt can become part of the structure supporting a future higher order gestalt.

This is the underlying mechanism Kenneth Stanley and Joel Lehman were getting at with their book ‘Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned.’ Planning an end goal requires the destination to be a branch you can already see and take. The priors and paths that would allow you to see the true end goal at the beginning are built by the journey itself, so the start is simply that: a naive way of beginning a journey that is needed to loop for refining knowledge and action. At the start, the endpoint is genuinely outside your representable space. You reach the “there” by crossing a threshold that allows you to see a new gestalt that was once unimaginable, and you can no longer quite say why it was ever hard to see from “here.”  

These core observations about novelty can help us understand the importance of constraints when creating. Moderate, well-chosen constraints boost creativity, too few cause paralysis with infinite possibility, and too many shut the system down due to not having enough slack. This parallels the edge-of-the-field zone that underlies learning and positive differences: too few constraints leaves the search space impossibly large, so there is no path cheap enough to resolve into a gestalt (paralysis), while too many minimizes the space so much that nothing can be combined into something novel; the sweet spot is where constraints minimize the space to something navigable while leaving enough slack to recombine chunks into a gestalt that wasn’t there before. There is an underlying point here that creativity is search through a possibility space within an ontological framing and the search needs structure to be feasible. Constraints prune certain branches of the load-bearing tree so enough precision can be used for action so a new combination is interpreted as meaningful. 

We can take something like play being helpful for creativity since play is accepting constraints that structure the search space of a situation while leaving enough slack for exploration of meaningful recombinations. A frame of play also enables us to place less precision on branches that would constrain our search too much, like needed validation. If we are in a state of play, we can “override” what we would normally value which can result in creative acts we otherwise would not stumble on. Play is valuable because it lowers the predicted cost of failed output without removing structure from the creative search entirely.

In essence, training data gives us available structures that can be used and organized around, the field’s propertiesm like constraintsm organize the search space, slack and play keep alternative branches in the load-bearing tree alive, and the Loop of Refinement generates and tests organizations of things. Crucially, a structural threshold is crossed when certain input becomes sufficiently stable to appear as a new gestalt, which is how we get from “here” to “there” in terms of state. As we move to novel states and gestalts, the field can represent its new organization which results in more action policies that are made available through branches of the load-bearing tree. 

5. Conflict, Control, and Suffering

5.1 Field Dissonance

There exists a mechanism that allows expression to differ from internal states. We can call this field dissonance, which occurs when conflicting properties or action policies remain active within the current field while the system must still settle on a workable organization or action. The field holds properties from multiple sources at once, like deep priors, the current bodily state, representations, and social constraints, and these can conflict with each other. Field dissonance is therefore broader than cognitive dissonance because the conflict does not need to occur between explicit beliefs. It can occur between bodily predictions, social constraints, representations, targets, and available actions.

For instance, a social constraint, which is downstream of deeper priors, could demand that you smile and say you are fine, while an embodied protective target makes withdrawal more salient. The field can hold both properties, but overt action compresses this complexity. A person may have several conflicting properties active while still having to enact one policy. When two policies require mutually exclusive actions, the system resolves the tension by acting on the more heavily weighted properties, leaving the violated ones unresolved.

The dominant policy may appear as cached such as the cliche statement of “doing good, how about you",” while the “losing” ones continue to influence expressions like posture and tone. Rather than simply disappearing, the violated property can “leak out” through automatic or involuntary behavior, making the dissonance externally observable. It can also remain active internally as somatic contraction and thoughts. This continued activity does not necessarily produce friction. Friction appears when the resulting state is more costly or difficult to integrate than predicted, while an unresolved target may otherwise persist as target error without producing a noticeable difference in felt tone. Though friction and negative felt tone is most common here. As another example of “leakage” we can take acts of lying to avoid conflict, where the story you tell stays surface-level while expressions like forced laughter and avoided eye contact expose the unintegrated target.

The weight of a property is a manifestation of precision weighting, which contributes to what gains control before an action occurs rather than just explaining the result after the fact. This is why dissonance phenomenologically feels like being right and wrong at the exact same time: one part of the field was satisfied while another part was violated through action. We can connect this to active inference and say that the field contains competing preferred states and policies, all attempting to coordinate the same limited channels of action. The conflict comes from multiple targets and demands remaining active within one changing field. 

However, resolution of conflicting properties is not limited to simply acting on the behavioral survivor. Because the system can reduce error either by changing the world through action or by updating its internal state, alternative resolution paths exist. Through weighting, one target can overpower the others. Through suppression, conflicting material can remain active while being kept away from control or awareness. The system can utilize reframing, like changing the narrative, to update the underlying model and reduce or remove the contradiction. It can resort to freezing, stopping action altogether when weights are stuck in uncertainty. Or it can try sequencing, which means separating the conflicting targets temporally, such as allowing ourselves to smile now to satisfy social priors and cry later to satisfy the embodied demand.

Another resolution method is that of integration through a structure preserving transformation. Instead of choosing to stay within a fantasy o safety for example, the system identifies the invariant properties inside the fantasy that are load-bearing and develops a policy that can be enacted in the real world that satisfies them alongside practical constraints. This reduces dissonance because conflicting target structures can partially resolve rather than compete. Integration is therefore another kind of structural threshold. Istead of selecting between existing policies to reduce conflict, the field produces a new gestalt of action that preserves important properties protected by the targets. For example, a math student struggling with an abstract proof stops choosing between rote memorization and blind guessing, instead constructing a visual geometric model that satisfies both the strict logical axioms and their need for spatial reasoning.

This can help explain why our words and behavior can diverge. It is a matter of the field reweighting its properties incredibly fast rather than two parallel fields running in the background. One major form of this is a conflict between a local target, like present comfort, and a global target, like maintaining long term health by going to the gym. A local target organizes action across a narrow horizon, while a global target coordinates outcomes across a wider span of time and context. The distinction is a gradient rather than a binary, ranging from targets that organize the next few seconds to those maintained across years.

A global target is not a property sitting in the field continuously. It is a label applied across time as the field repeatedly reinstantiates a target over a long time. In one moment you may be acting toward that goal because its relevant properties are weighted heavily, but a few moments later your actions can betray your words because the field has reconfigured toward immediate comfort. Failure to reach global targets is therefore not necessarily irrationality. It can be a local target temporarily gaining more motivational precision than the wider target.

Self control, then, is not simply overpowering parallel desires but keeping the global target alive and weighted in the field long enough to act on it. Discipline is repeatedly making the global target active and weighting it heavily enough to act on before the field defaults to a local target. For nuance, the global target should not always dominate. A long term target can be poorly calibrated, while a local target may contain information that should update it. Discipline is the ability to reliably reinstate a wider horizon target when short horizon competitors become salient, without becoming unable to revise targets when conditions change. A key goal here to cultivate discipline is to ensure the attractor states that are relevant for your goal are have high reachability. This means designing your environment and routines so that the path from your current state to the intended action requires minimal friction, while blocking off paths toward short-horizon defaults.

Attractors make conflicts asymmetrical. One target may be seriously weighted while the opposing field configuration has a wider or deeper basin. A person can consciously target calm while repeatedly returning to anxiety because the anxiety configuration is easier to reinstate and makes protective policies, like somatic contraction more reachable.

Field dissonance itself is not identical to suffering. It describes the broader situation in which active properties cannot all be satisfied together. The suffering loop begins when one of those demands becomes a highly weighted need that present experience must be different, while the preferred state remains difficult to reach.

5.2 The Suffering Loop <third order thing?>

I want to provide a powerful synthesis of ideas related to suffering to understand why emptiness can be so helpful. I will be explaining the synthesis using both theoretical ideas and examples that tie the concepts together. To make the mechanism clear, we can separate it into first order hurt, second order control, and third order contraction.

First, we start with the basic chain of experiencing some type of sensory experience. For every interpreted input, there is a predicted integration cost. Let’s give it a value of 20 here, just as a metaphor rather than a literal measurement. Then we have to actually integrate the interpreted input into our system, which is where the fit distance property comes into play because input can be far away or close to fitting into our field. Let’s say the actual integration cost is 30 instead of the predicted 20. This results in a negative difference in felt tone experienced as friction and, typically, some degree of unpleasantness.

This is first order hurt. Something may hurt, threaten a target, violate an expectation, or cost more to integrate than predicted. First order hurt can be genuinely unpleasant before the system adds any secondary interpretation or control effort. It also does not need to begin with friction. Pain, exhaustion, loss, and sensory overload can remain hurtful even when they are not surprising.

Then enters craving and aversion. If our currently most weighted target is something like “this state should not be happening,” this means there is a gap between the actual state our being is in and the system’s preferred state. This typically produces aversion, and the system may attempt to escape the current state. We can also go in the other direction toward a predicted pleasant or resonant state, where the most weighted target would be something like “I need to reach or maintain that state.” Again, a gap shows up between the actual state the system is in and the preferred state. The preferred state comes from active targets and their precision weighting, which then organizes action intended to reduce that gap. This gap is target error, although prediction error may also be involved when sensory experience differs from what the system expected.

Roughly, what we have so far is:



First order hurt + “I don’t want this” target → aversion
Absence of desired state + “I must have” target → craving

More deeply, craving and aversion can be understood as co-arising signals of the same discrepancy. Every instance of craving can contain an aversion to how things currently are, and every instance of aversion can contain a craving for a different state. When our actual state is not the preferred state, there is craving for the preferred state and aversion to the actual state. Every craving can implicitly reject the present configuration, while every aversion can implicitly want something else. They are not always phenomenologically identical, nor do they always appear together consciously, but mechanistically they can be understood as co-arising signals of the same active inference process trying to close target error.

Then comes active inference. We take action in some manner when our actual state differs from the preferred state. The system can attempt to change the world or body to match the target, change its model, or update the target itself. We can take a look at an example of being hurt in some way to understand this better. First, when we are hurt, we can leave the situation, which changes the state of the world. Second, we can suppress the sensations that are downstream of input being processed, which may change our state temporarily. Third, we can reinterpret the event, which changes the model. Fourth, we can allow the sensation without suppression, which reduces the precision of the secondary demand that the hurt must immediately disappear. These are all second order responses to first order hurt, although they are not equally useful and do not necessarily remove the original pain.

It’s important to mention somatics here and how the body plays a role. As established earlier, priors may held and stabilized through recurring patterns of muscular, visceral, vascular, and autonomic tension, which means an active target does not only predict a preferred state but prepares the body to enact it. When input violates that prediction, friction can be felt interoceptively, and the existing bodily pattern may tighten in an attempt to defend the prior. Through active inference, the body then prepares an action to change the experience.

Haptic contraction is not identical to craving or aversion, but it can be one of their somatic expressions. If sustained, the contraction keeps the target active and highly weighted while also physically reinstantiating it, which helps explain the phrase “what you resist persists.” The loop can then reinforce itself because the contraction is itself unpleasant and continues to violate the active target, producing further craving, aversion, and contraction. As a whole, craving and aversion describe the direction of the system’s control effort, while haptic contraction can be one way the associated prior is held, defended, enacted, and felt in the body.

The feedback loops becomes extra rigid when the unwanted state is highly salient and the preferred state has low reachability. The system continues weighting the target heavily while lacking a viable policy for reaching it due to barriers. This leaves the difference in preference active and  produces repeated attempts at control that fail to resolve the error:



unpleasant experience
→ “this must not be happening”
→ action policy to stop it
→ contraction
→ additional unpleasant evidence
→ stronger belief that the state is intolerable
→ stronger action to stop it



your suffering loop leans on rumination without naming it: replaying a past instance is a probe that reinstates the field configuration, which is one of the main ways an attractor gets regenerated when nothing in the environment is triggering it.

The contraction can then become evidence for the original model. A threat prediction produces contraction, the contraction is interpreted as interoceptive evidence of threat, and threat signals receive even more precision. Through repetition, the suffering loop can become an attractor state itself that repeatedly reinstates the field configuration supporting it. One common starter of this is rumination on memories, which are reconstructed as present representations, that bring about unwanted sensations, so recalling one reinstates the field configuration it came from and the body responds as if the situation is happening in the present moment. 

Reducing the gap between our actual state and preferred state through meditation is one reason why some practitioners report feeling less motivation or intensity when it comes to their desires. If we lower precision on targets that we once held heavily, we can feel a much smaller gap between the actual and preferred state, which means less pressure for active inference around those old targets. This can feel like reduced motivation because some ordinary motivation is powered by contraction around “this must happen” or “this must stop.”

However, direction and pressure should be separated. Direction comes from the target, while pressure comes from the added prediction that the target must be reached or something we will have aversion about will follow. In the long term of “good” meditation practice, what often drops is compulsive desire, not necessarily effective action. But if meditation also lowers salience too broadly or becomes disengagement, it can produce apathy rather than flexible equanimity and motivation. Equanimity leaves targets and actions available while making them less compulsory. 

It might be tempting to believe that to increase wellbeing, we should simply raise our predictions about how costly input will be to integrate so that actual costs repeatedly come in lower, producing resonance and positive felt tone. However, the predicted baseline is not a free variable we can easily manipulate in this way. As mentioned in the Energetic Triad section, physiology plays a major role here and can affect our baseline in ways outside immediate conscious control. If we try to “fake” a high expected cost, that estimate is not a genuine prediction, and knowing we inflated it collapses the surprise that would have produced resonance. If we managed to genuinely condition an expectation that everything will be costly, we would be training a prior that the world is difficult or threatening. That could lower baseline felt tone and increase prediction that things are bad rather than producing genuine wellbeing.

Increasing baseline felt tone (which is one part of wellbeing) can be done however through our own agency. One objection here is that any increase in the baseline gets absorbed and becomes the new zero all other experience gets compared against. This is a very common situation: you feel better for a while, it becomes the norm, and you end up at the exact happiness level you were at before hand. That mechanism stays consistent for things the mind-body system treats as a stable condition which stops tracking of felt tone eventually through integration into the baseline. However, at least one area where this does not stay fully consistent is that of contraction, both epistemic and haptic. Epistemic contraction here being similar to haptic, but instead it is held “in the mind” rather than “in the body.” One could argue these are the same thing, but for clarity here they are separated. As the loop above shows, contraction is regenerated moment-to-moment rather than sitting there as a concrete “fact” about the situation. So, releasing contraction is not acquiring something new that will get absorbed into the felt tone baseline like almost everything else, it is ending a cost the system was making every moment taking away from our total capacity. Just as you stop noticing when an old pain disappears, like a headache, you stop registering the increase in baseline felt tone itself, even as its absence frees up system capacity. Resources are no longer wasted maintaining the pattern, and the body-mind stops sending signals to confirm the interpretation of the pattern. This is why advanced phenomenology practitioners describe these changes as lasting and durable as well as “unremarkable” in the flashy sense, since both are true pertaining to different things.

It is important to recognize that first order hurt is a perceived solid gestalt, like “anxiety” or a “thing” that should not be happening. But on closer phenomenological inspection, the gestalt becomes a part of a greater process and can be decomposed into many different parts. Remember, a gestalt was defined as “the unified whole that appears in the present moment when parts are organized together.” This means that if we can separate the parts effectively so the gestalt no longer appears as a unified solid thing, our system can reveal many more available policies to enact like wise action. Luckily, we have emptiness on our side as a tool to investigate the processes contributing to suffering. Contemplating and realizing emptiness as a tool can liberate us both phenomenologically and epistemically by recognizing construction of things as an active process. 

6. Transparency 

6.1 Emptiness & Ontological Authority

Emptiness, or suññatā, is an extremely load bearing concept within this framework that leads to insight about time, wonder, suffering, and frankly everything. As a primer, here is what Rob Burbea has to say about emptiness: “We can, at least for now, define emptiness as the absence of this inherent existence that things appear to naturally and undeniably have” and “Emptiness then, is more a quality that we can recognize in something, a way something is, a property it has. A thing is ‘empty’ of its seemingly real, independent existence.” Emptiness means that no “thing” exists in the independent manner it appears to. Its identity and properties depend on causes, conditions, relations, and the field organizing them into a specific gestalt. I like to think of emptiness as seeing through an optical illusion and realizing that multiple interpretations can be valid as perceptual organizations, while none exist inherently within the drawings/lines themselves. The conditions and manner in which you look at the illusion determine what appears. It is empty of being one way or another. It is flexible, just like all other “things.” Emptiness can be a simple tool, like understanding that your perspective depends on your conditioning and mood, but taken to great depths, it is an amazing tool for addressing problems we think we have, like suffering.

To fit emptiness into this framework, it is important to know that it is the negative space of the model, not another component among the others, but a way in which the core components are seen from the other side, like the back of a coin compared to the front. Every component and mechanism so far, like compression, chunking, the field, gestalts, and the load-bearing tree, has been modeled to understand how “things” get constructed. Emptiness is simply telling us that the construction of “things” is just that: a construction. The constructive process runs roughly as follows: 



priors structure sensory experience into features and relations → those relations are compressed into chunks → chunks are packaged as a gestalt → precision makes the gestalt “stable” and useable

Emptiness actually reverses this process:



the solid thing is examined as a gestalt → gestalt is decompressed into its relation and parts → relations are traced back to the causes and conditions allowing it to appear like active priors

No independent thing can be found outside that process.

To see a thing as empty is to understand how the field is constructing and maintaining it. For instance, a chair is a field configuration built from co-arising conditions like shapes, relations, context, and prior knowledge. If you change the priors or their weighting, the “thing” of the chair appears differently or not at all, which is exactly what happens with the duck rabbit illusion, the unreadable image, and mathematical insight. Emptiness is not nihilism or a denial of realness since the chair remains real and useful at its level, but it is not “real” independently of the processes that make it appear and function as what is registered as chair.

Gestalt appearances do not require a thing to receive complete ontological authority. The system needs enough compression and precision for a chair to appear and function as a chair, but it does not need to treat “chair” as an inherent structure that exists independently of the field. Ontological authority is the degree to which the field treats a gestalt as fixed rather than mutable when building further structure on top of it like predictions and action. Fixed here means both that the field resists updating the gestalt under counterevidence, and that dependent components like priors and action are built on top of it. To be clear about the difference between precision weighting and ontological authority, we can say precision is how strongly the system holds a prediction, and ontological authority is how much else the system builds on a gestalt once it has emerged.

If “I am unsafe” has high ontological authority, the field still makes predictions about input, but ambiguous input is assimilated into branches associated with “I am unsafe” and lowers the precision assigned to safety evidence. An unsafe attractor state may therefore have a deep or wide basin due to high ontological authority, making alternative configurations difficult to reach. In the attractor state, only certain elements like specific priors have high ontological authority, not necessarily everything within the field configuration. However, high ontological authority does not necessarily always give way to a deep or wide basin. A thing can be authoritative within a narrow context yet disappear outside it, like a certain frame applied by choice. As a more flexible example with lower authority, a chair can appear clearly and guide the act of sitting without its “chairness” governing every interpretation. It can become a step, sculpture, or pile of wood, so its ontological authority is contextual and limited. Ontological authority differs from reification because reification concerns a construction appearing independently as “real” so it is more static, while ontological authority concerns how strongly that construction governs further perception and action. Reification is about how the gestalt appears to the system, while authority is about how the system uses it. This also connects to the process of real-making as laid out in the loop of refinement section as anything that is made real through the loop, such as religion, has high(er) ontological authority. 

Ontological authority determines how much of the field a violation has to propagate through, which is what the energetic triad measures as cost. High authority means many other priors rest on the gestalt, so revising it forces recomputation of everything built on top. A low authority gestalt can be dropped cheaply because little structure was resting on it. This helps us refine the definition of what depth implies in the load-bearing tree: a prior’s depth matches the degree to which other gestalts use it as a premise, and updating cost comes from partly how many of the dependent priors must be recomputed. This also means authority is self-maintaining because a high-authority gestalt structures ambiguous input, so that input integrates cheaply in the field and reads as confirming evidence for the active predictions while counter-input is encoded as friction to be explained away. This means resonance and friction end up reinforcing whatever already has authority rather than telling you whether it is accurate. This is why open refinement loops are important so we do not fall into the trap of treating cheap integration of input as evidence that we are right. This also means the two orientations the refinement loops take, real-making and truth-making matter: real-making raises ontological authority which makes real-made things expensive to update, and truth-making requires exposing it to external feedback the system cannot plan for. Authority also keeps integration of input cheap by pruning the search space of the load-bearing tree’s branches, so lowering it means holding open alternatives the system had stopped computing, which is why lowering authority requires slack and tends to fail when the system has little. 

Connecting this to somatics, it seems as though rigid or latched contraction is ontological authority held in the body. This is why a simple representation of safety typically doesn't work against a highly tense body. Haptic contraction gives a gestalt authority in the embodied substrate, since the contraction a prediction produces becomes interoceptive input that the predicted state is real, and interoceptive priors sit deep in the load-bearing tree so the signals are weighted heavily over most other input. Bringing slack into this, having alternative branches of input available means not latching or contracting, so there is a beginning point of using a lot of resources in order to hold hold other branches possible instead of defaulting to what the field is conditioned towards. A latch, meaning a contraction pattern held long enough to become rigid, does the same job as a premise because holding it in the body keeps one interpretation of input “sitcky” and the system can build on top of it without recomputing the held knowledge.

In terms of the compression gradient, to examine a gestalt for learning or updating we must first objectify it by making it stable and clear enough to inspect, which means compressing it from the embodied layer, what the load-bearing tree uses as its physical substrate, into a representation or a piece of notation. A gestalt that holds high ontological authority resists compression because it exists as the structure itself doing the compression rather than as an object obvious to be compressed. This is why the inverse of the constructive process is hard: the field has to make stable and inspect what it is currently using as its guiding structure. For instance, a chair decompresses easily because not much else rests on the “chair” construction, while the sense of self does not because almost everything else is built on top of the “sense of self” structure. As another instance, the grammar rules of a language of a native speaker stay hidden because those rules are the structure generating the judgment rather than content available. To describe the highly authoritative grammar rules explicitly requires the hidden structure to be objectified, which means moving from the embodied layer into notation which is essentially the loop of refinement. 

To sum up the relationships going on here: precision is how much weight or confidence a prediction is given in the current field. Ontological authority is how strongly an emergent gestalt functions as a structure for organizing further perception and action. Depth describes how many other structures depend on it across contexts, while reification describes the construction becoming invisible and appearing inherent or independently real.

precision makes the gestalt stable and useable → other inference starts treating it as structure, which is authority → dependence accumulates across contexts, which is depth → the construction stops being noticed at all, which is reification.

If we question what is or was high ontological authority, we have the chance to break through our current way of seeing things which is typically the core target of novel discoveries. This is exactly what Grothendieck meant here in this quote: 



But let us return to the dream, and to the prohibition that has been imposed on mathematics for thousands of years. This is perhaps the most inveterate of all the preconceptions, often implicit and rooted in habit, which decrees that such and such a thing is “maths” and such other things not. It took millenniums before things as childish and ubiquitous as the groups of symmetries of certain geometric figures, the topological forms of some others, the number zero and the sets found admission into the sanctuary! When I talk to students about the topology of a sphere, and the shapes that can be deduced from a sphere by adding handles - things that don’t surprise young children, but which confuse the students because they think they know what “maths” is - the first spontaneous echo I get is: but this is not maths! Maths, of course, means the Pythagorean theorem, the heights of a triangle and second degree polynomials... These students are no more stupid than you or me, they react as all the mathematicians in the world have reacted from time immemorial until today, except for people like Pythagoras or Riemann and maybe five or six others. Even Poincaré, who was not the first to come along, managed to prove by a well-felt philosophical A plus B that infinite sets were not maths! Surely there must have been a time when triangles and squares were not maths - they were drawings that children or pottery craftsmen traced on the sand or in the clay of vases, so as not to make mistakes...

To be clear, Grothendieck is speaking moreso of categories and frames here like, “When I talk to students about the topology of a sphere…” but categories can just as easily be gestalts as an item like a chair can be. The core difference is in legibility: categories are not typically well defined in the notational sense while items, like chairs, are obviously “there.” Again this is because in the structure the thing is difficult to see clearly: categories and frames are operating as the structure itself which makes it harder to objectify than an object “out there” like a chair. 

Emptiness as a tool lowers ontological authority not by preventing gestalts from appearing, but by revealing that their authority is made from causes and conditions, and it is mutable. We can use emptiness wisely to develop a more fluid state of being in which we hold more capacity for moving between states. This reduces suffering and increases the potential for intelligence by widening the range of field configurations that remain reachable. Constructional transparency, a property of a field configuration in relation to particular gestalts, is the practical capacity to maintain this recognition while keeping structuring gestalts alive, allowing them to guide experience without becoming a rigid structure that everything else has to be recomputed against. To be alive, to survive as a human, we must structure sensory input somehow, so some things need high ontological authority. The key question is not “should we stop giving authority to things” but rather, "what should we give authority to, how much, and in what contexts."

6.2 Transparency 

As mentioned before, transparency is the degree to which the processes producing a gestalt remain apparent in experience, on all three layers of the compression gradient, while the gestalt is still being experienced and used. This is not to be confused with phenomenal transparency, which is the invisibility of a model’s construction. Transparency is hard when ontological authority is high because a gestalt that other structures are built on top of is what the field is currently running on rather than something the field can perceive as an object. Reification is the “extreme” case of this: construction of a thing is not seen at all and is taken to be inherent to reality. Transparency is not preventing all structure; we still need that to survive and go about our day. It is preventing structure from becoming hidden so we can see through what other interpretations of input can offer. Transparency becomes practical then rather than theoretical because it offers us paths of freedom in perception we would not usually have due to branches in the load-bearing tree opening up to us.

Transparency is about redistributing precision in helpful ways. We can move precision in the load-bearing tree from the whole of the gestalt towards its constituent parts in its construction process. This means the whole loses its high authority status without fully being removed from experience. This leads to ontological authority and organization of experience coming apart. A gestalt can keep organizing experience while still remaining highly transparent because what goes away is those structures resting on it as a given so the dependency relation loosens. Once a gestalt stops being treated as a fixed structure, it still integrates input at the same cost, if not less, so its usefulness was never coming from its authority level. If we look back on the constructive process and its inverse, it is clear transparency is the practical version of decompressing a gestalt into relations and tracing them to conditions. If we want to use this inverse process successfully, reification must drop so the internal workings can start to be seen through rather than as invisible. This leads to the deconstruction process leaving more branches open in the load-bearing tree due to components becoming separately addressable, so each one suggests paths the original gestalt left out of reach. 

Crucially when trying to achieve high transparency, this can be difficult due to perspective limitations. A high authority gestalt is what the field is running on for structure, so naturally trying to see through it will be like trying to see your own eyes. Transparency then in some cases becomes an achievement of getting around perspectival limitations. Holding alternative organizations of experience costs resource capacity like attention, which means we need a sufficient level of slack. Transparency can fail under conditions like fatigue and threat due to this if it is not conditioned in the field enough. Take for example trying to decompress the parts of high anxiety when in a deep attractor basin. You can know the “correct” move in this case, but seeing it as transparent is too costly from the system’s perspective. This also tells us why depth of priors and gestalts predict difficulty to see as transparent: trunk-level structure resists change because almost everything is built on top. Another problem of gaining transparency is context-sensitivity in which ontological authority can drop in one context while staying high in another, which is one reason why you need multiple passes of the refinement loop. Like with high anxiety states, one context can make the state authoritative enough that transparency does not occur, while in another the same components separate easily.

Another important aspect about cultivating transparency deals with the depth of the gestalt you are attempting to recognize as transparent instead of authoritative. We can view experience across a ladder of perspectives, where each step down reveals a layer of construction that is progressively harder to see through. For instance if we take the high level anxiety state, at the top the gestalt might be something like “these sensations are scary” and seeing that as transparent just means deconstructing the sensations themselves. Farther below that sits something like “these sensations are happening to me” which is a threat prior that made the interpretation of sensations feel highly authoritative in the first place. Making this gestalt transparent changes every situation in which similar sensations occur, not just this one. We can go all the way down the ladder until we hit something like thing-ness itself or innate priors. Each level is harder to see through as a construction because more is built on it which makes updating proportionally costly. Depth of the gestalt being targeted predicts both the difficulty and the generality of the change through transparency. Whether repeated practice at high levels loosens deeper structures through propagation or simply builds the clarity and slack needed for direct low-level work is unclear; either way, working with high-level structure matters.

When it comes to somatics, as stated earlier, latched contraction associated with the trunk of the load-bearing tree is high ontological authority held in the body. Because of this, epistemic transparency without bodily tension release allows the reified gestalt to propagate back through the system, as interoceptive evidence remains structured by the very thing being deconstructed. This means holdings transparency and releasing bodily contraction are processes that end up with similar results, since both lower the precision and embodied reinforcement keeping a prior stable. However, this mechanism only applies to contraction that has not been held long. If contraction has been held long enough in the body, the range of patterns the mind-body system can produce and manage becomes narrowed and stays that way. At that point, releasing the contraction and restoring the patterns that were closed off becomes two different processes, not just one. Meaning, releasing deep, latched contraction that has been in the system for a long time (exact time unknown) does not mean you will automatically regain the patterns that were lost due to that stuck contraction. An analogous metaphor here is like a joint stuck in a cast: short term you remove the cast and the joint moves again, long enough and the literal tissue changes, so producing the range of the joint again means practice. This implies that a collapsed load-bearing tree due to deep, latched haptic contraction produces reachability constraints to other field configurations. So, you might be able to “see” through a deep prior that is “stuck,” but other field configurations will be unreachable because the body cannot supply the state the configuration needs. This also increases the importance of slack for deep change because we need to inch ourselves closer to the states we want to be in that were once unreachable through repetition in small amounts until the body can handle them.

Expanding on the importance of sensory clarity, it seems as though sensory clarity is transparency viewed through an embodied lens: de-chunking a sensation gestalt by resolving it into changing lower-level sensations. This is why sensory clarity reduces suffering according to the energetic triad, because once a sensation stops functioning as an authoritative structure, less of the field has to be organized around it, so the ongoing cost of integrating input lessens and the negative felt tone decreases. 

And in general this is why transparency reduces suffering. The suffering loop as laid out in section 5.2 runs on first order hurt appearing as a gestalt that carries high authority which organizes the field around it and its subsequent branches like beliefs that the hurt should not be happening. This then leaves control, craving or aversion, as the only available action policy to be enacted to remove that target error. Once the construction of the gestalt is revealed, less of the field has to be organized around it, so the ongoing integration cost of input falls, and each part of the gestalt branches out separately in the load-bearing tree, so there are more action policies available.  

Making transparency of structure obvious can make certain field configurations more reachable by opening paths the constructed gestalt closed. When a gestalt the configuration depended on loses enough ontological authority, other contending branches in the load-bearing tree stay active as available paths rather than being pruned. This then weakens the attractor basin holding the current field configuration as being stable by reducing depth and width. The conditions for exiting the basin become easier to meet due to barriers being dropped and more options being open, so the asymmetry between entering and exiting the state is reduced. Transparency does not completely remove the basin though since a configuration is also instantiated by other things like bodily states, environment, and attention which can all keep reinstating it. 

Importantly when training transparency, it should be made clear that the level of the thing you are targeting to recognize as being transparent matters a great deal. Meaning, the hierarchical position of the structure you are attempting to see the construction of determines if it will actually have a meaningful impact or not. If we decide to make gestalts transparent that sit on the same level in the load-bearing tree, we would end up working towards little. Let’s say the gestalt of “this anxiety is dangerous” is seen as constructed and its inner workings are made obvious which reduces its ontological authority status; no longer will it organize the field to its liking. However, there’s a high chance that the gestalt of “this frustration is unbearable” will simply “take over” that spot because they lie on very similar levels in the load-bearing tree. By remaining shallow, they both do not have a deep organizational impact on experience, so the chance transparency effects dependent structure that lies on top of those gestalts is low. Now, that is assuming the transparency insights simply stay at those high levels, but because transparency targets their construction, it is not uncommon for deeper changes to propagate deeper in the load-bearing tree; these types of changes however will take longer to occur rather than just the shallow gestalt becoming transparent. If we spend enough time developing holding transparency around these high-level gestalts, there is a chance the insights will propagate downwards and reach lower-level gestalts like “there are solid sensations.” However, if this happens, and if high-level gestalts are simply “replaced” when they no longer have high ontological authority, then it would make sense to target low-level gestalts that have a much more profound impact on organizing the field. This way, we can make bigger changes faster which have the potential to allow us to move between states with more freedom. Intuitively, the system would need to be holding a sufficient amount of slack here for these changes to be optimal instead of destabilizing, which is why moving downwards at a steady pace can be helpful instead of simply only targeting the lowest level gestalt we can. 

It seems as though “thing-ness” is the deepest gestalt we can practically target to hold as transparent within our system without becoming completely destabilized. Crucially, thing-ness is simply a construction like all other gestalts, meaning its parts and interactions matter in the way we target it to hold it as transparent. Its construction seems to be made up of processes like input being segmented into bounded units, those units cohering as single things, and their persistence across a seemingly continuous temporal slice. If we can lower the ontological authority of thing-ness, we have a chance at reducing a massive amount of suffering due to almost every other gestalt depending on it, so the core action policy of craving and aversion loses the structure it was using as a substrate and that policy stops being the only available response. In terms of the energetic triad, holding thing-ness as transparent means the field no longer has to hold it as a high authority structure, so the ongoing cost of integrating input falls, and an update pertaining to any particular gestalt that depends on thing-ness propagates less through the system, so negative differences in felt tone shrink. This also changes what resonance and friction are signals of, since they now keep track of integrating input into a field with more available branches which makes the surprise of resonance or friction propagates through less structure in the system so the difference in experience is smaller. In terms of the baseline of experience, the background felt tone rises because less resources go to holding reified things, and because fewer of the load-bearing tree’s targets are ones that the world cannot fulfill, like permanence, so there is less error and hurt in experience. A situation is then held in the body as something with moving parts rather than as one solid thing that has to be enacted, which is why the felt shift becomes available more often due to more pathways being opened up by differentiating the solid thing.

(Credit to Roger Thisdell’s A Very Streamlined Enlightenment Protocol for first introducing me to the concept of targeting low-level priors)

To be clear, holding transparency around gestalts does not mean the way you model things is now more truthful or corresponds with reality. Something can be seen as constructed yet still be wrong, which is why open loops of refinement are necessary. However, transparency does make the loop itself easier as it clears barriers that would normally prevent updating. Objectification is a step in the loop, since we have to clearly isolate the thing first to inspect it, and high ontological authority blocks this by making the thing the structure itself instead of an object. Once we can successfully inspect the parts a gestalt depends on, we can run the loop on those which means resonance and friction can pertain to deeper, more fundamental priors which means more things on top get updated. This helps undercut the self-maintenance problem of gestalts confirming themselves through regeneration of contraction. This is analogous to the sensory clarity skill in meditation: seeing conditions of a high authority gestalt is like seeing how bodily sensations are made up, through input becoming more differentiated over time. This is why blurriness, the opposite of sensory clarity and transparency causes suffering: it regenerates the conditions of the substrate giving way to the suffering in the first place. 

As for failure modes like depersonalization and derealization, the conditions leading to these states are complex and I’m not sure if there is any one algorithmic formula to understand how they work, but what is obvious is that these types of states are not about holding transparency, but over-reifying things. The structures organizing experience are still given high authority, there is just a disconnection between certain ones that matter to the system like “real-ness” and “self-hood” through the loss of interoceptive evidence. I believe states like DP/DR are configurations we enter into when we do not carry enough slack for our system to handle the way it is organizing experience. This is why releasing contraction and “surrendering” to the state can be helpful: it stops the system from spending resources on the policy that the state be different, which is the second-order control keeping the state up in the first place. That freed capacity through the dropping of contraction is what the field needs to hold the state without trying to suppress it. 

{we can actually model transparency as like going from one singular pipeline to like a y branch or somwethign in the load-bearing tree}

Lastly, developing transparency should not lead to paralysis. A system holding wise and genuine transparency can still compress things and commit to singular interpretations. Nothing about recognizing construction of gestalts should require holding every alternative branch open, which would be costly and useless. The difference is that the system can update when context changes, rather than being stuck with whatever field configuration it started with. Acting firmly while and holding a gestalt with low ontological authrotiy are not in tension, since compression is what makes action possible and transparency makes updating possible. Think of someone who holds a model so close and tightly that nothing can penetrate it, like a flat-earther. Than action may be firm but in no way can they enter the loop of reifnement oriented torwards truth-making whichn would allow them to see the belief as transparent. This is often what happens as we age and continue to hold rigid models about how things work. 

This points to what the important target is in this framework: ontological authority is not the problem itself, it is authority that the system cannot see clearly and drop. Giving a gestalt high authority but being able to drop it in the next moment is what kids do when they play, what adults do when thinking with specific frames, and what phenomenological practices can do when propagating feelings of safety down into the system on purpose. All of these moves depend on the system’s ability to treat a thing as given, then stop on command. The core question then becomes what to grant authority to and when. This is how sanity and wisdom come to matter. 

7. Sanity, Wisdom, and Practice

7.1 Sanity & Wisdom

Now that we have established that any gestalt can in principle be held transparently, that they are made of causes and conditions no different from optical illusions, it is now a question of how to use this insight wisely. What exactly should we be moving towards? This of course is a highly debated topic already, for example what ethics argues about, but I want to give the answer that best fits this framework. 

First we must differentiate between sanity and wisdom as they work together to provide maximal wellbeing and flourishing. Sanity is defined as holding gestalts with enough compression to take action and enough transparency to update, while having the ability to move between levels of abstraction when the situation calls for it. Wisdom is that same skill oriented towards targets that are worth weighting and whose subsequent actions matter. Sanity is a prerequisite for wisdom rather than something that sits next to it, as you cannot select targets freely from inside a field you have no clear view of. 

Here are two quotes that capture the essence of these tools:



“[Consciousness of abstracting can be defined as] awareness that in our process of abstracting we have left out characteristics”
— Alfred Korzybski





Sanity = Lucidly climbing up & down the branches of abstraction

A big source of confusion I spot in people is when they get stuck attending to reality at stifling levels of abstraction; either by going too complex or too simple, and not being able to scale to a different level when that would get them out of a confuddlement.

“Everything is Yin and Yang! No, it’s all One! No, it’s neither, it’s non-dual! But my five family members are coming over for dinner and I need to remember to buy 14 items at the supermarket, and calculate my taxes. And my brother is probably going to bring up politics which is so overwhelming complicated! But it’s all One...”

So many minds get stuck trying to figure out the forest, when maybe what they really need to do is look at the individual trees. Or sometimes they need to get down to ground level and pick through the dirt, or sometimes take a bird’s eye view of the canopy, except they’re stuck or don’t realise everything else has moved on.

The mind chunks and fuses reality all the time - you may have noticed. But how much have you really noticed, and how much flexible control do you have over this process? At least become lucid of it.

I have found that by recognizing this feature of mind, and becoming better at navigating strata of abstraction, my sense of clarity and sanity has gone way up.

Great spiritual development is to be able to nimbly both reach lower on the levels of abstractions - all the way to zero* - AND be able to hold onto higher and higher levels of abstraction, extending our consciousness capacity, before we project to infinity (aka ‘more than I can make sense of’).

— Roger Thisdell

The abstraction ladder operates as a continuous hierarchical spectrum across experience, acting as an interface for different levels of the load-bearing tree. Moving up the ladder is an act of compression by dropping details into high-level patterns which collapses a dense data set. This is seeing the entire forest out of many trees or concluding that “it’s all one.” The inverse is decompression; moving down the ladder. This is decompressing a summary into its working parts and details, like seeing the individual branches of all the trees in a forest. Neither end of the ladder is superior since they both come with trade-offs depending on the context. Compression gives you strategic orientation, while decompression gives you specific available action. You can see this pattern in goals: the goal is the high-level global goal-post that orients attention and the details of the goal are what set the actions to get there. The main failure mode here is getting stuck at a point in the ladder. When the mind-body system loses the fluid capacity to move between levels, confusion and suffering sets in and we try to navigate certain contexts with the wrong level of compression in mind, like getting buried in details when all we need is a high-level overview. 

However, flexibility by itself does not guarantee sanity, since a mind-body system can be infinitely flexible within a closed or limited field. When a gestalt becomes large enough to structure every piece of input, it creates a rigid loop where every piece of counter-evidence is integrated as confirmation instead of error. Inside this closed loop, we receive lots of internal resonance because our interpretations feel right due to rigid structuring. However, resonance is a signal of unexpected lower integration cost; it says nothing of the system’s level of corresponding to reality. This is why sanity depends on open loops. Without constraints that the field could not have produced on its own, there is no way to tell a model that corresponds with reality from one that has simply become good at integrating everything. Sanity then fails in two ways: through rigid contraction around a single level of abstraction, or through too much flexibility with no external feedback that only provides resonance. 

You can see these mechanics play out in how a child integrates something they lack the accurate priors for. Without the average adult-filtered gestalt to compress input into, children reach for whatever priors they do have, often animate and relational, and build a representation out of them. 

Take this monologue from a child in this short-form video:



Why do humans skip rocks?

I think that the river likes games. Not big games, just tiny ones. 

You throw a bad rock, it eats it. You throw a good one, it lets it skip a few times… like it’s decising like no, noo, maybe, no, yes!

You think you’re throwing rocks but really you’re asking the river a question and every time it answer.

The first time I heard this I was amazed, because what adult is ever going to come up with that kind of metaphor? Adults have the compressed and factually correct dead gestalts that explain rock skipping like angles and spin. The child’s version does not correspond with reality, but it is genuinely “alive” and an act of construction reaching across a large fit-distance to build a vivid gestalt, mapping the felt sense of the event onto the embodied, animate priors they have stored. This is the same metaphor mechanism described earlier in the essay, but it is coming from a less-pruned, less conditioned field so it takes a different orientation.

I think the optimal state of maturity for an adult is to be like a Wise Old Child" Vacha states on Twitter. This is the mature form of childlike wonder. A child sees things as more empty without trying, because their field has not yet solidified into "solid" reifications, which is why so many things feel fresh and vivid and new. The average adult's world feels solid and dull because heavy conditioning has compressed things into stable, cheap to integrate, reified chunks. Interestingly, psychedelics have similar results through loosening rigid deep priors, just as seeing emptiness and releasing contraction does. As Michael Pollan puts it, "My own gut is there is something childlike about the consciousness at certain stages in the trip. Just judging from the experience of having had a kid, that there are levels of sensitivity to environment that children have that we lose as adults because of our desire to focus. We shut things out." Contemplative practice is partly a deliberate return to that newness, not by having fewer priors like the child, but by holding them in expansion rather than contraction: keeping them as light as a feather but as useful as a sharp knife, seeing their emptiness while keeping the competence.

However, this sanity is not yet wisdom. We may describe children as whimsical and full of life, but not wise. So what are they missing? Orientation towards targets the moment calls for. 

Take for example a conman. A conman that scams people can be sane; they can read a situation well, move between states flexibly, and update when they need to. But, we would not call this wise. Flexibility alone does not choose the right targets we should be oriented towards. Wisdom adds this orientation, like which targets are actually worth weighting, and what outcomes matter to the field. 

What makes this hard to fully state is that wisdom does not seem to be algorithmic. When we find someone, an action, or notation wise, we do not apply that characteristic because they are enacting some set of rules that follows the right guidelines. This is frankly impossible in this framework from all angles because a formula would depend on set conditions like the context and people involved which means they would have to be fixed, but that’s of course not how things are. Wisdom seems closer to something known through negation, like good health, which is not something added on top of experience, but what remains once contraction preventing flexibility is released. Here, you do not acquire wisdom by just adopting the correct model, you understand it by releasing the contraction and reified structure that were blurring what you could see and do when in other states. Wittgenstein's point applies here too, since the target(s) of wisdom cannot be fully compressed into a statement of what it is and what it is aiming towards. This makes negation easier to describe wisdom because it is side-stepping the direct compression needed to put its felt sense into words. This is because holding gestalts as transparent that we must use for structure is a difficult task due to an apparent “invisible” quality. It might be said wisdom is reflexive, only known after-the-fact through examination of the person-environment system, but distortion is still needed to be dropped for it to be cultivated and used in the first place. However, just because some contraction is dropped does not mean wisdom is guaranteed. This is the case of the spiritual leader who has ethical scandals or acts in such a way that it is obvious some amount of blockage is still present in their field preventing their load-bearing tree from containing what  . 

Lastly, the targets of wisdom matter. Removing targets from the field is impossible and not the goal of wisdom. From the suffering loop, direction comes from the target while pressure comes from the second-order control we believe we must enact to prevent something intolerable. Wisdom here has the ability to keep the direction but drop the pressure, which makes action decisive without a single action policy feeling necessary. Equanimity here comes into play as a side effect by leaving targets and actions available without making them feel necessary. Apathy contrasts this by dropping salience across the field so nothing feels worth targeting. Wisdom and equanimity work together by leaving more options alive for the mind-body system to enact well-calibrated policies by dropping pressure.

Practically, the most useful model here is running the loop of refinement with transparency underlying its process. When a gestalt forms, you can recognize it as a construction and which level of abstraction it sits at like being too detailed or too broad. You can attempt to check how much the field is building on top of it and what alternatives that is preventing you from seeing. You can act from a level of compression knowing the advantages and disadvantages for climbing the layers. And you can question what in the field needs revising like precision, the model itself, the targets, or the action policies. Then all of it can run again, iterating on itself. Crucially, there is no global endpoint here, there never is to insight, both in terms of wisdom and intellctual capacity. This is because holding a structure as transparent eventually ends up as the default in the field, which then eventually becomes transparent in its own turn. Each structure you see through becomes available to be objectified for the next round, so each iteration of refinement updates what is reachable in the field by expanding the space of what is available as interpretation and action rather than shrinking availabilities. The priors underlying all of this are not thrown away, just reinstantiated moment-to-moment. This is why holding different levels of the load-bearing tree as transparent matters, as it enables you to change your relationship to the structure rather than just sitting on the same insights.

All of this is driven by a recognition that felt senses can be wrong, both friction and resonance. Resonance only deals with the field you are working with, so a field that can integrate everything without question will feel right even when it has stopped corresponding to reality and complexity. Sanity and wisdom is not about arrive at a singular, correct representation or field. It is understanding that flexibility and feedback are crucial to increase well-being and our intellectual capacity, and knowing that maps are never the territory, but they can still be accurate.
