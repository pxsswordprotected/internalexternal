I use “cost” here in a relative sense, not as a measure of calories burned. At any moment, the brain has limited processing capacity and attention to go around, and different possible interpretations of what you’re perceiving compete for resources. “Cost” means how much of that limited capacity an input demands and how much additional capacity is used to process it.

Information costs energy, which is why the amount we can spend is finite. This is also why compression is a necessary mechanism our system uses, not one that is done by choice; an agent that would try to track and memorize everything would die before it could capture what it's after.

Our system runs on a finite budget of processing capacity and spends much of it maintaining a stable, workable interpretation of ongoing input. For every input, the system estimates how much processing it will require and then registers how much capacity it actually demands. This is always happening in experience because sensory input is constant. Here, processing refers broadly to the resources used to handle an input, while integrating it means the field reconfiguring so that the input is accounted for, a process that subsequently produces a certain felt sense if deviations are consciously noticeable.&#x20;

Valence correlates with the gap or difference between the field's configuration and what predictions it holds, weighted by precision. Most priors (which are the substrate for active predictions) update when they do not correspond with reality, so small differences fade. Targets, a sub-category of predictions, are different. A target is a preferred state the system holds and works to reach by varying its means, instead of revising the state when reality is different. Target errors therefore persist until the target is reset by a higher-level target, loosens, or is dropped. Targets are constrained by levels, meaning a target like "survival at all costs" will constrain sub-targets, as discussed in the bow-tie architecture.&#x20;

And when this cost to integrate input is actually different from the one predicted, a spike in the body and valence is felt. Put simply, this is integration-cost prediction error. For instance, imagine glancing at a math problem. At first, it may appear simple and you may think you know how to solve it. However, as time goes on, the input becomes harder and harder to integrate as you don't have the necessary prerequisites in place. This will generally show up as negative spikes in the body and in valence.

This gives us three possible outcomes that can be extrapolated into a triad: input that is cheaper to integrate than predicted is correlated with positive valence and resonance, input that is integrated about as expected is neutral (fit-baseline), and input that costs more to integrate than expected is correlated with negative valence and friction.

Some practical proxies for whether an input is high cost to integrate are time (rereading or contemplating a passage), effort (using “extra focus”), and bodily tension (like a furrowed brow). For an input that is low cost to integrate, some proxies include immediacy (clicks fast), freed attention (gained capacity for more input), and immediate tension release (relaxation).

Input that threatens a heavily weighted target is expensive to integrate, even when it is easy to perceive. For instance, if you saw a lion in the wild, this would be massively threatening to your well-being, clashing against targets dealing with "survival." So although the actual gestalt of "lion" is perceived quickly and cleanly, the organizing gestalt is weighted by its threat to heavily weighted targets, and the field reorganizes itself relative to that threat.

The fit-baseline rests at the level of the background felt tone or valence, the average level experience lies at, which is set by multiple factors like physiology and how much capacity the system has. This capacity is bottlenecked by held contraction and by the pushing away or pulling towards targets the system cannot satisfy (later expanded on through taṇhā). These are target gaps, which do not fade the way surprises in "regular" prediction do.

As a helpful metaphor, we might describe this whole thing as the ocean. The background felt tone is the level at which the ocean sits, while the fit-baseline is the surface resting at that level. A swell up is when input is cheaper to process than predicted and a down trough (or an imagined "reverse wave") is when input comes in more costly to process than predicted. Crucially, a singular wave of the same size will hit different levels based on the ocean level that day. This represents that the same insight that is processed less costly than predicted can be experienced either mundane one day or exciting another day due to the background felt tone sitting at different levels. 

Input is a constant stream and most of it integrates near its predicted cost, so these baseline-level integrations produce a cumulative embodied tone of fit at whatever level the floor sits at. Fit-baseline is where the trunk of the dependency tree structures incoming input without major surprises in either direction, and it is what differences are compared against. A fully predictable input that is also predicted to be cheap to integrate, like elevator music, produces little or no difference and sinks into the fit-baseline as mundane. A negative difference occurs when an input costs more to integrate than predicted, often because it cannot be compressed into a single known gestalt, so the system spends extra resources parsing it. A positive difference occurs when input that is expected to be costly, perhaps because of its perceived richness or complexity, instead fits neatly into the current field configuration as a known gestalt and integrates more cheaply than predicted. In cases of fast perception, like seeing an object, this can happen within milliseconds. Slower forms of this also exist like going over a math problem over minutes. You can see this pattern across aesthetic experiences, like a painting, or sensations in the body.

The size of any spike compared to the fit-baseline depends on where in the tree the affected priors sit. A branch further out on the tree (i.e., not dealing with foundational priors) is cheap to change and hardly registers as an event, while violations that occur deeper in the tree affect deep priors and force changes to everything else that depends on them, resulting in a much larger and more obvious felt tone difference. Hence why some surprises or changes in experience feel trivial while others feel like deep fragmentation. This is why shame can hurt so badly, since it affects deep priors dealing with the sense of self and security, such as "I am bad," which produces many discrepancies in the integration of input, even if low-level. Note that outer branches are where most updating happens (which can have cascading effects toward the trunk), whereas deep priors, which are active in nearly every moment and rarely violated, contribute to much of our ongoing feeling tone, mostly through steady fit-baseline integration with occasional spikes.

The main measurement we can use here is compression progress, the rate at which integration cost is reduced relative to a target. We can model this in three broad ways: too easy, productive struggle, and too hard. Of course, these slopes do not always appear in such simple forms; they are just practical proxies for measuring feeling tone over time. The "too easy" signal tracks experiences in which there is little integration cost left to reduce, because input compresses into dominant, low-cost gestalts, which typically results in boredom and a mundane feel. The "productive struggle" signal tracks experiences in which input starts as costly and uncertainty remains, but we are actively able to compress it into known organizations. If cost falls steadily, this can result in curiosity, flow, or aesthetic pleasure. Interestingly, there seems to be a "Goldilocks Effect" in cognitive science and learning in which people disengage from sequences that are either too predictable or too surprising. The "Eighty-Five Percent Rule" demonstrates something very similar in which "there is a sweet spot in which training is neither too easy nor too hard, and where learning progresses most quickly." Lastly, the "too hard" signal tracks experiences in which integration cost does not decrease over time or even increases, because no structured compression emerges and specificity cannot be iterated towards, so unresolved input accumulates into tension. This commonly results in frustration or overwhelm.

Crucially, compression progress is always relative to a target, like finishing a problem set soon or becoming a mathematician over years, which operate across different time slices. An activity is not by itself too easy or too hard; it is evaluated relative to what the system is currently trying to accomplish through its heavily weighted targets. For example, solving a problem set in a math textbook may feel like little immediate progress while still feeling meaningful based on the long-term target of becoming a mathematician. Likewise, a more difficult problem may feel locally productive while seeming irrelevant to a larger target, like learning side branches in math that aren’t necessary for becoming a mathematician. Several targets can remain active at once, and precision weighting determines which of their progress signals contributes most strongly to the feeling tone of the moment. When these targets align, short-term work can inherit meaning from longer-term targets. When they conflict, the same activity can produce mixed feelings. Felt tone in any moment is therefore shaped by compression-rate signals across several nested targets, with deeper, more heavily precision-weighted targets carrying greater weight and tending to dominate feeling tone. In this way, felt tone of experience over time is a signal of whether integration cost is falling as quickly as the field predicts relative to its active targets.

This cost-prediction model is not meant to claim that the brain literally reports exact metabolic cost in experience. It is more like a phenomenological synthesis of predictive processing, interoception, allostasis, processing fluency, and aesthetic prediction error. The Free Energy Principle models perception and action as processes organized around reducing prediction error. Active inference and allostatic models then help inform this synthesis through bodily regulation, where we predict and manage our physiological needs rather than reacting after the fact.&#x20;

The felt tone of this triad is heavily modulated by allostasis, which is the body's proactive regulation of its own resources: "Efficient regulation \[of the body] requires anticipating needs and preparing to satisfy them before they arise." Other modulators at play include the state of the nervous system, stress hormones like cortisol, and inflammation, which are all processed into the field through interoception, which is "the process by which the nervous system senses, interprets, and integrates signals originating from within the body, providing a moment-by-moment mapping of the body's internal landscape across conscious and unconscious levels." Another well-supported finding is that inflammation can produce sickness-like behavior such as fatigue, minimal activity, social withdrawal, and a depressed mood. Relatedly, heavy stress can bias perception and attention toward potential threat, which causes feedback loops of threat-detection. This means a drained or threatened body with little slack has lower capacity to handle complexity and interprets more inputs as costly to integrate before any specific input is received.

What can modulate the background felt tone is how much capacity is already used before input is integrated, which includes capacity spent holding contraction and running targets about experience, like this thing should be different. By releasing held contraction and lowering the precision on targets, we can gain capacity and raise the background felt tone, the way chronic stress and bad health lower it. Put simply, the background felt tone partly reflects the brain's expectations about bodily state and available resources.

Importantly, these expectations can include long-horizon targets, not just short-term bodily needs. A future target is held in the current field as an expectation that present actions are moving the system toward it, and the body can regulate differently depending on whether that target feels achieved, achievable, or threatened. This helps explain why someone can feel a sense of deep okay-ness while doing difficult work if the system trusts the current actions to reach the long-term target, or remain tense when feeling pleasure if a deeper target still feels unachieved. In this sense, feeling tone is partly about whether the system models its highly weighted targets as sufficiently handled, not just pleasure. Handled here can mean changing the world to reach the target, changing the action(s) that are supposed to reach the target, or simply changing the target itself.

Every action taken to handle a target is a bet on what the actual target is. So if I leave a relationship because I felt as though I would be happier outside of it, this behavior is acted out to close the gap between my current situation and what I want, what I predict to be better for my well-being. If the prediction error is misidentified, the action closes a proxy while the real gap stays open. The clearest sign is the same dissatisfaction reappearing in a new setting, keeping invariant sets of errors within the dependency tree.

Beyond physiology, background felt tone shifts durably only when persistent target gaps change. Acquiring material items, for instance, closes a target gap that soon becomes a prediction, so the increase in valence fades. Loosening a target (placing less precision on it), or closing a gap that had never faded, like ending chronic pain, removes a durable cost to the system, so the increase in valence stays.

Luckily, we have agency over a portion of this physiological aspect of the background felt tone since it is not exactly fixed. The things that are so far best studied to help are exercise, sleep, morning light, slow breathing, humming, and dancing, but I suspect the literature is far behind what is possible by an individual, so I would recommend not constraining yourself to what the current "science says." For instance, much of the literature on meditation pertains to novices or relatively inexperienced practitioners, and advanced practitioners/states are understudied, which is a limitation when trying to understand the upper limits of human agency in this area. If you were to experiment, finding ways to reduce background processing demand or increase the system's available capacity, whether by releasing held contraction (more on this below), loosening highly weighted priors, or supporting the body so it has greater physiological and psychological capacity, would be a helpful guiding principle.
