One must start with error and lead it over into truth.
That is, one must uncover the source of the error; otherwise hearing the truth is useless to us. It cannot enter as long as something else occupies its place.
To convince someone of the truth, it is not enough to state the truth; one must find the path from error to truth.
I must again and again dive beneath the water of doubt.

Ludwig Wittgenstein, Remarks on Frazer’s Golden Bough

We have two facts that make refinement necessary: resonance does not guarantee factual correctness, and notation is always lossy because it compresses knowledge as it moves up the layers. An internal representation can feel right but still not correspond with reality. Understanding therefore cannot be a single act of finding the right expression; it must be part of a greater whole, something like a loop of refinement.

The felt sense of “I understand this” is not enough. Take educational YouTube videos, which have become a tragedy of modern learning. A viewer may have a sense of understanding the thing, yet if they were to write an essay on what they learned or understood, the paper might remain mostly blank. This is why the testing effect is so important. Because of cognitive limitations, we must find a loop to ensure our understanding is being correctly iterated upon and tested for correspondence with reality. This active iteration is one central mechanism of learning.

This loop tests three things that are important for understanding: fit, coherence, and truth. Fit is how well a probe satisfies the current field, coherence is how well your model holds together internally, and truth is correspondence with reality. Accurate correspondence means something like the way you model the problem being in accordance with the actual shape of the thing, which gives intended/wanted feedback from reality. This requires keeping standard nuances in mind like framing effects and the fact that the map is never the territory, etc. 

There are two ways of running the loop, either open or closed. A closed loop tests a representation using properties already available within the system, like when thinking about an idea on a walk by yourself. An open loop introduces information or constraints from outside the current model, like working with a tutor to learn a subject. To note, just because you are running an open loop does not mean you are inviting correspondence with reality. Someone like a flat-earther could indeed be running an open loop but arrive at obviously false claims. Open loops make external correction possible, but do not guarantee it. What improves the chance of moving toward correspondence with reality is a complex combination of accurate feedback, falsifiable probes, beliefs favoring truth, and enough slack for contradictory evidence to be integrated rather than defended against.

In totality, this loop looks something like this:

felt sense → internal or external probe → test against the field  → resonance, friction, or baseline → locate the source of the response → update representation → new felt sense → ↻

As established earlier, intuition needs this loop to become well calibrated and attuned to the relevant context you are working within. However, the loop also needs intuition to run, it is a contingent relationship. Feeling resonance or friction is not the external feedback itself, but the change in felt tone produced once the field interprets that feedback. Locating the source of friction may involve intuition catching which properties were violated or satisfied, deliberate analysis, or using both (which is common). Intuition and the loop work together as you use intuition to generate and interpret each pass, and each pass tunes the field which then reshapes intuition.

It is important to note that we do not only use this loop when we deliberately sit down to learn or think. Implicit re-ordering happens constantly and mostly below immediate awareness, since sensory input is continuously being tested against the field and small updates occur as we move through the world. Most passes of the loop are quick and implicit and never come to be recognized as an explicit turn, like the micro-adjustments we make when interacting with others. Deliberate usage of this refinement loop is a more specific version of this process where a representation is kept stable and scrutinized enough to be interacted with, like when writing an essay or solving a math problem. 

Relatedly, closed and open loops also run in parallel rather than only one being ran at individually. You can be changing a representation internally by imagining how something works, and in the next moment external feedback can affect your modeling of the thing through contradiction. The field integrates both channels nearly in parallel. The loop is therefore better modeled as many overlapping cycles happening continuously at different temporal variations and differing levels of awareness. The clean process laid out here is a simplification of one pass compared to the more dense and mostly automatic version that exists '“in the real world.” Still, understanding its core mechanics allows us to use it deliberately and wisely. 

Felt sense

We must begin with our felt sense of understanding, our current model of how a thing works, which is experienced first as an embodied sense. This is how the field sits in the body at a given moment; it’s our most immediate access to where the layers currently stand, although it can still be incomplete. The felt sense includes the default field and any additional properties introduced by representations, like using a metaphor to describe your current embodied sense of what you know. This is Gendlin’s findings at play.

Internal or external probe

A probe is an expression of what your felt sense is “saying.” It is a candidate expression of what the field currently holds, like “I think this works like this…” or “A metaphor I would use to describe this is…” It does not matter yet if this probe is factually correct; what matters is that it expresses your current understanding clearly enough to be tested. As Wittgenstein puts it, “One must start with error and lead it over into truth.”

There are two ways this probe can manifest: internal or external. An internal probe is run inside the mind using a representation like a simulation or an image. An external probe compresses the felt sense of understanding into notation, like drawing a diagram or writing about it.

To present a probe is to objectify your current model. You are making it a stable object you can inspect and manipulate. We do this internally by turning attention toward the representation we are holding or externally by translating it into notation. Externalization is especially powerful because it makes the probe legible enough for other people and systems.

Roger Thisdell offers a useful structural parallel in his streamlined enlightenment protocol. He argues that before a phenomenon can be deobjectified, it must first be objectified, isolated clearly enough that the mind can recognize it as an object of experience. Only then can it be seen through as empty, contingent, and not ultimately solid. In non-meditation jargon, before we can deconstruct our perception of something, we must first bring it into immediate experience and make sure our system registers it as an inspectable object.

The epistemic loop uses an analogous move. To refine embodied or representational knowledge, we must first objectify it by making it stable enough for the mind to inspect. Attention can do this internally by sustaining a representation like an image, while notation can do it externally through a fixed sentence or diagram. Thisdell’s deobjectification and epistemic testing are not identical operations since his concerns contemplative action which is geared towards phenomenological changes, while this loop tests where a model breaks against internal or external properties. The shared principle is that implicit material must first be isolated before its construction can be examined.

Remember that externalizing is lossy because of compression, so forming a probe does not capture the full essence of understanding, but makes part of it usable and inspectable. If your understanding is not rich enough yet, the externalization will be especially imprecise because the knowledge has not yet developed deeply within the bottom two layers.

Working memory places an additional limit on the internal loop because, in simple unchunked tasks, we can typically hold only around three or four items or chunks in focal attention at once. This limitation is one reason a vague felt sense of understanding can be misleading: the system may be holding only a highly compressed outline rather than the whole structure. To work around this constraint, we can offload items into external notation or develop organized chunks in long-term memory. As Justin Skycack puts it, “Whereas a beginner perceives individual isolated pieces of information, an expert perceives ‘chunks’ of information organized into meaningful patterns and structures. These chunks are physically encoded as wiring in the expert’s long-term memory, and they are the building blocks that make up the expert’s representation of what they’re looking at in working memory.” Externalization and expertise therefore extend the loop in complementary ways by adding structure into the environment, while the other compresses it into retrievable internal structure.

Test the probe: resonance, friction, or baseline

This part covers how the field responds when the probe is tested. If the probe produces resonance, this does not mean the probe is entirely truthful; the field itself may not correspond with reality. It simply means that the probe integrated more cheaply or satisfyingly than the system expected, producing a positive difference in felt tone. If we hit friction, we are encountering input that is more costly or difficult to integrate than expected, producing a negative difference in felt tone. If the probe fits about as expected, the response may remain at baseline and provide little noticeable signal at all.

Because resonance and friction are differences from the background baseline, neither is guaranteed to appear every time a probe fits or fails to fit. A baseline response may mean that the probe is unsurprising which does not produce a noticeable spike.

There are four places where a mismatch can become detectable. The first is the embodied layer: you might write a sentence explaining something and, when you do, your body disagrees. There’s a slight twinge that tells you to try again. Second is the representational layer: through externalizing the model, it becomes clear what is missing, how your representation was incomplete, or how your memory is not up to date. Third is the notational layer: the sentence has implications or contradictions you could not have registered while it was abstract. Lastly, external evidence can produce friction after being interpreted by the field. This can happen by sending a probe out and having the world push back, such as a tutor saying it is wrong, or by taking in new information that contradicts your model. In this final case, external reality gives the constraint, while friction is the internal felt response to it.

It is important to parse out these four locations and categorize the first three as closed, meaning the model is tested using internally available constraints, and the fourth as open, meaning a constraint enters from outside the current model. Closed loops primarily test fit and coherence, while open loops can test correspondence with reality. Even then, the outside source may itself be mistaken, so open feedback must also be evaluated.

This testing can be fast and implicit, like saying a phrase out loud, such as “How are you?” and seeing how it lands in the system, or receiving feedback from others and immediately wondering, “Why did they say that?”

Locate the source of friction or resonance

Because friction simply tells you something is off, not what is off, we need a diagnostic step to understand how we can precisely update our understanding. With this step, we can know that something has failed to integrate and then investigate whether our model is actually what needs to change.

We first must locate where the response arose. Is it coming from the embodied, representational, or notational layer, or was it triggered by external evidence? Is the friction coming from some new information I just read or from my internal representation not being complete? We can also locate which field property is being violated, like a variable or constraint. For example, “This variable cannot be this, so that is where the friction is coming from.”

As a caveat, just because friction occurs does not mean updating needs to happen. The response could be coming from mistaken notation, a body in fight-or-flight mode, or a misreading of the question. With a diagnosis, we can better determine whether our model is incorrect and ensure that we are updating in light of accuracy.

This step is one place where expertise matters: within a well-calibrated domain, experts can locate the source of friction much faster and more accurately than a novice. Because they understand their field so well, the violated properties become clearer, and they can surgically manipulate the things causing the friction. Expertise does not make the response truthful, however, especially outside the domain and context in which that intuition was trained.

The other side of locating the source of friction is locating where resonance is coming from. I would argue this part is not as needed to train understanding, but it is helpful in conditioning what your system finds good. For example, if I generate a metaphor in my head that I then explain and it checks out as being accurate, it might be helpful to assess why this is. Is it the feedback loops that made it a good model? What about its variables? This can then let us in on different principles that could be extrapolated elsewhere, like, “Okay, this shape takes this form when X or Y, so I can apply this to this other problem.” This is training intuition on the resonant side.

If the response stays at baseline a new question comes to light that asks was the probe already expected or was it too weak to test the relevant structure? In that case, the next step may be to construct a more specific probe rather than just update the model. 

Update representation

After encountering friction and locating its source, we can update our representation of the thing to be a better fit, either externally or internally. When open, we are updating it toward better correspondence with the available input we believe to be of higher order; when closed, we are updating it toward fit and coherence abiding by the internal substrate. In any scenario, revising the representation retunes the field and its properties, like variables and constraints, which is why the embodied felt sense may eventually shift after an explicit update.

It must be said that there is generally an update-speed hierarchy. Notation updating is fast: we can quickly erase a sentence we just wrote and change it to match what is better supported. A representation update is slower: we must change its interactions, what it looks like, its feedback loops, or its general shape in our mind. Surface-level somatic retuning can also happen quickly when the representation changes, but deeply embodied updates are usually the slowest because they involve deep priors that structure perception in the first place. One contradiction won’t rewrite a prior repeated thousands of times, just as doing a task one time won’t generate true tacit knowledge.

To address the field of experience, this loop makes the landscape clearer, like making the edges of a thing more visible so you know where you stand. The refinement loop makes the field more navigable, like a foreigner becoming accustomed to new land. By iterating on how we model the thing, we redraw the boundaries, slopes, and paths of the field to better align with reality. Externalizations help us redraw the landscape, while feedback reveals where the new map still fails. Friction can make violated variables and constraints visible enough that the internal representation can be updated accordingly. The mistakes created by each lossy externalization reveal hidden constraints, potentially making the next expression less wrong and the field itself more accurate and differentiated. Over time, territory that was once foreign becomes familiar as it is slowly untangled, like a messy bundle of yarn being cleanly unwound.

This act of making representations and the field clearer seems similar to the skill of sensory clarity in meditation. Sensory clarity involves learning to distinguish phenomena in experience without immediately becoming lost in their movement or story. It’s like tracking the different sensations occurring in one specific area of your left foot; it’s a skill that takes time to develop. This parallels the process of making internal knowledge clearer by untangling confusion. Sensory clarity is resolution-building at the embodied layer, while refinement is resolution-building at the representational layer. Both skills use a similar move of taking an abstract blob and differentiating it over time to see what is actually there. In this limited sense, sensory clarity can reduce suffering while representational clarity can reduce ignorance.

One caveat here is that updating toward resonance alone without an independent constraint can make wrongness feel true, even if it has no correspondence with reality. A field can become internally coherent while remaining detached from the world. Empirical models therefore require empirical feedback, while formal models require constraints like proof. Other people and “factual” information can provide additional checks. Some independent source of constraint is needed to keep refinement from becoming only the production of increasingly satisfying representations.

New felt sense → loop

For an update to be “successful,” it must eventually become integrated into the field, which may generate a new felt sense. This new felt sense does not need to be deep, like a core belief; it can just be a new way of looking at things. For example, maybe you updated a metaphor to be more accurate, and now the update has generated new constraints you did not have in mind before. If the notation changes but the felt sense does not, this may indicate that the update has not yet propagated into the representational or embodied layers.

Now that this new felt sense exists, we can restart the loop with our new understanding and form another probe. Each new probe can become better calibrated as long as reliable feedback is incorporated, allowing the field to become more accurate and differentiated.

This loop is not a one-and-done thing. Many passes are needed for deep change, especially for embodied knowledge, which is where expertise lives. Repeated passes can also propagate surface-level representational changes into deeper branches of the tree, much like repeated practice gradually turns explicit knowledge into tacit knowledge.

This active iteration is a central mechanism of learning: it uses the mechanisms of knowledge and their different layers to loop on updating toward truth. Passive input, like educational YouTube videos, pales in comparison to actively participating in this loop of refining internal states, although passive input can still supply material for later testing and refinement.

The end state of this loop is not to reach perfect truth, but to progressively improve calibration. Through fine-tuning our field, we are orienting ourselves toward experience in a manner that is better aligned with available feedback while remaining useful for our targets.

I believe Grothendieck almost perfectly captures the essence of refinement:



When I am curious about something, mathematical or otherwise, I question/[interroge] it. I question it, without caring if my question is perhaps stupid or if it will appear so, without it being carefully weighed. Often the question takes the form of an assertion - an assertion which, in truth, is a knocking probe. I will then believe more or believe less in the assertion, which depends of course on where I stand in the comprehension of the things I’m looking at. Often, especially at the beginning of a research, the assertion is completely false - but this still had to be done to convince yourself. Often, it suffices to write it down for it to become obvious that it is false, whereas before writing it down there was a vagueness/[flou], like an uneasiness/[malaise], instead of obviousness. It then enables us to return to the probing with less ignorance, with a question-assertion that is perhaps a little less “off the mark”. More often still, the affirmation taken literally turns out to be false, but the intuition which, still clumsily, tries to convey itself through this is correct, though remaining vague. This intuition will, little by little, be decanted from an equally shapeless gangue of false or inadequate ideas, it will gradually emerge from the limbo of the misunderstood which only asks to be understood, of the unknown which only asks to be known, to take a form which is her own, to refine and sharpen its contours, as the questions I ask of these things in front of me become more precise or more pertinent, to surround them more and more closely.

Alexander Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles
