Language as a medium, whether it be body language, or a natural language like english, is used to transfer information. I like to think of language as a ‘shared compression channel’ in the sense that it binds together nodes of compression spread across the three layers of knowledge— embodied, representational, or notational. Meaning, tokens of a language, say words in english or gestures in sign language, bring about different interconnected networks of gestalts, whether consciously or unconsciously. If I say the word “unicorn,” this will spawn a plethora of nodes for the individual agent taking in the word as input, such as maybe a mental image, some memories, and general feelings in the body. Crucially, this spells out a key characteristic of language: it can point to what is *not physically present* but nonetheless still generate and capture a plethora of associated feelings.

I say language is a shared compression channel because the agent that is interpreting and using the language must decompress the tokens (individual units/symbols within a symbolic system) that are present through their own bow tie and field. Language of course collapses many possibilities, meaning a word cannot possibly capture the whole of information dealing with an intuition for example. Because of this collapse, the receiving agent must decompress the tokens of language by bringing online nodes of the three layers that are interconnected to form the gestalt known as the token being digested. Of course, this means language can be heavily constrained by both the producing and receiving agents’ bow tie and field. A negative valenced field configuration means the language set by the producer will be flavored by that tone of valence, and then the receiver must decompress that, and sometimes this results in not recognizing how the producer’s state is flavoring their language.

Writing of language such as the words you are reading now is maximally compressed as it lies in the notational layer. However, mediums like body language, prosody, and facial expressions lie more so in the embodied layer. This presents an interesting heuristic: language that is more compressed is easier to interpret and know the specificities of what it points to, but harder to decompress accurately in terms of preserving the producer’s original feeling, while language that is less compressed is less specific about its referent but transports more of that feeling directly. This becomes even weirder when you consider language feeds off felt senses, meaning, [tokens are tokens because they bring about the “right” felt senses](https://opentheory.net/2018/08/a-future-for-neuroscience/#iv-model-interpersonal-dynamics:~:text=What%20is%20often%20missed%20is%20that%20language%20is%20a%20felt%2Dsense%20phenomenon.). This is most obvious when writing: we often come across words or phrases that simply “feel off” and thus need to be rewritten. I predict this has to do with resonance and friction, and the actual cost to integrate those written tokens into the current field was more than the predicted cost, producing a feeling in the body that spawned actions of deletion within the dependency tree.

Interestingly, natural languages are recursively compositional— you combine tokens to generate new and connected pointers towards nodes in the three layers of knowledge. Most of the time these nodes will lie in the embodied layer, as most reading and production of language is “automatic” and occurs within a quick time-scale. A shared decompression key is simply a set of priors and gestalts two people hold in common. It is the structure the receiving agent uses to decompress a compressed pointer back into the right cluster of felt-senses, representations, and notation. Sharing a key means sharing important priors, which is why one word can regenerate the same feeling across a multitude of people. Because of this, not sharing the same priors or gestalts means receiving agents “misinterpret” the original producer’s meaning by decompressing towards specificity that is not aligned with the producer’s priors that were upstream of the produced language.

Language itself is simply an input like any other thing like a water bottle or your phone. The core difference between language and other input, what makes language its own category, is that it serves the function of communication through linking bow ties. Because of this, language can use shared keys to decompress through, meaning sharing the same invariants across bow ties for decompression. This scales with spatiotemporal levels of specificity: as language becomes more specific, such as going from a word to a book or from everyday language to expertise, compression keys come apart across agents. One word is typically easy to track across populations, while a poem or book is much harder due to specificity branching out which becomes unique to an agent.

My general claim is that language does not determine field configurations, but it can be influential in a way that feels deterministic. It cannot be fully deterministic because language is only one type of input that the bow tie compresses. Bodily sensations and the environment, for example, also steer how the system compresses input and then decompresses towards action. Language biases which felt senses or other nodes of knowledge become salient and easy to instantiate by biasing the right side of the bow tie in a certain direction through the agent’s act of compression. Remember that in the bow tie, the left side’s act of compressing input occurs through existing memory and structure, and language cannot be separated from this process. Considering that [words and other tokens of language are known within an associative network, like “cat” connecting to “animal”](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.82.6.407), due to the bow tie producing invariant structure, putting an embodied sense into words can bundle together properties of its neighbors, like associated valence. For instance, if we were to research what we might be feeling as depression, we might come across suicide as being associated with depression. This then creates the possibility for our system to bundle together whatever properties feel connected between depression and suicide, which then influences our field configuration(s). This only increases with repetition: [constantly associating tokens](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x) further couples their networks of nodes of knowledge, which then retunes our structure and priors towards those biases. However, repetition also works the other way around as it can weaken token-to-body links, which is why a word or phrase repeated enough times starts to feel empty (semantic satiation). I had this insight once when I was attempting to send a heartfelt voice message and after "trying to get it right" after 5 or so attempts, the original feeling my body was completely gone and the words felt empty. Be cautious as to not kill the ineffable. Downstream of this is pragmatic advice: repetition is a tool. We can use it dissolve what you want gone, and keep it away from what you want alive. Now, there is nuance here since purely word-to-word repetition can strengthen certain things, but generally repeating body-to-tokens will kill that part of you. [Metaphor here is no different](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0016782), as metaphors simply instantiate and bias our bow ties to compress and decompress towards the associated properties of the metaphor being used.&#x20;

Thinking about language here reminds me of the famous madeleine passage from Marcel Proust, in which a single bite produced a wave of memories and feelings, each feeding into each other allowing the narrator to relive what is associated with the food, like how language reconstructs connected nodes of knowledge:

> No sooner had the warm liquid mixed with the crumbs touched my palate than a shudder ran through me and I stopped, intent upon the extraordinary thing that was happening to me. An exquisite pleasure had invaded my senses, something isolated, detached, with no suggestion of its origin. And at once the vicissitudes of life had become indifferent to me, its disasters innocuous, its brevity illusory – this new sensation having had on me the effect which love has of filling me with a precious essence; or rather this essence was not in me it was me. ... Whence did it come? What did it mean? How could I seize and apprehend it? ... And suddenly the memory revealed itself. The taste was that of the little piece of madeleine which on Sunday mornings at Combray (because on those mornings I did not go out before mass), when I went to say good morning to her in her bedroom, my aunt Léonie used to give me, dipping it first in her own cup of tea or tisane. The sight of the little madeleine had recalled nothing to my mind before I tasted it. And all from my cup of tea.
>
> ― Marcel Proust, *In Search of Lost Time*

If stances, as mentioned in section 2.2 on somatics, are “discrete patterns of muscle tension” that constrict certain priors/predictions within a given context, and if language is a symbolic projection of felt senses and internal representations (simplified), then certain stances bring about different linguistic patterns. A stance is the constraint that sets the linguistic possibility space, and output works within it. This is obvious in acting: actors who hold their character’s stance well naturally produce dialogue, without a script, that matches the character, because the tension they’re holding constrains the possibility space in a specific way. The same shows up when you try to emulate someone else’s writing — you’re copying their exact patterns of tension, which is very hard. You can get decently close to a style, but never exact, and never as informationally rich or generative; the low-level informational landscape is too rich to be fully known and copied.

This gives more context to very good speakers: they have the ability to release the tension holding common linguistic constraints. A professional environment “flips on” certain patterns of tension, making things like cursing unreachable, or at least harder to reach. Charismatic speakers are orthogonal to this — they’re uniquely free to traverse language landscapes, and probably stances of speech as well, like tone of voice. And if tension is held as a prediction to prevent bad things from happening, then the language a stance permits is the language that keeps those predictions safe. There’s a “linguistic self-fulfilling prophecy” here: we don’t speak of things that would endanger our deeply held patterns, so the language a stance *prevents* is often a tell of what’s going on inside you. Vulnerability with others often requires temporarily stepping out of the tension pattern that was preventing you from saying the scary thing in the first place. As Johnson puts it, [“to know someone is to know their list”](https://x.com/johnsonmxe/status/1878648168356954567) — which becomes observing what a person cannot utter in their specific stance.

This also reframes why “language is magic” feels true: language binds certain stances together, especially when a group organizes around a shared set of knowledge, like religion. I model language as a kind of “cognitive glue” (borrowing from Levin) that binds connected nodes across the three layers of knowledge — embodied, representational, notational — as addressable tokens that recursively generate connected clusters of addresses pointing toward more nodes. Buddhism holds specific Buddhist language precisely to elicit the right nodes, held in place by patterns of tension. Since Michael Johnson (creator of the vasocomputation and "stance" ideas) [models the nervous system as an FPGA](https://x.com/johnsonmxe/status/1872482551677194267?s=20) (a programmable chip), language can be read as an activation signal for certain arrays, language-tokens indexing into vascular-addressable-units. This is also what framing effects do: a frame — a title, label, or metaphor — is a token that indexes a configuration of vascular tension, mental and physical, and because that tension holds predictions in place, it colors perception to confirm what we so deeply predict sensory input should be. A generative frame therefore works as an unclenching device, meant to loosen stuck tension patterns so we can literally see things differently. None of this makes language deterministic in the way this sketch draws it — context, environment, and the people around you all shape language and behavior in complicated ways, and behavior is often decoupled — but the idea that language is constrained by your vasocomputation “stance” is valuable.

There is actually quite interesting research on second languages, which often finds that people who speak a second language [can be more vulnerable in their L2 than in their L1.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19401183.1986.12461512) This might be because [the first language's words were acquired in high-arousal settings](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/taboo-words-and-reprimands-elicit-greater-autonomic-reactivity-in-a-first-language-than-in-a-second-language/63F2EBB7F69512FDACFDCC55DB8C48DC), so each one indexes into more unpleasant addresses of tension patterns. The second language was likely wired in emotionally neutral or positive contexts, so it maps to a stance that isn't as unpleasant. In someone's L1, they would need to "break out of" their current stance to utter what their L2 allows in most stances; in L2, you "step out of the tension pattern" simply by changing which address-space you speak from. [Caldwell-Harris et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1367006910379262) found that interviewees preferred English for emotional expression because its social environments felt less restrictive, noting that "respondents nonetheless preferred to express their emotions in English, citing more relaxed social constraints in English-speaking environments." It is plausible to me that Chinese culture would clench more around endearments (e.g., "thank you," "I miss you," "I love you"), making that vulnerability harder to reach in a stance dealing with Mandarin, while English would produce "looser" stances.

Language is also a feeling in the body. Literally, when you talk you can feel the vibrations in your vocal cords and whatnot. Furthermore, language indexes different parts of our mind-body schemas, allowing us to traverse our phenomenology using different tokens. The dialectic between language (the head) and the gut (feeling your feelings) is important and shouldn’t be disregarded. Feeling the “raw data” of feelings comes at a cost if you never give it a resonant handle. The head gives the gut a way to be heard, and the gut gives the head a way to feel without biasing towards combinatorial explosion of indexing phenomenology.

This is why, when you are dialoguing with your body, e.g. in IFS, you are finding the tokens that resonate with what that physical part of you is trying to communicate. We only think this is weird/esoteric/not “real” because our self model lies in the head area— it’s where our senses lie. We draw our self-model boundary around our head and generally draw language with a stricter boundary. But imagine if your eyes were at your stomach: communicating with these neurons would seem much more natural because we’d be drawing the sense of self boundary over that physical location. All this to say, “talking to” your body is completely rational, and shutting parts off closes off versions of ourselves we’ve yet to discover. A heart-opening is literally allowing the heart to communicate with other parts— it now gets a say in things. And closed-off parts produce symptoms, like suppression of connection, that we think need to be solved.

As mentioned above, good framing effects work the same way. I think all good framing loosens the boundaries the subject is drawing. Say I’m nervous about an interview. A good frame from a friend isn’t just “you’ll be fine,” it’s language that loosens my held contraction on what I think the interview is/will be, like “it’s just two people asking you questions.” Or we can take stand-up comedy as another example: the setup builds a frame, and the punchline shows a second perspective that also fits, resulting in surprise, revealing that the first boundary you drew was arbitrary and not solid. Laughter is a behavioral projection of this.

This is also why frames work as keys, while arguments work as force against the lock. The way most people’s ontological beliefs work is “bottom-up,” meaning there’s a set intuitive felt sense about the presented thing, and reasoning is generated from that felt sense. Like with abortion, for whatever reason one person may simply have a strong felt sense of this thing being wrong, so all of their reasoning will come from that, causing them to confirm that feeling with language (e.g., reasoning). This is social intuitionism: the idea that “[moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached.](https://phm.znu.ac.ir/article_242282_en.html)”

Now, one way we update our ontological beliefs is through top-down guidance, meaning we use language to manipulate/control the intuitive felt sense we have about something. This can either work by changing the feeling in the body, or the reasoning fails to reach the feeling because the feeling is so strong (this is what happens when people agree with logic/”facts” but still return to their original beliefs). Top-down guidance works best when the language evokes a new felt sense rather than arguing against the old one, which is what a good frame does. This is because the old felt sense is inherently defensive against argument; it is trying to keep itself alive. If we can evoke a new felt sense in a person, then inject the needed language for a belief update, we will be a lot more successful than fighting against the old pattern.

This makes epistemic humility a bodily skill, not just a cognitive attitude. If we clench anytime a counterargument is presented, there’s no hope of our original belief being changed. Sensory clarity plays a role here in detecting when you’re doing this so you can simply stop. If we unclench successfully, new arguments can reach the felt sense for change. Of course, this does not mean if we unclench we should be susceptible to every single belief of ours changing; good sensemaking skills are required after the fact to assess validity of statements, but unclenching is the prerequisite first step to doing this.

This insight about language is not about dropping certain things entirely. Just as we need some physical tension to survive, we need language to operate. But we can also solidify this tension into something deeper that produces suffering and offers no other affordances. The goal is to release the extra contraction that is only there to control experience, to make you think you have control over sensations. And the goal with language is to "see through" the tokens that address areas of structure that attempt to freeze patterns and conceptual boundaries, which can result in suffering.
