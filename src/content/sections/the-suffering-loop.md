I want to provide a powerful synthesis of ideas related to suffering to understand the mechanisms going on. I will be explaining the synthesis using both theoretical ideas and examples that tie the concepts together. To make the mechanism clear, we can separate it into three phases: first-order hurt, suffering as a field configuration, and maintenance.

To define suffering, we need a working definition. In this framework, suffering can be thought of as a negatively felt state that "spreads" across the field because a highly weighted target makes experience feel as though it must be different in some way, which includes making it permanent. It persists when the mind-body system cannot find an easily reachable thing to do, such as action, updating the model, or changing the target, that either closes the difference between its current and target state or lets the difference remain without resisting it. To be clear about the terminology here, target error is the difference between the current state and the target, and action policies are the paths that might remove the difference.&nbsp;Technically, ordinary wanting of a different field configuration, like more valence from a material good, is suffering since the target is weighted enough. This is why the state of cessation, or nirodha samāpatti (consciousness briefly ceasing during meditation), is ultimately the only state we can be in with absolutely zero suffering. Practically, suffering in its ordinary usage typically names the inflection point at which the target reorganizes the field enough so it is mostly geared towards that target. In the practical sense, there's also a temporal aspect at play since most people would not consider a craving that can be satisfied in one minute to be suffering. For example, the preference to get rid of sensations of sitting on a chair due to mild restlessness would be suffering, while practically someone might only consider suffering to be when the sensations start hurting and the field organizes enough around moving away from that state (so either getting up or changing the interpretation of the sensations). However, in both instances, suffering is present due to the negative felt tone of not being in a weighted target state. In the case of suffering across the framework, the wise goal is not fully getting rid of it, but reducing enough of its magnitude as practically possible and increasing reachability across field configurations so we avoid getting stuck in states.

First, we start with the basic chain of experiencing some type of input. For every interpreted input, there is a predicted integration cost. Let’s give it a value of 20 here as a metaphor. Then we have to actually integrate the interpreted input into our system, and input can be far away or close to fitting into our field which is what the fit distance property measures. Let’s say the actual integration cost is 30 instead of the predicted 20. This results in a negative difference in felt tone experienced as friction and, typically, some degree of unpleasantness.

This is first-order hurt, though not the only source of it. With any first-order hurt, there is the immediate negatively valenced aspect of the experience, considered before any interpretation or action. The hurt can come from threatening of a target, violation of an expectation, loss, sensory overload, or wrongly predicted integration cost. In this framework, it is defined as the immediate negatively felt aspect of an experience that is registered before the field produces a target that it needs to be different. In Buddhism, [this is known as the first arrow](https://suttacentral.net/sn36.6/en/sujato?lang=en&layout=plain&reference=none&notes=asterisk&highlight=false&script=latin) which represents unavoidable painful experience.

First order hurt can be unpleasant before the system adds any secondary interpretation or control effort. Factors like emotional or physical pain, exhaustion, loss, and sensory overload can be hurtful even when they are not surprising. First-order hurt is not identical to suffering considering the negative felt state has yet to organize the field around a high precision target for experience to be different. Once the mind-body system feels this hurt, taṇhā occurs. [Taṇhā literally means thirst](https://suttacentral.net/define/ta%E1%B9%87h%C4%81?lang=en) and is conventionally translated as craving, though this might be contested in some areas for its technicalities. For example, [Romeo Stevens says](https://neuroticgradientdescent.blogspot.com/2020/01/mistranslating-buddha.html) the term might be better known as 'fused to' or 'welded to' which seems more like an accurate phenomenological description. Anyways, the important part about taṇhā is that it precedes suffering and is the "grabby" or "pushy" thing that interacts with sensations of experience. So it is not that suffering is directly taṇhā, but taṇhā is the mechanism by which suffering is born through the conditioning of organizing the field around targets. For accuracy, in the Four Noble Truths, [Buddhism identifies taṇhā as the principal condition giving rise to dukkha](https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11/en/bodhi?lang=en&reference=none&highlight=false), rather than as dukkha itself: "...the origin of suffering: it is this craving which leads to renewed existence..."

Next is where first-order hurt becomes suffering in the sense defined above because the target is now weighted enough to organize the field around obtaining it. [This is known as the second arrow in Buddhism](https://suttacentral.net/sn36.6/en/sujato?lang=en&layout=plain&reference=none&notes=asterisk&highlight=false&script=latin) which represents the additional mental second-order stress that comes from reactivity. Targeting can go in two different directions: craving and aversion. In the direction of aversion, the target is something like "this state should not be happening," so the mind-body system attempts to evade the current state. In the craving direction, the target is something like "I need to reach or maintain that state," so the mind-body system attempts to obtain the target. Both directions implicitly contain the notion that there is a difference between the actual state and the target, which is target error.&nbsp;Precision weighting is what organizes the field around closing the difference. The reachability of the closing of the difference is how long the suffering might last and how intense it is. For example, craving for a piece of cake to obtain sensory pleasure is highly reachable if cake is near you, making the magnitude of the suffering not very large. Compare this to something like grief where the target of bringing someone back to life or denial of emotions: the reachability of the target is impossible so the magnitude of suffering will increase.&nbsp;

Roughly, what we have so far is:

First-order hurt + "I don't want this" target → aversion\
Absence of desired state + "I must have this" target → craving

(Absence of a desired state is still hurt, like a felt lack)

More deeply, craving and aversion can be understood as co-arising signals of the same discrepancy. Co-arising meaning the thing implicitly signals another thing in experience, like the way 'up' only means anything if 'down' is also available. Every instance of craving will contain an aversion to how things currently are, and every instance of aversion will contain a craving for a different state. When our actual state is not the preferred state, there is craving for the preferred state and aversion to the actual state. Every act of craving can implicitly reject the present configuration, while every aversion can implicitly want something else. They are not always phenomenologically identical in how they feel, nor do they always appear together consciously, but mechanistically they can be understood as co-arising signals of the same process trying to close target error.

Then comes active inference which is the start of the maintenance phase. We take action in some manner when our actual state differs from the target state. The mind-body system can attempt to change the world or body to match the target, change its model of the hurt, or update the target itself. We can take a look at an example of being hurt in some way to understand this better. First, when we are hurt, we can leave the situation, which changes the state of the world. Second, we can suppress the sensations that are downstream of input being processed by the field through contraction, which may change our state temporarily. Lastly, we can reinterpret the input, which changes the model. We can also lower the weighting of the target instead by allowing the sensation without suppression through expansion, which reduces the precision of the demand that the hurt must change. These are all attempts to close the difference between the target state and the current state, or responses to target error.

It is crucial to mention slack and its importance here because without enough, the system cannot hold target error open long enough for competing branches in the dependency tree to stay available, which means hurt will automatically turn into suffering. Without slack, taṇhā becomes destiny.

It’s important to mention somatics here and how the body plays a role. As established earlier, priors may be held and stabilized through recurring patterns of muscular, visceral, vascular, and autonomic tension, which means an active target does not only predict a preferred state but prepares the body to enact it. When input violates that prediction, friction can be felt interoceptively, and the existing bodily pattern may tighten in an attempt to defend the prior. Through active inference, the body then prepares an action to change the experience through the ways listed in the paragraph above.

Haptic contraction is not identical to craving or aversion, but it can be one of their somatic expressions. If sustained, the contraction keeps the target active and highly weighted while also physically reinstantiating it, which helps explain the phrase “what you resist persists.” This is maintenance: the recursive coupling of hurt and taṇhā which keeps the field organized around the target and produces cascading suffering. Maintenance can reinforce itself because the contraction is unpleasant and continues to violate the active target which confirms the prediction that the state needs to change, producing further taṇhā. As a whole, craving and aversion describe the direction of the mind-body system’s control effort, while haptic contraction is a way the associated priors are felt and enacted in the body.

The maintenance loop becomes extra rigid when the wanted target state is highly weighted and has low or impossible reachability. The system continues weighting the target heavily while lacking a viable action policy for obtaining it due to barriers. This leaves the difference in preference active and produces repeated attempts at control that fail to resolve the error.

unpleasant experience → “this must not be happening” → action policy to stop it → contraction → additional unpleasant evidence → stronger belief that the state is intolerable → stronger action to stop it

It is crucial to mention slack and its importance here: without enough slack, the system cannot hold target error open long enough for competing branches to stay alive, which means hurt will automatically turn into suffering. Without slack, taṇhā becomes destiny.

Through repetition, the suffering maintenance loop can become an attractor state itself that repeatedly reinstates the field configuration supporting it. One common starter of this is rumination on memories, which are reconstructed as present representations, that bring about unpleasant sensations, so recalling one reinstates the field configuration it came from and the body responds as if the situation is happening in the present moment.

Repeated maintenance of craving or aversion can make the contraction latch in the body, which then holds the suffering configuration in place without ongoing effort. One downstream effect of this is that the latch itself, or contraction pattern stops needing the original trigger for it to be enacted through active inference. Once the latch can become stable on its own, the configuration reinstates from mundane conditions rather than from anything substantial happening, hence why long-term patterns just feel like the way things work. This is where habitual suppression of unpleasant sensations, like tensing the body to not feel emotions, can do its damage, since suppression is a form of contraction.

Interestingly, this suffering loop is not only phenomenologically relevant, like dealing with bodily sensations, but it is epistemically relevant as well. Meaning, suffering can occur in the direction of epistemic ignorance, when the thing being defended is a model or concept. Epistemic contraction is just like bodily contraction, in the sense that it is a mechanism as to prevent unwanted experience from occurring. Disconfirming input that does not go along with your current model of a thing is registered as friction, which can be both bodily and/or cognitive. This friction can result in aversion, a reaching towards of a target of a more pleasant state of the model being "right". Typically this results in suppression, like blocking the input or denial of the ignorance. Just as habitual bodily contraction can bury priors deep into the body making them the default way of viewing things, so too can the same thing happen to epistemic models in which it is buried deep into the field if contracted around enough. This means revising the epistemic model of a concept or thing means revising everything else built on top of it, which is typically costly to the system, resulting in more defense for deep models. So, phenomenological and epistemic ignorance are the same failure mode pointing in specific directions, since both treat constructions as the way things have to be. In this sense, open and closed loops of refinement to update models must require that the thing needing updating is not contracted around, since defending against change would make refinement very difficult, resulting in more ignorance. For nuance, tacit knowledge might also be seen as the same way, contraction living in the body, but crucially it runs on open loops along with holding diverse action policies so the system does not shut down in the same way it does with patterns related to suffering.

If we lower precision on targets that we once held heavily, though practice like meditation, we can feel a much smaller gap between the actual and preferred state, which means there is less pressure for active inference around those old targets. This can feel like reduced motivation because lots of ordinary motivation is powered by contraction around “this must happen” or “this must stop.”

However, direction and pressure should be separated. Direction comes from the target itself which can be wholesome like the desire for connection with others, while pressure comes from the added prediction that the target must be reached for whatever reason. This added prediction that the target must be reached is held as contraction, both in the mind and body, and as [taṇhā](https://suttacentral.net/define/ta%E1%B9%87h%C4%81?lang=en). Crucially, desire and pressure is what produces suffering, while desire by itself without pressure has the ability to be  not characterized by suffering, what Buddhism might call [wholesome forms of chanda](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn56/sn56.011.harv.html). However, this form of wholesome desire without added pressure is very hard to achieve without some sort of deliberate practice like meditation, so the average person experiencing desire will always be suffering, even if in small amounts. For example, mild wanting of cake is low-grade suffering since the added pressure is usually not that much. If you're starved and haven't had dessert in a while, more pressure will appear and the desire will be more intense, causing more contraction. However, this same desire for the cake can be held without added pressure. This also means the desire can naturally fall away if the person chooses to drop it, sense it is not organizing the field in such a way that is load-bearing.

Desires that feel deep and load-bearing, like wanting of love or safety feel that way because they target priors and branches sitting low in the dependency tree. Deep priors like a sense of permance of things and selfhood sit in the trunk of the tree. When a desire somehow routes through these, the target error becomes massive and expensive to deal with, so the pressure itself feels big. Loosen these priors through practice like meditation (what transparency does in section 6 as well), and the same desires stop containing so much pressure due to the priors not being so important to the system. However, if these practices that can help alleviate this type of suffering lowers weighting of targets too broadly or becomes disengagement somehow, it can produce apathy rather than equanimity and motivation.

Underling all desires, whether it be desire for items, people, or states of being, is desire for a certain field configuration. The thing you think you desire is simply a proxy, a pointer towards that field configuration that is the ultimate target. Ultimately, this is because things are gestalts which organize input, they are not external objects that are neutrally handled. You do not desire the atoms of the thing, you desire the field configuration the object is expected to produce. The cake is not the desired thing, what is desired is the configuration associated with sweetness, pleasure, and comfort that the cake is predicted to instantiate. Wanting of objects is simply a case where a gestalt is an expected route towards the target field configuration. If desire really is soley about indivual objects, substitution of objects, like trading cake for a another dessert, would be impossible. The fact that a different object can satisfy the same desire proves the target is the assoaicted configuration, not the thing itself. This also helps inform us as to why when we get the thing we wanted, often we are dissapointed and the desire does not fully end: the object succesfully regiestered as the wanted gestalt, but not the deeper configuration that the target was aiming for. And the configuration the target aims for often has to do with inaccurate priors that simply cannot be satisfied, like object permanace and selfhood. So the proxy resolves, like through obtaining the item, but the real target error persists. This same mechanism is what often powers the hedonic treadmill: what targets we desire often cannot be satisfied due to ignorance or configurations not showing up how we want. It's important not to completely collapse objects since they are reliable causes of certain field configurations to show up, which is often where deep attractor basins can show up since the object becomes a reliable path towards a desired configuration. Tying this back to the energetic triad, an unmet desire is target error which registers as friction. And because deep desires come from the trunk in the dependency tree like permanence and selfhood, that friction of not satisfying the target is large in magnitude due to many dependent structures having to be recomputed. This is where equanimity comes to be helpful as a tool, since we need to hold target field configurations as reachable without holding pressure while keeping its direction.

Equanimity in this framework leaves targets and actions available while making them less compulsory. Equanimity also acts as prevention of collapse. In section 1.3, it was outlined that a general principle exists that collapsing states from many to one is "easier" than decompressing from one to many, like notation to the embodied layer. Equanimity then becomes a tool as to prevent collapse as a result of deep haptic contraction. Mechanically, equanimity means the demand that states of the system be different does not get weighted heavily, so no contraction latches in the long run, and the possibility of a wide range of states is preserved. Equanimity is not simply calmness or low-temper; it is a specific working preventive mechanism that allows us to increase the intensity of experience without producing deep latched contraction.

It might be tempting to believe that to increase wellbeing, we should simply raise our predictions about how costly input will be to integrate so that actual costs repeatedly come in lower, producing resonance and positive felt tone. IN theory, this could reduce first-order hurt which would prevent suffering in general. However, the predicted baseline is not a free variable we can easily manipulate in this way. As mentioned in the Energetic Triad section, physiology plays a major role here and can affect our baseline in ways outside immediate conscious control. If we try to “fake” a high expected cost, that estimate is not a genuine prediction, and knowing we inflated it collapses the surprise that would have produced resonance. If we managed to genuinely condition an expectation that everything will be costly, we would be training a prior that the world is difficult or threatening. That could lower background felt tone and increase prediction that things are bad rather than producing genuine wellbeing. Another important point here is that decreasing hurt does not guarantee that craving will go away. In fact, based on the maintenance loop, without the proper skills, decreasing hurt will just increase craving by making wanted states more salient.

Increasing background felt tone, which is one part of wellbeing, can be influenced through our own agency. One objection here is that any increase in the baseline gets absorbed and becomes the new zero all other experience gets compared against. You feel better for a while, it becomes the norm, and you end up at the happiness level you were at beforehand. That mechanism holds for things the mind-body system treats as a stable condition, which it stops tracking once the change integrates into the baseline. Contraction is different, both dealing with mind and haptic contraction. Mind contraction is held "in the mind" rather than "in the body," like holding a representation about something to be true without budging. Releasing contraction is not acquiring something new that will get absorbed into the baseline like almost everything else. It is ending a cost the system was paying every moment out of its total capacity. Just as you stop noticing when an old pain disappears, like a headache, you stop registering the increase in background felt tone, even as its absence frees up capacity. The rise stays noticeable, but only through comparison to past states or when a change in conditions drop you back into an old configuration with worse felt tone which makes the difference obvious. But, even when this happens, with new skills like equanimity, being dropped into conditions that used decrease background felt tone can now be handled by not closing the repertoire of the field which increases felt tone. Baseline rise in felt tone also has the ability to decrease craving and aversion by making less demand that current states need to be different. This allows our system to hold more slack for holding intensity and makes more field configurations easily reachable which results in fluidity. These are structural changes, not just temporary adaptations. Resources are no longer spent maintaining patterns of suffering, and the body stops sending signals that confirm it. This is why advanced practitioners describe these changes as lasting and also as unremarkable, since they are both true about different things.

These processes of suffering leads us to the tool of emptiness as a way to prevent the mechanisms at play. It is important to recognize that first order hurt when suffering is a perceived solid gestalt, like “anxiety” or a “thing” that should be different. But on closer inspection, the gestalt becomes a part of a greater process and can be decomposed into many different parts. The same thing happens with suffering itself. Remember, a gestalt was defined as “the unified whole that appears in the present moment when parts are organized and glued together.” This means that if we can separate the parts effectively so the gestalt no longer appears as a unified solid thing, our system can reveal many more available policies to enact which do not maintain the state of suffering. Luckily, we have emptiness on our side as a tool to investigate the processes contributing to suffering. Contemplating and realizing emptiness as a tool can liberate us both phenomenologically and epistemically by recognizing the construction of things as an active process rather than a rigid, stuck noun.
