Intuition is your priors, your implicit structure, speaking directly. It is the field generating a salient representation or feeling before any explicit derivation. It is salient because the field gives it enough precision to make it worth attending to. Intuition is not experienced as algorithmic, but is a valuable signal about experience. The result is fast and pre-verbal, and it does not land in the notational layer, but in either the representational or embodied layer. Intuition is not itself a fit signal: a fit signal reports whether something satisfies the current field, while intuition is the rapid process by which something salient is generated. Results of intuition, like having a feeling that two things connect, are handed to you by the field, which is why an externalization or representation generated from intuition can reveal information about experience.

Intuition reaches toward possibilities through positive differences in felt tone and pulls back through negative differences. This is why a feeling of something being “off” is still a matter of intuition: the field is handing you a pre-verbal embodied assessment of fit before you have derived it step by step.

Neuroscientist Patrick House recounts a story about a young military driver taking an officer down a dangerous road in Iraq who suddenly slammed the brakes and turned around, saying, “I don’t have a good feeling.” In this framework, his trained field may have detected a violation: the road was unusually silent. This violation then surfaced as an embodied “off” feeling through a negative difference in felt tone, produced by the external input conflicting with the field. Hours later, the driver compressed that embodied feeling into notation, suggesting that the mothers may have kept their children inside because they expected danger. This is exactly what intuition is: the trained field delivering an embodied, pre-verbal feeling before notation can name the properties of the field being violated. This is the same mechanism that can contribute to “aha” moments. It is background field processing generating a result, just occurring on different time horizons.

There exists an optional step after this pre-verbal intuition surfaces, which is explicitly naming what properties in the field have been satisfied or violated. Sometimes this is easy, as in, “I have a feeling that these two things connect because of X and Y”; at other times, we must derive the properties slowly, by hand. When this explicit step occurs, we are compressing that intuition up into notation, which will not capture the entirety of it. Articulability of an intuition measures how compressible it is, not whether it is an intuition in the first place. If we do not have the proper notational knowledge in place, it will be harder to name that intuition since we don’t have the proper ways into which to compress it.

This also explains why intuition can be right or wrong. The answer lies in our field, and luckily our field can be fine-tuned through how we shape it. Untrained intuition, like that of a novice, is a rapid output from default priors that are not well tuned to the current context. Trained intuition, however, like that of an expert, is a rapid output from a field that is well tuned to what the context calls for, hence why the tacit knowledge of expertise is so valuable: it is the actionable expression of these intuitions that have been conditioned substantially. As David Bessis puts it, “Mathematical intuition is the same intuition we use every day, but developed and solidified by its confrontation with language and logic.” This confrontation has a name that I like to call “the loop of refinement.” Without this loop, one may confidently believe that an intuition from a miscalibrated field is correct, since it can feel identical to an intuition coming from a calibrated field. Intuition is only as trustworthy as the field it comes from, which means a refinement loop is needed to calibrate it well.

Mathematics makes this process especially visible because it forces pre-verbal intuition to be translated into explicit notation and then tested through formal proof.
