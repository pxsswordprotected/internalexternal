Emergence is a very relevant concept in this framework because it brings together many of the mechanisms already defined to explain how whole things become available to experience. In the sense being used here, for a thing to be emergent, it must appear whole, not fractured. Emergence, then, is the appearance of properties produced by the organization of the whole rather than by a component taken separately. By this I mean that initially separate components are meaningfully organized, rather than something magically appearing from nothing. The resulting whole depends on conditions like sensory evidence and precision weighting. If you change those conditions, a different whole may appear.

The duck and rabbit illusion provides a simple example:





The markings remain largely the same, but changes in the weighting of possible interpretations allow either a duck or a rabbit to organize the field. One interpretation of the image typically dominates at a time because the same features must be organized through different relations. Neither interpretation about what is appearing exists within the markings themselves. Each appears when the markings and the field’s available structure are organized into a specific gestalt.

Let us take failed higher order gestalts as an example. What I mean by this is that some things can appear to us as almost forming something whole, but do not quite reach a level of higher order organization that satisfies enough of the field’s properties to become whole. Take this image, for example:





Some things within the image can still be named, such as patterns of shapes, colors, edges, textures, lighting, and a general vibe. These are already lower level gestalts, meaning less obvious than higher order ones. The image is therefore not completely without organization. The failure occurs at a higher level because those lower-level things like shapes imply that we are looking at a real scene containing known things, while the possible objects never organize enough to satisfy the field’s threshold for objecthood. Several interpretations may partially activate, but none explains enough of the structure to be dominant.

The unsettling vibe of the image therefore comes from the field attempting to maturate recognizable lower level qualityies into higher order things and failing. The lower level cues predict a resolvable scene that should be easy to process, but the actual cost of integrating the cues into real objects is greater than predicted. Since a very default low-level target of “this image should be recognizable” is dominant, this failure produces a negative difference in felt tone experienced as friction. If the system did not predict the image to process or had no target that depended on it being fully comprehensible, however, the same failure to produce high level objects could remain closer to baseline rather than producing noticeable friction.

This demonstrates that things are not simply “there” and received in their completed form. They are actively constructed as lower level features and relations are organized by the field. In this image, many of the necessary low level tokens are present, but the whole never comes alive because no available interpretation satisfies enough of the relevant properties the field is holding. It is plausible a differently trained field might possess chunks that allow all objects in the image to correctly process if the organization fits neatly.

If the image were gradually morphed into a recognizable object, say through an animation, the external conditions supporting the gestalt would change until the object became valid to the current field. If this resolution happened more easily than the system predicted, it would produce a positive difference in felt tone experienced as resonance. If the resolution of the morph was fully expected, it might simply return the system to baseline.  When the viewer takes actions such as changing their perspective to reduce uncertainty, those actions can be understood through active inference because the system is actively using the image in ways expected to produce a valid interpretation.

This same general process appears in a lot more cases than just weirdly designed images like the one I presented. A sentence can contain familiar words while failing to produce a coherent, whole meaning. A math problem can contain individually understood pieces while its organizing structure remains questionable. 

The same general process appears in less obviously perceptual cases. A sentence can contain familiar words while failing to produce a coherent meaning. A mathematical problem can contain individually understood pieces while its organizing structure remains unavailable. An emotional experience can contain recognizable sensations like a fast heart beat while the person remains unable to interpret what they mean as a whole. In every case, lower level recognizable features are available while the relevant higher order gestalt fails to stabilize. And in every case, we have the ability to “morph” the relations of the features, and the features themselves so a whole gestalt can be recognized. For instance in a sentence that has recognizable words but produces friction, we can morph it into a form that registers in our system as valid, and sometimes resonant.

More precisely, this kind of morphing can be understood as a structure-preserving transformation. When rewriting a sentence, the intended meaning and important relations act as the structure we are trying to preserve, while properties like word choice and order remain open to change. Each reformulation becomes a probe generated by the Loop of Refinement which then is tested for resonance or friction. The goal in this instance is to find a notational form that preserves enough of the original embodied or representational structure for a coherent gestalt to be reconstructed.

This is also why we cannot fully instantiate a gestalt for which our system does not yet possess the needed priors or chunks. We may be able to construct an approximation using existing structures, like guessing what we might feel in a novel situation, but the actual organization cannot become available until its prerequisites have been learned and can be in relation together. Failed gestalts therefore show emergence in reverse since the organization has not yet become stable enough for a valid whole to appear. 

The next question is why those conditions can develop gradually while the resulting gestalt made from those parts seems to arrive all at once.
