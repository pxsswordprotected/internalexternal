> "For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g., men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts."
> 
> – Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*

Through this statement, Aristotle asserts that practice is of utmost importance, for without practice, we cannot obtain higher virtues. We cannot think our way through getting better at a skill, just as we cannot become just by thinking about justice. The thoughts and reasoning themselves can contribute but only if practice is enacted, for without practice, no demands are made onto the world, and our field cannot cultivate those results.

This framework gives a clear and intuitive picture as to why practice matters so much: the field can be trained by cumulative experience. The current field our system holds and uses has been shaped and handled by a compressed history of what we ourselves have repeatedly perceived, attended to, believed, enacted, and received feedback about. Practice matters because it molds the field in a way that will determine how later input is reasoned about, interpreted, and acted on. It is one of the most controllable mechanisms we have over the field, which is crucially what gives humans a unique edge over other living systems. We have the ability to use tools like reasoning and representations, whether internal or external, to enlarge what can be called the '[cognitive light cone](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02688/full)' of living systems. Practice then not only is important for pragmatic applications of theoretical thought, but inspecting and using the field itself to result in self-authorship, an ability to reshape the substrate experience relies on. We need to practice wisdom to be wise, to practice seeing construction of gestalts to hold transparency, to practice not habitually contracting so we don't suffer.

Practice then is repeated engagement that directs attention and awareness across varying spatial and temporal levels, using feedback to retune the field's dispositions and dynamics to match our targets. Practice can vary across differing levels of precision weighting, not just repetition. Experiences can be weighted differently due to factors like mood and situational context, so a few highly weighted practice moves can be more influential than many weak ones. Two people can practice the same thing for the same amount, but come away with different results. Thus attention plays a role: what we weight and attend to when practicing affects field retunement. Practice is selective training of the field because of this, not just passive absorption. Temporally, practice can work across multiple timescales through attention and awareness. Moment-to-moment attention changes is what is available right now, dwelling on something retunes the current field, repetition changes parameters and deeper priors, and "offline" periods like consolidation while sleeping supports structural and implicit changes. These varying levels offer a reason why insight by itself without practice is not enough: insight may alter the moment-to-moment field, but not the deeper underlying properties of our default field that regenerates future states.

Tying this to the compression gradient, different depths of knowledge change at different speeds which matters for the amount of practice you must do to reach a target. Notation can change quickly like rewriting a sentence, updating an internal model can be harder due to interacting parts, and embodied knowledge or deep priors can take the longest since the body is where our deepest compression lives. A single piece of feedback containing contradiction you must update towards will not instantly update your tacit knowledge that lives in the body, but it can update your notational expression. This is field dissonance coming into play, since different levels of the compression gradient update differently, so they may seemingly be at odds.

When it comes to slack and practice, holding enough of it is of utmost importance so updating and action can be done as effectively as possible. Slack is a prerequisite itself for corrective practice, since it allows the field to hold error which is competing branches across the dependency tree. If we cannot hold error without immediately resolving towards habitual interpretations and active inference, we will not be able to gradually refine them. Without enough slack, repetition tends to reenact existing habits instead of producing exploration or updating. And as stated in the slack section, it is specific to the task at hand, slack is not just one out-of-context thing. Someone can access abilities in one field configuration while being unable to access it under states that reduce slack, like anxiety or social pressure. Thus, the needed capacity for practice to be effective needs to be reachable during the practice itself. This emphasizes the importance of mediums that increase channels of slack like routines, rituals, external notation, and supportive friends, because these things reduce background uncertainty and preserve more capacity for ideal updating. On the inverse, a volatile environment requires more slack in certain areas for the same updating to occur. We can also talk about slack in terms of the domain and problem space the practice is being done in. Too few constraints on the practice creates a very large search space of possibilities that leaves too many branches open in the field, too many constraints and you force premature collapse, preventing diverse practice or solutions that explore other branches. Good practice provides enough structure to make search navigable while preserving enough capacity to explore dynamic possibilities you otherwise would not.

From the loop of refinement section, a natural cycle or path for practice looks like this:

> begin with a felt sense of understanding → turn that into a probe you can test → test the probe against the field or external structure → notice resonance, friction, or a baseline felt sense → locate what causes the response → update the representation or notation → begin again with the new felt sense of understanding

Crucially, you cannot force an embodied recognition of the updating of new knowledge. The embodied layer only comes from practice that is weighted enough. This is why notation and representations are crucial, because without them, our range of knowledge would be very limited in terms of the embodied layer. Because of this, making implicit material stable enough through notation and representations is crucial. A vague felt sense cannot easily be revised until it is made objectified. Saying this, not all practice necessarily involves using this loop exactly as stated, but the general mechanisms still drive change. For example, taking wanting to practice a conversational skill, this is still stabilizing a felt sense through words. The words, the notation, is making the implicit stable so you can understand your current positioning. Without this, we may assume we can just enact the skill at any moment without practice, but when we try in a real situation, we come to realize our habitual interpretations and action take over without deliberate weighting, marking the importance of practice in general, and more specifically testing probes so you can really see where your knowledge breaks down.

Regarding resonance and friction when it comes to practice, these two signals simply measure surprise of integration cost, not truth or rightness. These two signals are useful guides, but taken to their extreme we get delusion and gradual habituation of branches in the dependency tree that may take us away from our long-term targets. A "false" idea can resonate through familiarity, and "true" information can produce friction, all the more reason to not let these things hold high ontological authority. Just as these things cannot give us accuracy about truth, they do not identify what is wrong or right. Resonance or friction may arise from the embodied, representational, or notational layer. It may also come from external evidence or be manipulated by lack of slack, so it is up to us to identify the source. Effective practice must diagnose the thing that was violated or satisfied by input before deciding what exactly to update. This is where open loops come into play because without them, repetition simply becomes self-reinforcement. Open loops introduce external feedback that otherwise could not be generated from the field doing the practice, meaning we get to explore branches of the dependency tree that would otherwise not be available for us to use.

The mechanics of practice and what it structurally changes is crucial to understand. Repetition with enough weighting turns recurring relations into chunks. When several priors activate together repeatedly, form a stable relation, and support successful action, the system compresses them into a single unit. The parts no longer need to be integrated separately and can be used together automatically. What this does is compound into chunks reducing more and more future search and integration costs. A chunk that has been used a good deal in practice shrinks the search space and frees attention assuming targets remain stable. Of course often this isn't the case as chunks allow us to explore targets that increase in their temporal and spatial qualities. For instance, learning new chunks in math now allows us to explore harder problems that otherwise would not be possible, naturally our system gravitates towards these problems to satisfy underlying targets. This also means practice makes chunks return to our baseline perception. As an organization becomes predictable and cheap to integrate, it stops producing obvious resonance or friction and becomes a part of our default field. This frees capacity, but it also means fluency is not a signal that new learning is occurring. It is also not a signal the chunks that are being integrated are not accurate, which is a danger. Every repetition of using a chunk makes it cheaper, but it also makes more depend on it. Practice deepens ontological authority, making the gestalt have more and more organizational power. If lots of things depend on inaccurate chunks, then relearning the thing will be costly to the system, making it much harder to pursue.

This is why ease alone is not enough for learning and effective practice: integrating input that is not costly in some way means we already have stored chunks related to that thing that are stored in the baseline. Slack is meaningful here when talking about deliberate practice, "[mindful repetition on performance tasks just beyond the edge of one's capabilities.](https://www.justinmath.com/files/advice-on-upskilling.pdf)" Difficulty by itself does not produce learning. Repeated perceived failure may update conditions towards threat detection, helplessness, or a freezing of committing to action policies. On the inverse, frictionless repetition of practice may deepen automaticity but cease producing refinement on one's edge of capabilities. Of course there are varying levels of worry here based on targets, like maybe some knowledge is needed to be conditioned deeply into the embodied layer so it's automatic, while some only needs to be brought up in a representation when needed.

It is crucial to understand the role of memory here because practice changes memory, and memory changes what can be perceived, constructed, and done. Thus, practice changes the problems the field can effectively understand and solve. Long-term memory, an outcome of practice, is not a simple stored thing then. Learning is a durable change in the dispositions from which future fields are constructed, influencing the configurations themselves. If we were to hold an account for the act of remembering something, we could say it is the moment-to-moment reconstruction of a gestalt from those dispositions under current cues like targets and constraints. Because of this, properties of the field like reachability matter because they determine if you can access a field configuration you need under certain cues. Something can be retained but inaccessible due to lack of slack, or unfamiliar framing. A configuration can be reachable under the present cues, but be inaccessible when those cues disappear. Therefore understanding invariants, factors that stay the same across problems, is important as to not be reliant on specific cues. Practice should remove support that will not be there later, like reference material, though this [depends on the thing you are practicing](https://jonabram.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2968/2018/11/Blakey-et-al.-2019-judicious-use-of-SBs.pdf). Spaced repetition or notational generation can test whether the field can reconstruct the correct structure after the temporary context has disappeared. This highlights the utmost importance of prerequisites when it comes to learning and therefore practice, because without the proper ones in place, the field configuration we need to access is literally unreachable based on the current priors we hold. As Justin Skycak puts it, "Having your prerequisites in place is the difference between something seeming confusing and inaccessible versus 'wait… that’s all it is?'"

The novice and expert therefore do not merely apply different amounts of reasoning to the same represented problem, their fields reconstruct the problem entirely different. As Terence Tao says, "You’re so certain that a certain approach is going to work or a certain answer is going to be what you expect. So, sometimes asking the right question is key to actually getting a satisfactory answer." The expert will see relations, invariants, constraints, and available actions that the novice literally doesn't perceive, not because of magic, but because practice puts into place the proper dependency tree pathways. This highlights the importance of having fundamentals in a domain heavily conditioned, since all advanced and more specific chunks will depend on these fundamentals. Having fundamentals in place means lowering integration costs across the board against a wide variety of input, making later learning and integration easier. This leaves more slack for our system to handle uncertainty and higher-order possibilities. Based on the dependency tree model of how priors work, it can be said a great deal of learning is a matter of settling and connecting prerequisite priors into unified gestalts, each being a step towards greater understanding. Additionally, experts compared to novices do not merely perceive larger patterns than the novice with more nodes, but they can also distinguish differences within those patterns that the novice's field collapses together.  This resembles the skill of sensory clarity discussed earlier: sensory clarity acts in the embodied layer to de-chunk a unified gestalt of sensations into its changing smaller parts, while expertise in a domain does the same thing with knowledge. The expert can notice fine details the novice might blackbox away and notice which parts of the chunks they are using are causing error.

When it comes to training data, learning, and practice, meaningful abstractions can be built from concrete examples which makes them valuable. Encountering an abstraction, like a broad mathematical definition, gives the field little input to compress and work into itself. Practice with concrete examples therefore makes the compression wider, which has a higher chance of settling knowledge into the embodied layer. For the compression gradient, this is turning notation into representations, then folding this into the embodied. We can fold concrete examples into representations and properties of the field like constraints and variables, and by doing so we chunk these things together into increasingly efficient gestalts that organize experience. This knowledge can then be expressed back through notation, allowing us to share it for feedback. This is why memorizing a definition without true understanding can occur: the symbol, the notation, exists but is not held up by the lower layers through relational dynamics which therefore cannot be applied to new problems that have not been practiced. Without a representation behind a definition, how can we conserve the properties the definition holds and apply it elsewhere? High quality training data allows us to practice holding structure and underlying chunks which can be applied to new input. This is the skill of structure-preserving transformations– comparing several things and detecting which properties hold across them. Practice of a thing will change context, cues, and examples, and using structure-preserving transformations trains the field to preserve and reinstantiate the relations and variables that hold, allowing us to generalize across situations. Abstractions can remain connected to intuition, hold structure across varying context, and connect in novel ways if we so choose to practice.

Practice therefore is not merely a means to an end, like becoming competent. It is how the field participates in its own construction and perception. Every repetition gives weight to a way of seeing and acting, making some configurations easier to reach while making others difficult to even see. Thus, practice is never entirely neutral as it is training us to have new dispositions that influence our consequences onto the world. We can practice towards ethical behavior, compassion, or wisdom. Or we can practice towards harm, contraction, greed, or delusion. It can make either of these categories fluid and easy to use. The important question alongside how we can effectively practice is what we are practicing towards, which is what the section on wisdom is getting at. As we return to wisdom, it's important to consider how we can translate practice into its domain. Sanity gives us the transparency and flexibility to revise the field, as to not hold current models so rigidly. Wisdom orients that flexibility towards targets worth pursuing. Yet wisdom cannot be described here as a finished model we install and work towards with practice. It takes reorientation and refinement on its path. We must practice acting decisively without contractive defense, and to not hold gestalts with high ontological authority as to remain open to feedback without losing direction. If, as Aristotle states, we become builders by building, then we become wise by doing the things that construct wisdom, and thus it becomes an orientation the field holds.
