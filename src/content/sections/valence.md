I'd like to introduce a definitive way to understand valence in terms of phenomenological experience. I'm not here to posit a working formal theory that's more \<ontological?> like [The Symmetry Theory of Valence](https://opentheory.net/2021/07/a-primer-on-the-symmetry-theory-of-valence/), but rather I want to understand how it works in subjective experience, and how we can take advantage of its working principles to increase our well-being. What this requires is working compressions that survive test cases like jhana states, feelings of mixed valence, and \<>. I intend to provide these working principles that work within my established framework here, which also explains things like dips in valence due to target-error and valence of predictive costs vs actual costs of integration of input, as discussed in section 2.1.

Essentially, we can determine felt valence by determining the cost of integrating input into the field, given a slice of time. It feels worse the more the input conflicts with the field's predictions and targets, weighted by their precision, and it feels better as that cost falls. This is a working principle for ordinary experience. States that act on the substrate directly, such as drugs, are not accounted for.&#x20;

Again, this is a high-level phenomenological descriptor of what we can track of valence in most states, and I'm sure there are lower-level explanations that might be more consistent in their predictive nature. For now, we can find some insights that fall out of this descriptor which inform us of pragmatic ways perception deals with valence.

For one, I already state multiple descriptors throughout that influence valence, but are not deterministic. For instance, in Section 2.1 (The Energetic Triad) I state “This is not a complete theory of feeling tone but one proposed contributor to phenomenological experience” and “All this supports the fact that results of the difference between expected cost and actual integration cost of input is one contributor to the felt tone, or valence, of the field rather than felt tone itself”, and in Section 3.4 (Resonance & Friction) I say “Because an input’s relation to a target can also produce positive or negative felt tone, not every pleasant response is resonance and not every unpleasant response is friction.” Importantly, these fall out of the working definition above.&#x20;
