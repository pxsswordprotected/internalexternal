Chunking can be seen as a way we save energy by compressing and encoding information so that its structure is preserved while its full richness only needs to be revealed when necessary. It is the process by which parts that once had to be integrated separately get compressed into a single unit that the field then treats as one thing. The process of chunking forms chunks which are learned, reusable compressed units that reduce future processing or search cost. The mechanism of precision helps here in which we can prune many branches of the dependency tree into one usable unit, which shrinks the search space of interpretations and frees attention for other input. The current experiential result we perceive is a gestalt: the unified whole that appears in the present moment when priors are organized and glued together which becomes instantiated in experience. A novel image can suddenly become a gestalt even though you have never seen it before which requires existing priors.

If you encounter and use that organization of the novel image repeatedly, the system may store its relational structure as a chunk. This results in feedback loops of repetition, stable relational structure, and successful action that confirms our predictions.

To put this in terms of the dependency tree, lower-level priors predict separate characteristics and relational dynamics, and when input provides evidence for several of them to activate at the same time, such as shape, expected use, interactions, and weight, chunking compresses those priors into a higher-level one that can be used as one unit. When that higher-level prior successfully organizes current input, meaning the underlying lower-level priors make sense together, the field experiences the result as a gestalt. For example, if we take a water bottle, the high-level prior of “water bottle” is a gestalt formed from lower level priors that are chunked like shape and usage that interact together successfully,

Repetition builds the priors that let a bundle of parts resolve cheaply as a single gestalt, which is why mathematicians can parse a problem and have several pieces of knowledge quickly come online. Experts do not simply perceive richer chunks; they possess better-calibrated chunks and greater flexibility in moving between the whole and the details it leaves out when needed. Chunking also occurs across time, not just between the parts of an object in a single moment. The sense of a stable “now” is the field binding a stream of changing sensory input into one continuous gestalt, the same compression that pulls spatial parts into one thing applied to temporal change. This is what it means for deep priors to be re-instantiated moment to moment: a prior is a persistent dispositional structure (stored in some substrate not yet understood) that must still be actively reproduced among the field to be present in a given context. The trunk feels permanent because the regeneration is stable and reliable, not because nothing is stored somewhere. This means the trunk can change, since a truly permanent foundation could never shift. Skills like sensory clarity can de-chunk temporal gestalts, like explicit slices of time, allowing experience to appear as changing sensations and events rather than one continuous thing. Because chunks make experience practical, the goal is not to eliminate them but to recognize what characteristics they omit and regain access to their components when the current gestalt stops serving you.

Repetition of field configurations and their reinforcing loops produces attractors. These are configurations the system repeatedly settles into because its components reinforce one another, whether or not the system consciously prefers it. The dependency tree explains which structures support a given field configuration, while attractors explain why that configuration persists against competing options. When the same chunks repeatedly activate together, the field begins treating their relations as one even larger gestalt, so activating one part helps reinstate the rest. As this gestalt becomes easier to reproduce than competing configurations, it gains stability and increasingly pulls the field back toward itself. For example, an anxiety attractor might contain bodily arousal, threat-focused attention, catastrophic interpretation, and less precision on corrective evidence. This differs from targets because the person’s current target may actually be safety or calm, but the policies used to reach safety can reinforce the anxiety attractor. An attractor is also not one singular prior. It is a recurrently stabilized field configuration produced by interacting priors, precision weights, bodily states, attention patterns, environmental cues, and policies.

Attractors then have their own attractor basins which are ranges of states from which the system still returns to the same configuration. A person does not have to be fully anxious to remain inside an “anxiety basin” because if certain co-arising conditions come alive in relation to each other, like bodily arousal and threat signals from environment, they can pull the rest of the field back online. Basins can differ in both width and depth. A wide basin can capture the field configuration from many different starting states, while a deep basin strongly resists disruption once held. Repetition, bodily reinforcement, environmental regularity, and enactment of associated policies can widen or deepen a basin, while corrective evidence, new chunks, alternative policies, and increased slack can make competing configurations more available for usage. The boundary between basins acts like a threshold because gradual changes may accumulate without an obvious shift until the current pattern stops reinforcing itself strongly enough and another pattern takes over. Since active field configurations and mind-body states select for biased evidence and action to reduce target and prediction error, entry into and exit from an attractor may also require different conditions like environment. A small threat signal may trigger an anxiety attractor, while leaving it may require strong safety signals which can be much harder to perceive and maintain than the conditions that led to the attractor itself. This asymmetry of entry and exit is a form of path dependence called hysteresis.

Hysteresis in this instance, the asymmetrical effort of entry and exit of states, is one formulation of a more general asymmetry that can show up: collapsing many possibilities into one is "cheap" in effort because many paths lead to one collapse, while gaining back the states or information that was left out of the collapse is "expensive" because you must rebuild many out of one. Another formulation of this generality is when we compress from the embodied layer to notation, since compression is less time consuming and "cheaper." Putting an embodied feeling into language is quick; taking language you've never seen before and moving it to the embodied layer, moving from one-to-many, is slow and tedious.

Field configurations also come with a property of reachability. This describes whether a viable path to the attractor exists under the field’s current available actions. Two attractors could have the same width and depth while differing in reachability because one is separated from the current state by lacking sufficient actionable moves from where the system is currently at. Reachability is load-bearing for configurations because changing it means changing what actions are available by the system to move from one state to another. If a property, like felt tone, of a field makes it so the reachability of a state is difficult to climb to, the property is load-bearing for that configuration. This becomes especially important when considering psychological changes in which barriers block different pathways that would usually be open if not for load-bearing properties that have been conditioned which result in persistent attractors.

When many possible trajectories depend on one such constraint, reachability becomes load-bearing, since changing that constraint reorganizes what the field treats as possible.

Field configurations also differ in what they make reachable: basin depth describes how difficult an attractor is to leave and basin width the range of states it captures, while reachability concerns whether the current constraints and available actions permit a viable path to it at all. When many possible trajectories depend on one such constraint, reachability becomes load-bearing, since changing that constraint reorganizes what the field treats as possible.

Because our perception and actions depends on the priors and chunks currently available, it is impossible to fully instantiate and realize a gestalt that we do not have the prerequisites for. We can imagine an approximation, a proxy gestalt, using existing structural dependencies, but the actual organization can only become available after new priors have been established and are available for precision. More on the importance of this later.

Lastly, it is important touch on the idea of memory as a construct and what is going on there. Priors in the dependency tree are something like persistent dispositions, which are real structural changes left by past experience and conditioning. However, there is a core difference in what actually “shows up” moment-to-moment as memory instead of it just being priors interpreting what’s going on. It is more like the field constructing a gestalt in the present moment, using what resources are available and what the person-environment context gives you. This means access of memory can vary independently of what is being recalled or retained, since context can influence the field’s ability to hold certain things and structure. So a disposition can be present and shaping behavior and representations while remaining unavailable to explicitly access. This is why context like mood and state change what can be reached in the moment by the mind-body system. Episodic recall is therefore the same construction process as all other gestalts using past experience. The sense that a thing is a categorically different type of thing called a memory, rather than an internal representation, is itself a gestalt formed from features like temporality and familiarity rather than a separate, unrelated thing. This is why a memory can can be the cause of reactivity that would occur as if a thing were happening, while being wrong, and why rumination gives way to attractor states rather than describing one. As Michael Levin frames it, because our mind-body-environment system changes over time with context and targets, a trace is closer to a message that has to be remapped to the current system rather than a solid, unchanging thing, which internal representations are not.
