One of the main ways an abstract thing becomes meaningful to the mind-body system is through metaphor. Metaphor is a translation mechanism that dates back to the origins of language and is a part of the representational layer. Philosophers have long argued that early humans did not invent literal words first and embellish them later; rather, they used the physical world around them as an immediate landscape to grasp abstract realities. This marks the importance of metaphor: it is one of the clearest forms of content within the representational layer.

In Metaphors We Live By, George Lakoff and Mark Johnson establish an important thesis: “Metaphor is pervasive in everyday life, not just in language but in thought and action. Our ordinary conceptual system, in terms of which we both think and act, is fundamentally metaphorical in nature.” What they mean is that metaphors are not merely poetic devices or devices used in language, but they are unconscious structures that shape how we perceive reality, interact with others, and make decisions. We use language because we conceive of things that way, and we act according to how we conceive of them.

Returning to the example of “argument is war,” when we are having a disagreement, we can experience the argument as a battle. This is the act of conceiving a thing a certain way, which changes our actions due to changing field configurations. Using the war metaphor, our actions jump to winning and defending, the same way we would when fighting an enemy. If we were to instead use the metaphor “argument is a dance” on a societal level, our behavior might change to a more balanced and aesthetically pleasing interaction. The main point stands: metaphor in the representational layer shapes both the notational and embodied layers through language and behavior.

The embodied layer here plays a sneaky but important role: it is where metaphor earns its place. “Argument is war” works because you already know, in your body, what war-like conflict feels like, such as the bracing and the charge. “Time is money” works because you have dealt with scarce resources and felt the weight of using and running out. The metaphor makes the abstract felt sense intelligible by mapping it onto embodied structure; it is not arbitrary. This is what Johnson calls an image schema in The Body in the Mind: a recurring bodily pattern like containment, balance, or moving along a path, laid down by physical experience before any words. A felt sense that has no internal representation of its own can be made explicit through metaphor by using the embodied layer as crucial feedback. So the chain runs bottom to top, where the embodied layer supplies the felt sense, metaphor maps that embodied structure onto an abstract target to build a representation, and that representation is expressed through notation as language and enacted through the embodied layer as behavior. Without the embodied layer grounding it, the metaphor would not mean anything to the system; it would be hollow. The reason “argument is war” can move us to defend and attack is that the body already knows what defending and attacking feel like. We can also trace the stack back down as we process the notation, moving into the image of war, which activates the embodied characteristics associated with war. To give one more example, “feeling down” or “being on top of the world” relies on your physical experience of gravity, where standing upright can feel powerful and collapsing can feel disempowering.

Going back to mathematics to demonstrate metaphor’s importance for understanding, mathematics is a domain in which many of its objects are not directly sensory (you can’t touch a number), which is why understanding them often depends on grounding through metaphor and the embodied layer. This is why mathematicians reach for spatial and physical language constantly. As William Thurston puts it, “People have amazing facilities for sensing something without knowing where it comes from (intuition); for sensing that some phenomenon or situation or object is like something else (association); and for building and testing connections and comparisons, holding two things in mind at the same time (metaphor). These facilities are quite important for mathematics. Personally, I put a lot of effort into ‘listening’ to my intuitions and associations, and building them into metaphors and connections. This involves a kind of simultaneous quieting and focusing of my mind. Words, logic, and detailed pictures rattling around can inhibit intuitions and associations.” This illustrates why the notational layer is the “weakest” of the three in terms of richness: it can never capture the full felt sense of the underlying internal representation.

As a reminder, the representational layer is not only composed of metaphor. What matters is the general principle shared across the entire layer, which is that understanding is built by constructing a representation that fits the field. This could be a metaphor taken from the body, an image, or a mental simulation. Metaphor is simply the clearest usage of representation because it maps bodily structure onto the abstract, but it is just one method among several for developing representations within the layer where understanding actually resides.
