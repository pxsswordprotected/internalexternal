I want to touch on the importance of training data and why high quality training data is so important. By training data, I mean the books, articles, art, conversations, social situations, and other experiences that train how our system perceives and acts. In one sense, all sensory input is training data, but I am using the term more narrowly here to emphasize what we consume or repeatedly engage with in ways that shape perception. Your field, which determines what resonates, carries a compressed history of what you have been trained on, in a sense. Because of this, the quality and diversity of your training data strongly shape the loaded distinctions, chunks, relations, and models available to perception, which in turn constrains what you can parse and externalize.

Importantly, training data helps build the field that the Loop of Refinement uses as its substrate while also supplying many of the open-loop constraints through which that field can be corrected. The new information you take in or the feedback you get affects how your model is updated, so bad training data has the potential to erode the corrections the loop tries to make. Put simply, training data gives the evidence from which priors can be learned, while feedback helps determine which of those remain calibrated to reality, or the frame you are working in.

Our fields are not arbitrary, of course. Their structure emerges from the interaction between the trunk of the dependency tree and a lifetime of training data and action. Different instances of training data are weighted very differently depending on their precision, context, repetition, emotional importance, source credibility, and relation to active targets. For example, an “I love you” coming from your mom is going to be weighted much more heavily than a stranger on the internet saying the same thing. And, a fact coming from someone you respect and admire will matter much more to the system than the same fact coming from someone you find un-trustworthy. 

Importantly, training data matters not only because it tunes your field but also because it constrains the structures that the field can generate and recombine. What we can think or express is constrained by the priors and chunks already available to us. Novelty can then arise by recombining, decomposing, abstracting, and reorganizing those structures into configurations that have never previously been instantiated. But then this begs the question: how do new things come to be that seem novel? My answer is that existing chunks and properties can be reoganized while the field makes a new thing out of the configuration, even if in full is has never appeared in training data before. We may also be able to describe a possible state using our current chunks without being able to instantiate the gestalt itself because the priors necessary to organize experience in that way have not yet been learned or cannot be activated on command. For example, a student can understand the formal definition of a math proof, but still struggle to construct one because they lack the underlying processes to know which path to take for construction. 

So your field, which is “the frame” from which we act, is trained through this accumulated history. This gives us a principle of garbage in, garbage out: a field built on narrow or inaccurate training data can produce a resonant, but wrong understanding and the actions that follow from it. Of course, what counts as a bad action also depends on the active field’s targets and predictions. A field trained to anticipate rejection may experience acceptance as friction because the “good” outcome of acceptance violates what the field expected.

This is also why the distribution of training data matters. Remaining inside an echo chamber can produce bad overgeneralization because the field never integrates the variation that would force its properties to update toward a more “complete” model. This results in a type of expert who might be in a domain for a long time but only sees one way to handle things because their training data has been too narrow, resulting in a field with rigid variables and constraints. Broader and well-chosen training data can reduce this kind of overfitting by making the field to distinguish what generalizes from what works only under a narrow set of conditions. A field can therefore become internally coherent and highly resonant while remaining poorly calibrated the training data it has used never forces wise revision. 

Repeated, sufficiently weighted training data can gradually train its interpreted properties into the default field, particularly when those properties are repeatedly reinforced through feedback. What you repeatedly consume and engage with can become part of your baseline perception, felt as reality itself, so repeated training data should be chosen carefully. Because we have some choice in this matter, choosing your training data is partly choosing what your future default field may become if it is repeated and weighted for long enough. As an example, my aesthetic taste has undoubtedly been refined through years of scrolling Pinterest and Tumblr, slowly but surely training the variables and constraints my field uses. Over time, repeated exposure and selection have trained an increasingly high-dimensional aesthetic target into my embodied layer. I do not need to explicitly state all of its properties when evaluating a new image. The accumulated structure first appears as an immediate intuition about what fits, and language comes afterwards to compress that intuition into words.

Quality of training data is important as well. Think of the difference between content optimized to get clicks vs an expert compressing decades of experience into a careful explanation. The higher quality training data can tune the field to finer variables and constraints, sharpening your internal compass for resonance. In this instance, you are not directly downloading the expert’s embodied knowledge. Instead, you are using their externalization to reconstruct some of the chunks and relations that took them years to learn. This can shorten the path, but those structures must still be rebuilt and tested within your own field to reach into the representational and embodied layers. However, low quality training data can also shape the field, only toward inaccurate, shallow, or poorly calibrated structure. Of course, low and high quality depend partly on your target. When the target is truth-making, quality depends on whether the training data helps the field form chunks and heuristics that remain calibrated across different ontological systems. 

This matters especially when the system is attempting to confirm harmful priors like “I am not good enough.” Our system will find confirming training data resonant and disconfirming training data frictional. Bad training data might not feel bad from the inside because false information can still resonate with the existing field. Confirmation bias can orient us away from what is true or good for us while our structural limitations prevent us from seeing the error.

Finally, practice, like solving problems by hand, is important when dealing with training data because it determines what structure is extracted from it. Two people can consume the same training data, like a blog post, and build very different fields or chunks because varying factors like attention and active targets determine what gets conditioned. For this reason, though it is a crucial step, choosing better training data is not enough by itself. It must be paired with active engagement and open-loop correction.
