To develop a deeper picture of The Compression Gradient, we can turn to mathematics, which can reveal what the phenomenology of traversing these layers is like. Fortunately, many great mathematicians and mathematical thinkers have given us subjective reports of what it feels like to use these layers in practice. Ironically, they are reporting on their own tacit knowledge, so these reports will be lossy.

Let’s start with one of the most well-known figures in this space, Einstein. In his response to Jacques Hadamard’s survey of mathematicians’ mental processes, he wrote: “The words or the language, as they are written or spoken, do not seem to play any role in my mechanism of thought. The psychical entities which seem to serve as elements in thought are certain signs and more or less clear images which can be ‘voluntarily’ reproduced and combined.” He continues: “this combinatory play seems to be the essential feature in productive thought, before there is any connection with logical construction in words or other kinds of signs which can be communicated to others.” On the secondary status of notation, he wrote: “The above-mentioned elements are, in my case, of visual and some of muscular type. Conventional words or other signs have to be sought for laboriously only in a secondary stage.” Jacques Hadamard framed the survey around this question: “It would be very helpful for the purpose of psychological investigation to know what internal or mental images, what kind of ‘internal words’ mathematicians make use of; whether they are motor, auditory, visual, or mixed.” Taken together, these reports suggest that at least some epistemic invention is largely pre-verbal, with notation and language coming at a later stage in the process.

Next, let us turn to Alexander Grothendieck. In Récoltes et Semailles, Grothendieck gives us a vivid account of the representational layer: “What drives and dominates my work, its soul and reason for being, are the mental images formed during the course of the work to apprehend the reality of mathematical things... All my life I’ve been unable to read a mathematical text, however trivial or simple it may be, unless I’m able to give this text a ‘meaning’ in terms of my experience of mathematical things, that is unless the text arouses in me mental images, intuitions that will give it life.” Later, he says, “Interrogating things, listening to the voice of things, means trying to imagine them, examining the mental images that form within you, seeking to solidify these images and make them clearer, working at unveiling more and more details, as when you try to recall a dream.” And: “[…] my fundamental difficulty in following the thought of others, without first translating it into my own images, and rethinking it in my own style.” What Grothendieck is most obviously trying to communicate, just like Einstein, is that the representational layer is being manipulated more directly than the notational layer. The notation may be changed, such as by writing or deleting parts of a formula, but it is the representational knowledge that is being expressed and simultaneously updated. This is one example of the bidirectional feedback between representation and notation described earlier.

A more practical example comes from Terence Tao, a leading contemporary mathematician, who shows how a shared internal representation lets two people communicate with far less notation while maintaining richness: “I find there is a world of difference between explaining things to a colleague, and explaining things to a close collaborator. With the latter, one really can communicate at the intuitive level, because one already has a reasonable idea of what the other person’s mental model of the problem is. In some ways, I find that throwing out things to a collaborator is closer to the mathematical thought process than just thinking about maths on one’s own, if that makes any sense.” What he’s trying to say is that if two collaborators already share a vivid internal representation of the thing they are communicating about, their communication can be compressed more heavily because each can reconstruct structure that the other leaves unstated. It is possible to share a rough gesture, and the other person can decompress it correctly using the shared internal representation they already carry. With a peer or someone you are just conversing with, you may lack that shared internal representation, so more of the structure must be made explicit in notation, and the other person must reconstruct a model from that still-lossy expression. Shared context lets you transmit the representational and embodied layers more easily through notation, since the structure of the content does not need to be rebuilt from scratch. The more shared models two people have, the more heavily they can compress their communication without losing usable structure.

Lastly, there is David Bessis and his book Mathematica. He says that “the magic power of mathematicians isn’t logic but intuition,” and that the written proofs we see are downstream products, while the central activity is developing inner intuition through mental imagery, just as Grothendieck describes. Bessis places great stock in intuition; it is the actionable component of layers coming alive within the present context. He argues, “Secret math, also known as mathematical intuition, can be found in the heads of mathematicians. It consists of mental representations and abstract sensations, often visual, that are for them quite obvious, and that give them a great deal of pleasure. But when it comes to sharing these sensations with the rest of the world, mathematicians are often at a loss. What had seemed so evident to them is suddenly less so. To transcribe their ideas, mathematicians have had to invent that esoteric language and those indecipherable symbols, just as musicians had to invent a complex musical notation in order to transcribe their compositions.” That sounds a lot like traversing the Compression Gradient! Mathematicians must, as Bessis puts it, transcribe their representations and intuitions in a legible way so others can understand what they are holding internally. Without this compression, collaboration would not be possible and progress would be lost, so it is a matter of pragmatism. In terms of this framework, Bessis gives the embodied and representational layers a central role when he states, “mathematicians’ intuition is their masterpiece, their lifetime accomplishment.” The vast and rich bottom two layers are so important to this account that the trained intuition they support becomes the mathematician’s deeper accomplishment. I highly recommend Mathematica if you want to learn more about intuition and the representational layer and how they apply to doing mathematics, especially at a high level.

Based on these reports, it is not unreasonable to say that many great mathematicians agree on an implicit truth: notation is not the whole difficult part of the process; it is also knowing when and how the internal representation corresponds with mathematical reality or truth. We see this in Grothendieck’s work to “solidify these images and make them clearer,” Bessis developing intuition until it becomes obvious and provides “a great deal of pleasure,” and Einstein combining images in “combinatory play.” These reports do not provide a complete formal rule or system for generating and evaluating those images. In the pre-formal stage, there is not always an explicit methodology for checking whether a mental image has captured the thing accurately; there is often first an internal sense of direction, like a compass. Formal verification, like proof writing, becomes possible as the intuition is compressed into notation, though feedback from notation can then revise the intuition. This compass lies below the notational layer, most of the time below articulation. Its signal does not guarantee truth; as the intuition section argued, it is only as reliable as the field that produces it. This leads us to a signal the compass gives, something we might call resonance.
