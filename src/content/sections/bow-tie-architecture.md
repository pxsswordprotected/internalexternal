To tie (hah) all of the previous parts together, we can model the synthesis in such a way that is seemingly already existent within different fields of science. This model or architecture is in a shape of a bow tie, with one side starting wide, converging into a middle narrow points, then expanding back to a wide funnel. This type of model was first introduced by Marie Csete and John Doyle in their paper 'Bow ties, metabolism and disease'. This marks the core biological bow tie formulation, [in which nutrients feed into a small core of molecules and then are spit back out into "building blocks" and macromolecules.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15331224/) Expanding on this, [Hiroaki Kitano in his paper 'Biological robustness'](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrg1471) states that "there are specific architectural requirements for robust and evolvable systems — genetic buffering, robust modules and bow-tie architecture. These architectural requirements are the basis for the system's robustness against environmental perturbations, but congruent with genetic perturbations; they facilitate generation of a flexible phenotype." Essentially, robust biological systems stay stable by funneling many different inputs through a small shared core, then use that core to produce many different outputs. Lots of other papers cite this type of architecture in domains from [data analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0004057) to [the internet](https://www.usenix.org/conference/2000-usenix-annual-technical-conference/watching-waist-ip), even if not explicitly spelled out. Most importantly, [Michael Levin uses the bow tie architecture to illustrate how we compress information when learning and reinterpret those related memories in the present moment through construction.](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/26/6/481) Levin directly draws upon many papers already cited in this framework, such as '[Constructive episodic retrieval processes underlying memory distortion contribute to creative thinking and everyday problem solving](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-022-01377-0)' and '[The computational nature of memory modification](https://elifesciences.org/articles/23763)'. He draws upon autoencoders, neural networks that compress high dimensional input into a smaller latent representation and then decode it to reconstruct an output, explaining that “Bowtie architectures feature a low-dimensional compressed medium at the center through which information must come, and active decoding and context-sensitive interpretation on the output.” In the middle of Levin's bow tie is the "bottleneck," the portion of the system that limits data richness, while enabling abstraction and generalization. It is the section that enables constructive reinterpretation of input using "stored" units, memory engrams, which as Levin puts it is essential: "the interpretation by the right side of the bowtie (or the future Self) must be creative, not only algorithmically deductive, in interpreting it in future contexts (as both the environment and body internals shift)." There exists a paper called '[Optimal forgetting: Semantic compression of episodic memories](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7591090/pdf/pcbi.1008367.pdf)' (Nagy, Török & Orbán), in which it is argued that memory works like saving a compressed description that lets you rebuild something similar later. This sounds quite similar to what Levin is essentially arguing for, but interestingly he does not cite it in his 2024 paper. The full 2020 paper from Nagy et al. uses semantic compression, a process for preserving latent structure that is useful for reconstruction, to illustrate how we use a generative model of the world. Essentially, we have rich experiences, we "make" compressed latent representations, then this results in reconstructed memory. They also use "optimal forgetting" to claim that forgetting information isn't simply random deterioration, it is an optimal process of discarding the least useful details. To quote the paper, "If memory resources are to be distributed rationally, this memory decay should not result in random forgetting as information theory provides a principled way of discarding information so that memories degrade gracefully." It should be clear as to how this is related to Levin's paper: it is (in a way) a computational implementation of Levin's bow tie ideas. The paper explains the middle bottleneck in such a way to make sense of it: the bottleneck keeps latent structure or semantic gist rather than every rich detail, which results in a generative model expanding that into what we know as recollection. This is stated in the previous section: "recall is a dynamic act of decompression, meaning through generation, structure, which changes over time, runs in the present moment and generates an output."

It is implied human perception itself, as a whole, can be modeled this way as well. Drawing upon the paper, '[How higher goals are constructed and collapse under stress: A hierarchical Bayesian control systems perspective](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763420306965#fig0030)', it is possible to say that this bow tie architecture can be applied to frameworks like predictive coding and active inference. Goekoop and de Kleijn describe organisms as hierarchical Bayesian control systems with “information bottleneck (bow-tie) network structure.” Even Levin in his 2024 paper states that "this is a kind of top-down causation or pattern completion (low-level mechanisms affected by the higher-level gestalt) akin to predictive processing in cognitive systems." Even more directly, [Levin and Friston have worked on this type of synthesis together in the paper 'Knowing one's place](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsif/article/12/105/20141383/35345/Knowing-one-s-place-a-free-energy-approach-to)'. They model biological morphogenesis using the FEP, arguing that cells behave as if they possess a generative model of the target organism and act to make the actual body conform to that model. Sounds quite constructive to me.\
\
Why is all of this important? Across these models, many inflows of information, whether it be biological information or sensory information, or some type of data points converge into one point, while expanding upon its outflow points. It is not clear as to why exactly this occurs, but a conclusion can be inferred based on a large synthesis across the work already done: the world has more detail than an adaptive system can model through its structure, so surviving requires discovering lower dimensional causes and relationships that preserve what matters and then using those discoveries generatively. We can draw upon the previous parts of this section to see what this looks like from a holistic point of view. This results in something like an open-ended bow tie architecture, in which both ends form a loop, rather than a closed off pipeline going in one direction.

As inferred from the cited and discussed papers, this model uses the same general outline of "raw" input coming in, a bottleneck point is used to process this, then that is decompressed into output, whether it be any of the three layers of knowledge as outlined in the compression gradient, or specific muscle movements. It should be made clear here, that the smallest point of compression here, the bottleneck, is not to be understood as a singular place, but rather a distributed network of structure that operates across differing time scales and levels of abstraction. This can be observed through processes like [hierarchical cortical processing](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.3862?utm_source=chatgpt.com), where sensory regions exhibit relatively short intrinsic timescales while association and prefrontal regions integrate information over progressively longer windows (~50–350 ms), and [memory consolidation](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142050?utm_source=chatgpt.com), where synaptic consolidation occurs over minutes to hours, each operating across its “own timescales.” So, there isn't a clean moment where the first half of the bow tie finishes and hands something to the second half. The bow tie is better imagined as a recurrent cycle than a linear conveyor belt. In fact, from a broad perspective, bow ties can be thought of as continously overlapping in such a way to generate continuously evolving patterns within persistent architecture. Information is constly flowing, but some type of structure must persist in the first place, or we would not be able to find patterns that we can generate from, which would result in the termination of our adaptive system. Ultimately, there are many temporally overlapping processes that "begin" and "end" within each other, resulting in complex system of flows of information.

As stated in the field section, our current field is constantly changing based on rapid perceptual changes. Now, because of this, we must ask the question then how exactly a stitched together continuous experience is perceived if our sensory experience is constantly changing which feeds back into the changing of the structure and field itself, in more shallow ways. To expand on this, it must change in shallow ways (when dealing with small timescales) because if, for example, structure dealing with the sense of self was constantly in flux, our system would end very quickly due to ineffective action within the context it finds itself in. If we keep the changes "shallow", we can take effective action and use the invariant compressed variables produced from the bottleneck to support these shallow changes. This results in relative stability across nested timescales when measuring the reliability of deep priors. This is exactly what's seen on the diagram: the priors closest to the bottleneck point are seemingly the most compressed, as to be processed as quickly as possible. And what this results in is a perceived stitched together continuous experience. Every "new" moment inherits a large amount from the previous moment, like the sense of self. Then nested timescales come into play by varying levels of interpretation: fast variables, like changing light, depend and are constrained by slow variables, like "I am me." Fast variables are constrained by slower ones, meaning their perceptual implications have to fall within the compressed boundaries of slower variables. The light changing cannot be opposite to "I am perceiving this light" if that is a slower prior your system is using. Speaking to the loop, what you just experienced changes your internal state, which changes how next new input is interpreted which changes output. Because many of these processes overlap and occur within each other, we cannot definitely draw boundaries between temporal depths and timescales, like trying to say that was then and this is now. So continuous experience, which is a thing and things are constructed, seems stitched together because each present state of being is a result of multiple complex overlapping processes whose slower variables persist while faster ones continuously change. In this sense, continuity is not the preservation of something frozen, but the repeatead regeneration of deeper invariant relations across changing moments. Each overlapping bow tie cycle is therefore something like a structure-preserving transformation of the last while enough organizing structure remains "the same" for the system to experience sequences of moments as one ongoing gestalt.

It's important to consider what is actually going on at the middle of this process, the point of the most compression, a seemingly narrow bottleneck point of transfer between the two sides of the bow-tie. As mentioned earlier, this is not literally one point on a map that can be pinned down, but a distributed process working across different nested layers of processes. So, how this middle point is viewed really is dependent on what process and temporal variation you're observing, but for now I consider a "singular present moment" in relation to a task at hand. If we consider a target in a given moment, the bottleneck point is a scale-relative point of "maximum" salient compression.

It is the smallest currently sufficient latent organization for whatever target is being considered at that temporal level. More preciesly, it marks the minimum suffiecent target-relevant state.

As a very isolated example, if we are hungry and have a dominating target of findings something to eat, a rotted apple compresses input interpretation towards something like available food, while if we are not hungry and have a target of "find the red object," the property of redness will be more salient instead of the apple acting as a thing to satisfy hunger. Naturally, a question arises about the process occurring in the left side of the bow-tie. As discussed before, going from left to right, the most decompressed input to the most compressed, is a process of distributed hierarchical inference. Sensory systems, like retina and early visual cortex, filter and encode (give meaning to) input, and progressively higher levels infer latent causes, meaning hidden underlying states that best explain the sensory patterns being received, such as inferring an object from changing properties. At the same time (within the bow-tie), [top-down priors](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn0199_79) and current salient targets determine which interpretations matter. Then, it is important to address where those targets come from, since they are essentially dominating our experience. My answer is persistence structure and memory. Structure here meaning the relatively persistent physical organization that makes particular organizing tools, like priors, reproducible. What falls under the category of structure are things like [bioelectric patterns](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/jphysiol.2014.271940) and [patterns of synaptic connectivity/strength](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24298167/). Memory then is what that structure has learned to regenerate, like dispositions. For instance, a learned survival-related target is not regenerated every 100ms, instead the pattern is learned to reinstantiate when current input is encoded in such a way to make that target salient, which then drives compression towards it. How is this known? Because stable behahior persists across moments that do not share context and change sensory states, so the information that regenerates those targets must exist in some slower-changing way rather than being reconstructed from scratch. There's also a plausible survivable/evolutionary reason here: adaptive living systems thatrcan preserve and rapidly reinstate useful structure, which implies targets are in memory, do not have to relearn basic constraints in every moment, making behavior faster and "cheaper."

Next, we must turn to the field and how it relates to the bottleneck point. I posit that the field is what is instantiated now, while memory and structure is what *can* be, filled with optionality. The field is an object of currently instantiated variables, not a hidden latent space. The field is a configuration, emergent from conditions like active priors, context, and bodily state. It therefore determines and tells us what target-relevant compression is working towards and means in a given moment. Theoretically, if we were able to model this field mathematically, it would give us insight into which properties are active and which targets are salient, and therefore which interpretations and actions are most likely to be generated from the present state. Think of the field as an inspectable model of the whole current configuration emerging from input, structure, and memory, rather than as a separate agent within the system. This is not to subtract from the importance of the field, but to make clear that the field is not a separate thing acting on or within the system, but a description of the system’s currently instantiated configuration. Then, the field we know "sits" near or at the bottleneck of the bow-tie, since it is heavily compressed.

Getting into the right side of the bow-tie, we can label this side as "output." This side marks the act of decompression, creativity, and regenerative freedom. Here, the encoded input is spread across in a space of time, meaning we interpret some level of input, dependent on what we're measuring like from 100ms to a year, then the interpretation of the input produces what we output, which would be considered regeneration. This is modeled through the dependency tree: nearest to the bottleneck of the bow-tie lies deep priors which first interpret input, and farthest to the waist lies the most shallow priors, those that are most specific. Decompression can be thought of as going from priors that constrain almost everything, to priors that constrain less and less generation. Goekoop & de Kleijn state something simialr, saying "<>." When considering action and active inference, this process can continue from abstract goals to very specific proprioceptive predictions. This means there is not hard cognition/bodily split in these branches, but rather each branch provides a certain constraint which then provides a certain action to take to meet that branch's prediction. A very simple example might be like seeing a water bottle, interpreting those input signals, broad priors mark the bottle as a separate object, while more specific patterns bring about muscle/reflex activity to reach for the bottle. Again, these patterns are known through the structure and memory that "came before" the interpretation and action in relation to the water bottle. However, it's not that simple of a modeling if we consider overlapping and nested temporal targets and bow-ties.

If we are analyzing an overarching target in a moment, say a target to drink the water, this intropduces nested targets and bow-ties "within" that specific target we are analyzing. Then, the bottleneck portion of the bow-tie we are observing is also relative to the target at hand, whether it be a broad or specific target. Because of this, a slow latent target can constrain many "smaller" targets, which constrain even more fast inference-action cycles of interpretation and action. [Deep temporal active-inference models already consider this](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5998386/), marking the importance of constraints on downstream targets, which emphasizes the need to understand that what we model with this bow-tie architecture is just a slice of what is actually occurring in reality. There is no true "frozen" state of being, instead these nested architectures are constantly running, overlapping deeply. As a  simple example, a target of "solve this math equation" lasts for one minute, while producing many constrained sub-targets like "subtract 5" which branch out of the organizing target and has its own bow-tie, which then produces even smaller targets like hand movements.

It's important to mark the difference between inference and learning, as the two clearly differ in their mechanics. Fast cycles, inference, mostly instantiate and update the current field, while repeated prediction error and "successful" action can more slowly retune the underlying structure and memory that gives way to the field configuration. This emphasizes the importance of studying and understanding structure and memory and how it changes: if we can deeply understand how these things work, we have the potential to do many great things, like speed up memory processes, and [reduce suffering.](https://opentheory.net/2023/07/principles-of-vasocomputation-a-unification-of-buddhist-phenomenology-active-inference-and-physical-reflex-part-i/) [Vasocomputation](https://opentheory.net/2023/07/principles-of-vasocomputation-a-unification-of-buddhist-phenomenology-active-inference-and-physical-reflex-part-i/) is one example of this, as knowing how contracted smooth latches form can give us insight into how that structure gives way to field configurations filled with negative valence. This then draws a (not so hard) boundary between output that becomes new input right away, like bodily signals, and learned structure that eventually becomes persistent across moments of time.

Lastly, and the most interesting idea here, I want to note the importance of invariance and structure-preserving transformations  (SPT for short). This core property is one reason why this architecture works so well for our system. Compression here, moving from left to right in the bow-tie model, is important because it discards transformations that do not matter to the currently weighted target, while preserving those that do matter. What is it meant by transformations? By transformations, I mean changes in the input that can occur while some target-relevant relation remains the same. For example, a water bottle can be moved or rotated while still being interpreted as the same object; the surface level changes are transformations, while the invariant relations, the "bundling together" of priors, useful to the target is preserved. Under SPTs between slices of input, the inference side discards what does not matter for the slice's observed target, making the generative side preserve broad variables, while regenerating context-specific ones. Importantly, these more specific variables are constrained by the broad ones, meaning "water bottle" must come from a branch that is downstream of one that interpretes the input as an object. Equally as important in SPTs is the tracking of action-relevant variation through equivariance. The bottleneck does not "erase" every difference that occurs in input, rather it can preserve both a stable organization and structured information about how its present interpretation has changed. So, a SPT is therefore a change that alters the surface interpretation(s) while preserving some deeper organization that matters to the system because of salience.

We can have:

bottle&nbsp;at&nbsp;angle&nbsp;A→ bottle&nbsp;at&nbsp;angle&nbsp;B

The orientation of the object changes, but object identity and other deeper organizations remain invariant and allow for broader patterns to remain stables. Object-ness stays, while angle calculation, the more specific interpretation, changes.

Invariation's sibling is equivariance. While invariance means that some representation remains stable across a transformation or change to input, equivariance means that the representation changes in a predictable way along with the transformation.

We can have:

bottle&nbsp;at&nbsp;angle&nbsp;A→ bottle&nbsp;at&nbsp;angle&nbsp;B

This bottle rotates, and its identity as a bottle can remain invariant while its represented orientation changes equivariantly. Our subsequent actions will then match that transformation. Our muscles might move in a different way to match the rotation of the bottle, but the identity of the bottle remains the same. We must differentiate between these two concepts because compression does not simply discard all variation. Some variation must remain encoded in an organized form when it is relevant for prediction and subsequent action. In this sense, invariance can emerge through the integration of equivariant changes as the system learns what lower-level variables change together while a higher-level relation remains stable across lots of input.

The inference side of the bow-tie, the side dealing with structure, memory and compression , can be thought of as "preparing" input as for what needs to be preserved and what can be thrown away depending on current context and targets. Certain variables must be preserved so the system can survive, as having to reproduce broad organizing tools from scratch in every single moment would cost too much resources, as in time and energy for example.

The right side then "reverses" this process by using generative freedom. What this means is that the system can take the preserved deeper invariants and branch from those in such a way that looks like creativity. [Michael Levin states that precisely](https://youtu.be/c8iFtaltX-s?si=I_YBxVPChW1V7UeM): “you have to be creative when you interpret your own engrams.” Again, crucially, how agents act creatively is in service towards current concerns, not some universally privileged position. Context, like broader social narratives, is always shaping our creativity, how we use SPTs to navigate complexity. The right side then is creative in the sense that we must search through a space containing a very large amount of possibility, and we must commit to actionable branches. This gives us more understanding about deep priors as well: a deep prior is one that remains stable across a larger range of input and therefore has a larger invariance span. This would be in comparison to a more specific prior, one that is more shallow, which would be more volatile and have a lower invariance span. More specific priors often track how the current paths to a target change within stable constraints or boundaries, making their expressions more equivariant. For example, the broad predictions about a bottle can remain stable while more specific ones of its position or orientation change with the present context. Moving towards the bottleneck of the bow-tie can therefore be seen as increasing invariance span, while moving outward often increases specificity in equivariant transformation and subsequent action. Keep in mind this is a general pattern rather than a strict rule, since invariance and equivariance can be observed at every level of the hierarchy depending on the privileged perspective one is taking.

I believe this line of thinking directly expands to intelligence itself, at least what we know of as intelligence in humans. [William James (indirectly) defines intelligence as "reaching the same goal by different means"](https://ia600909.us.archive.org/33/items/principlesofpsyc01jameuoft/principlesofpsyc01jameuoft.pdf) in the context of goal-directed problem solving under changing context, where the end remains fixed but the path(s) used to reach it can vary. Well, crucially, here the "same goal" can be thought of as an invariant, while the different means can be the specific branches that spawn out of that deeper organization. From this perspective, intelligence involves both discovering what should remain invariant and tracking relevant changes equivariantly to ensure output is matched accordingly. The system must preserve what matters across ranges of input without becoming blind to the specific differences needed for "representation" (the knowing of interpretations) and action. It can then use this combination of broad stability and structured variation in specifics to find different means of reaching the same goal.

Say we have the variables A, B, C. These may look different at the surface, like maybe 3 facts that seem isolated, but intellgence can discover some relation that preserves these 3 facts, so R, the relation, is invariant across all 3. We can then use R to generate a novel variable, D.

This mechanism can be observed across many adaptive systems, such as within salamanders and moths. In the case of salamanders, specifically polyploid newts, extra copies of the genome make individual cells much larger, yet the system still builds a normal-sized kidney tubule by changing how it is constructed. Instead of 8-10 cells coming together to form the tube, one giant cell can bend around itself to produce the same final structure. [As Levin's paper on this states](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37156924/), “in the service of a large-scale anatomical goal, different molecular mechanisms … are deployed as needed for an unexpected change.” The invariant here, the more robust "broad" target, can be thought of as working towards the correct tubule morphology, while the transformation itself comes from cellular implementation. In my view, this is something like a biological SPT, which is structure itself transferring salient information while discarding what is not needed. In regards to the bow-tie, I believe this is ultimately what is occurring on the left side while input is being compressed into a useable point in which the particular system can commit from and find different paths to the same means. The system "understands" from the compression process that what survived is what is valuable, so it can then act that out without extra work. This seems like a very efficient system as there's no need to parse input while creatively attempting reaching an end point (setting aside nested processes and systems). Crucially, invariance, preserving what is important, is always in service towards a target and the scale at play. "This food should not be thrown away" does not have to be fully stable and invariant across contexts. For instance, if you're not hungry, the food might be marked as "okay to throw away" because finding something to eat is not a relevant target at that time. And because of this, sub-targets, like reaching for the food, will remain constrained by their top-down parents, such as wanting to satisfy hunger. This illustrates the importance of understanding behavior and beliefs within complex, nested architectures that are not immediately obvious. It is quite naive, in my opinion, to view generation within a confined, isolated frame not taking into account how input arrives or what other top-down constraints are at play.

Clearly, this idea of the system using SPTs to efficiently compress and decompress maps onto active inference by using targets as active constraints on behavior to reach future preferred states. However, constraints do not mean they lock down the system to just one path, instead the SPT preserves the ultimate target as the end, and policy selection works towards that target and weighs different means. In the dependency tree, many pathways may be available to take, but the system must commit to only one. And linking this to learning, holding and preserving the same variables gives our system, structure and memory, the chance to develop and maintain the dispositions that give way to these important invariants. We can go from a novel solution we've not yet seen, to the process of practice, to maturing the relevant pathways, which leads to the relevant priors being reliably instantiated when they are called for.

Having said all this, invariance on its own does not mean goodness, it is simply the way our system evolved for survival. This idea of intelligence can be good or bad (in relation towards suffering), all depending on the context. A person preserving the organization of "everything is dangerous" across a vast amount of diverse input would be considered generally bad. Same thing with a rigid ideology that is harmful to others: the invariants track, even when coming across opposiing eveidence.

Now, something underlying all of this talk so far has been the bow-tie diagram itself, which must be analyzed from a privield-human perspective which brings about intersting dynamics of modleing. Meaning, specificity, and width of bow-ties can vary depending on the analysis one is performing. This changes the questions one might ask about an organism or adaptivie system, thus it changes the answers you will find, so it is important to recognize how we can cleanly model the whole of something. So, getting into it, it can be said that the size of a bow-tie is relative to the spatiotemporal and problem-space sizing of the target being analyzed. If we are looking at a top-down target of an adaptive system, like "survive," this dictates the sizing of all three levels of the bow-tie: the inference side, the bottleneck, and the generative side. Larger-horizon targets, those that span across a large spatiotemporal scale, require the integration of a broader range of relevant input and can organize larger trees of nested subtargets and information, while smaller-horizon targets integrate and coordinate comparatively local states.

Think of a large-horizon target like "become a doctor." This requires the system to "consider" both larger subtargets, like going to medschool, and extremely specific targets like "reach towards the notebook" for when you're studying. Through this, the system must consider a vast range of input that is being analyzed for what is considered relevant for the long-term target of becoming a doctor, which constrains the nested targets. These nested targets are of course smaller from both a spatiotemporal scale and problem-solving level, how many means to reach the same endpoint, which means the sizing of these bow-ties will be smaller in comparison to those of the constraining, more dominant ones. Because of this sizing difference, we find that the model itself is now constrained in new ways: the overall size of the inference side and the specificity level of the generative side are both scaled in proportion to that of the target and context being considered. When looking at the target of becoming a doctor, the generative side of this bow-tie will be massively specific in terms of ways the dependency tree manifests itself. Looking at a smaller scale, like a cell wanting to maintain ion balance, it's clear both the inference and generative sides will be smaller in scale, meaning the system has to integrate and act on a smaller range of variables and possible states. A larger bow-tie can imply the system is acting on a larger temporal scale, possibly a large spatial scale, lots of nested subtargets, more constraints, and a larger space of knowledge. Crucially, the high-level top-down state of a bow-tie does is not necasily specific in its nature. In fact, these high-level organizing target states are quite abstract. Becoming a doctor is more abstract than "contract this figner muscle," and the reason this is is precicely because it spawns less specific generative output. More abstract constraints drive more long-term specificity. This also appears in the bottleneck section: in the case of the cell, that maximum compression point that is useful to commit from will be massively "small" compared to a much more complex system like a human wanting to become a doctor. In a large bottleneck, many invariants are kept in place and branch out into the generative side, and acted upon through nested trees, making the larger system more specific in its own top-down tree.

And again, these bow-ties continuously overlap, for they do not just cleanly map out in a linear fashion. Slower, large-scale targets constrain faster, smaller-scale inference-cycle. For example, while reading a paragraph, the target of understanding the text overlaps with smaller-scale targets involving eye movements, maintaining attention, and other small-scale targets which unfold together across the same span of time.

Coming off this, I believe Levin's idea of the cognitive light cone fits in neatly. This light cone concept is demarcated as "[the scale of the largest goal, in both space and time, which a system is capable of working towards](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13197194/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)." Originally I had made the assumption that we can simply model a system's light cone by looking at its "whole" bow-tie, but quickly this comes apart when it is recognized that a system does not have one singular bow-tie or target operating at once, even in simple systems that might seemingly have one high-level target spawning nested bow-ties. The cognitive light cone instead marks the outer boundary of the largest-scale target the system is capable of pursuing, not the size of every process running within the system. This naturally produces a question concerning how we know the largest-scale target a system is capable of pursuing. In general, this can become known through testing and tracking what occurs in the system when you prescribe perturbations that offsets invariant variables. Where you can "stop" observation in accordance to the system as a whole, is when you can no longer find evidence that the larger state is being actively restored across many disturbances. Crucially, we must first begin observation with a potential target that might track across many perturbations. Without a starting prediction, it will become impossible to course-correct findings into knowing the relevant highest-level target.

Let's say we have a starting prediction that a person wants to get home safely. We might add in many perturbations like delaying their train, their phone dies, or a stranger talking to them. When these perturbations occur, it is crucial to ask if behavior still reaches towards the target of getting home safely. If the target remains invariant, and nested actions are in service towards the target, we can say that at that level of analysis, the preserved higher-order variable is "getting home safe." Now, to find out the cognitive light code of the whole system, we need to ask a different question. For this, we might start with something like "survival" or "maintain viability." Many lower-level variables change continously, like eating different things, chaning cells, turnover of molecules, or changing internal temperates, while some high-level organi9zation persists, constraining all of these low-level variables. Here though, we must not be careful to confuse this with pure "survival." Here, viability is simply a useful high-level invariant for explaining a broad range of output, but it does not mean the system is acting in agentic ways towards the known mechanism of survival. [Levin specifically notes](https://osf.io/preprints/osf/p23se_v1) that collective cells can scale from local homeostatic variables toward much larger target states such as anatomical configurations, enlarging the spatiotemporal scale of targets they can pursue: "the scaling enables cellular collectives to enlarge their 'cognitive light cone' – the spatio-temporal scale of the target states they are able to pursue." So then, the light cone of what we are observing is like an outer envelope surrounding the largest bow-tie the system maintains across its existence. This implies that a larger cognitive light cone enables the capacity for larger-scale bow ties to exist. In a way, the generative side of the largest bow-tie of an adaptive system can be said to reflect the scale of its cognitive light cone.

If a cognitive light cone of a system specifies the largest spatiotemporal goal the system can pursue, and the scale of a bow-tie  specifies the range of salient variables integrated and possible actions organized for a goal, then the light cone can be treated as an upper bound on the scale of goal-relative bow ties possible to that system.

I want to speak to the physical constraints of this bow-tie architecture. All three parts, the inference side, the bottleneck, and the generative side, are constrained by the system's architecture itself. Every part faces binding constraints that make the system what it is. Different embodiments bring about different measurable variables and offer different affordances on the world. It might be possible to find principles that apply to each part that can give us insight into the system's level of complexity, which determines the sizing of the bow-tie and the system's capabilities. My guess goes like this: bow ties are bounded on the inference side by what they can sense, at the bottleneck by what they can integrate and preserve as target-salient organization, and on the generative side by what they can control. In humans for instance, we can sense light, sound, chemicals, touch, and internal bodily states, we can integrate and preserve a certain amount of information because of structure and memory, and we can control external things through our physical bodies. The cognitive light cone then would be constrained by what makes the bow-tie possible in the first place: physical architecture. One might argue that capabilities that contribute to cognitive light cone size, like human thoughts, are not necessarily "physical." Without getting into arguments surrounding physicalism and emergence, I would push back on this by saying these capabilities are only possible because of the underlying architecture at play, which gives rise to what is possible. This probably means there might be problems in mathematically modeling this, as emergent capabilities are inherently surprising, but nonetheless through privileged external observation, these capabilities are still observable as constraints on what the system is capable of doing. So, if we are trying to find a more general labeling of the three parts that scale-up continuously across all living systems, it might go roughly: observability → internal control state → controllability → loop.
