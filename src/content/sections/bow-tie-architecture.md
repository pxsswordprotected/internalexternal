The world contains more information than an adaptive system can model through its structure, so surviving requires discovering lower-dimensional causes and relationships that preserve what matters and then using those discoveries creatively.

To tie (hah) all of the previous parts together, we can model the synthesis in such a way that is seemingly already existent within different fields of science. This model or architecture is in the shape of a bow tie, with one side starting wide, converging into a narrow middle point, then expanding back to a wide funnel. This type of model was first pointed to by Marie Csete and John Doyle in their paper 'Bow ties, metabolism and disease'. This marks the core biological bow-tie formulation, [in which nutrients feed into a small core of molecules and then are spit back out into "building blocks" and macromolecules.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15331224/) Expanding on this, [Hiroaki Kitano in his paper 'Biological robustness'](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrg1471) states that "there are specific architectural requirements for robust and evolvable systems — genetic buffering, robust modules and bow-tie architecture. These architectural requirements are the basis for the system's robustness against environmental perturbations, but congruent with genetic perturbations; they facilitate generation of a flexible phenotype." Essentially, robust biological systems stay stable by funneling many different inputs through a small shared core, then use that core to produce many different outputs. Lots of other papers cite this type of architecture in domains from [information theory](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0004057) to [the internet](https://www.usenix.org/conference/2000-usenix-annual-technical-conference/watching-waist-ip), even if not explicitly spelled out. Most importantly, [Michael Levin uses the bow-tie architecture to illustrate how we compress information when learning and reinterpret those related memories in the present moment through construction.](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/26/6/481) Levin directly draws upon many papers already cited in this framework, such as '[Constructive episodic retrieval processes underlying memory distortion contribute to creative thinking and everyday problem solving](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-022-01377-0)' and '[The computational nature of memory modification](https://elifesciences.org/articles/23763)'. He draws upon autoencoders, neural networks that compress high-dimensional input into a smaller latent representation and then decode it to reconstruct an output, explaining that “Bowtie architectures feature a low-dimensional compressed medium at the center through which information must come, and active decoding and context-sensitive interpretation on the output.” In the middle of Levin's bow tie is the "bottleneck," the portion of the system that limits data richness, while enabling abstraction and generalization. It is the section that enables constructive reinterpretation of input using "stored" units, memory engrams, which as Levin puts it is essential: "the interpretation by the right side of the bowtie (or the future Self) must be creative, not only algorithmically deductive, in interpreting it in future contexts (as both the environment and body internals shift)."

There exists a paper called '[Optimal forgetting: Semantic compression of episodic memories](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7591090/pdf/pcbi.1008367.pdf)' (Nagy, Török & Orbán), in which it is argued that memory works like saving a compressed description that lets you rebuild something similar later. This sounds quite similar to what Levin is essentially arguing for. [The full 2020 paper from Nagy et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7591090/pdf/pcbi.1008367.pdf) uses semantic compression, a process for preserving latent structure that is useful for reconstruction, to illustrate how we use a generative model of the world. Essentially, we have rich experiences, we "make" compressed latent representations, then this results in reconstructed memory. They also use "optimal forgetting" to claim that forgetting information isn't simply random deterioration, it is an optimal process of discarding the least useful details. To quote the paper, "If memory resources are to be distributed rationally, this memory decay should not result in random forgetting as information theory provides a principled way of discarding information so that memories degrade gracefully." In a way, this is related to Levin's paper: it is a computational implementation of Levin's bow-tie ideas. The paper explains the middle bottleneck in such a way to make sense of it: the bottleneck keeps latent structure or semantic gist rather than every rich detail, which results in a generative model expanding that into what we know as recollection. This is stated in the previous section: "recall is a dynamic act of decompression, meaning through generation, structure, which changes over time, runs in the present moment and generates an output."

It is implied that human perception itself, as a whole, can be modeled this way as well. Drawing upon the paper, '[How higher goals are constructed and collapse under stress: A hierarchical Bayesian control systems perspective](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763420306965#fig0030)', it is possible to say that this bow-tie architecture can be applied to frameworks like predictive coding and active inference. Goekoop and de Kleijn describe organisms as hierarchical Bayesian control systems with “information bottleneck (bow-tie) network structure.” Even Levin in his 2024 paper states that "this is a kind of top-down causation or pattern completion (low-level mechanisms affected by the higher-level gestalt) akin to predictive processing in cognitive systems." Even more directly, [Levin and Friston have worked on this type of synthesis together in the paper 'Knowing one's place'](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsif/article/12/105/20141383/35345/Knowing-one-s-place-a-free-energy-approach-to). They model biological morphogenesis using the FEP, arguing that cells behave as if they possess a generative model of the target organism and act to make the actual body conform to that model.

Across these models, many inputs, whether biological or sensory, converge onto a small shared core, which then fans out into many different outputs. This is the key to understanding the conclusion stated at the start of this section: "surviving requires discovering lower-dimensional causes and relationships that preserve what matters and then using those discoveries creatively." We can draw upon the previous parts written here to see what this looks like from a holistic point of view across a range of systems. This synthesis results in a looped bow-tie architecture, in which compression and generation form a cycle, since what the system outputs changes what it senses next.

As inferred from the cited and discussed papers, this model uses the same general outline of "raw" input coming in, a bottleneck point is used to process this, then that is decompressed into output, whether it be any of the three layers of knowledge as outlined in the compression gradient, or specific muscle movements. It should be made clear here that the smallest point of compression here, the bottleneck, is not to be understood as a singular place, but rather a state carried by distributed structure that operates across differing time scales and levels of abstraction. This can be observed through processes like [hierarchical cortical processing](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.3862), where sensory regions exhibit relatively short intrinsic timescales while association and prefrontal regions integrate information over progressively longer windows (~50–350 ms), and [memory consolidation](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142050), where synaptic consolidation occurs over minutes to hours, each operating across its “own timescales.” So, there isn't a clean moment where the first half of the bow tie finishes and hands something to the second half. The bow tie is better imagined as a recurrent cycle than a linear conveyor belt. In fact, from a broad perspective, bow ties can be thought of as continuously overlapping in such a way to generate continuously evolving patterns within persistent architecture. Information is constantly flowing, but some type of structure must persist in the first place, or we would not be able to find patterns that we can generate from, which would result in the termination of our adaptive system. Ultimately, there are many temporally overlapping processes that "begin" and "end" within each other, resulting in a complex system of flows of information.

It's important to consider what is actually going on at the middle of this process, the point of the most compression, a seemingly narrow bottleneck point of transfer between the two sides of the bow tie. As mentioned earlier, this is not literally one point on a map that can be pinned down, but a distributed state working across different nested layers of processes. So, how this middle point is viewed really is dependent on what process and temporal variation you're observing, but for now I consider a "singular present moment" in relation to a task at hand. If we consider a target in a given moment, the bottleneck point is a scale-relative point of "maximum" salient compression. It is the smallest currently sufficient latent organization for whatever target is being considered at that temporal level. More precisely, it marks the minimum sufficient target-relevant state.

As a very isolated example, if we are hungry and have a dominating target of finding something to eat, a rotted apple compresses input interpretation towards something like available food, while if we are not hungry and have a target of "find the red object," the property of redness will be more salient instead of the apple acting as a thing to satisfy hunger. Naturally, a question arises about the process occurring in the left side of the bow tie. As discussed before, going from left to right, the most decompressed input to the most compressed, is a process of distributed hierarchical inference. Sensory systems, like the retina and early visual cortex, filter and encode (give meaning to) input, and progressively higher levels infer latent causes, meaning hidden underlying states that best explain the sensory patterns being received, such as inferring an object from changing properties. At the same time (within the bow tie), [top-down priors](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn0199_79) and current salient targets determine which interpretations matter.

Then, it is important to address where those targets come from, since they are essentially dominating our experience. My answer is persistent structure and memory. Structure here means the relatively persistent physical organization that makes particular organizing tools, like priors, reproducible. What falls under the category of structure are things like [bioelectric patterns](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/jphysiol.2014.271940) and [patterns of synaptic connectivity/strength](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24298167/). Memory then is what that structure has learned to regenerate, like dispositions. For instance, a learned survival-related target is not regenerated every 100 ms; instead the pattern is learned to reinstantiate when current input is encoded in such a way to make that target salient, which then drives compression towards it. How is this known? Because stable behavior persists across moments that do not share context and change sensory states, so the information that regenerates those targets must exist in some slower-changing way rather than being reconstructed from scratch. There's also a plausible survivable/evolutionary reason here: adaptive living systems that can preserve and rapidly reinstate useful structure, which implies targets are in memory, do not have to relearn basic constraints in every moment, making behavior faster and "cheaper."

Getting into the right side of the bow tie, we can label this side as "output." This side marks the process of decompression and creativity. Here, the encoded input is spread across a span of time, meaning we constrain generation at some level, dependent on what we're measuring like from 100 ms to a year, then that constrained generation produces what we output, which would be considered regeneration. This is modeled through the dependency tree: nearest to the bottleneck of the bow tie lie deep priors which constrain the most output, and farthest to the outside lie the most shallow priors, those that are most specific. Decompression can be thought of as going from priors that constrain almost everything, to priors that constrain less generation and eventually lead to a committed action policy. [Goekoop & de Kleijn state something similar](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763420306965), saying "the output parts then involve multiple outputs diverging from these throughput hubs onto lower level hub structures and so on, down the nested hierarchy to the level of individual nodes."

The same hierarchy of priors is used in both directions of compression and decompression. On the inference side where we compress sensory data, priors "explain" and organize input. On the generative side, the same priors produce predictions, what should happen from the organizations, both perceptual and proprioceptive (action). This same mechanism can be seen in the [Helmholtz machine](https://direct.mit.edu/neco/article-abstract/7/5/889/5898/The-Helmholtz-Machine?redirectedFrom=fulltext), which couples a bottom-up recognition network with a top-down generative network. The recognition network inputs data and infers hidden causes, while the generative network runs the other direction, from those causes back out to the data. The authors note that this "may relate to the function of bottom-up and top-down cortical processing pathways." [Variational autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114) use the same encoder and decoder pairing, describing a similar pattern in neural networks reconstructing data from samples of a compressed latent space.

When considering action and active inference, this process can continue from abstract goals to very specific proprioceptive predictions. This means there is no hard cognition/bodily split in these branches, but rather each branch provides a certain constraint which then provides a certain action to take to meet that branch's prediction. A very simple example might be like seeing a water bottle, interpreting those input signals, broad priors mark the bottle as a separate object, while more specific patterns bring about muscle/reflex activity to reach for the bottle. Again, these patterns are known through the structure and memory that "came before" the interpretation and action in relation to the water bottle. However, it's not that simple a model if we consider overlapping and nested temporal targets and bow ties.

If we are analyzing an overarching target in a moment, say a target to drink the water, this introduces nested targets and bow ties "within" that specific target we are analyzing. Then, the bottleneck portion of the bow tie we are observing is also relative to the target at hand, whether it be a broad or specific target. Because of this, a slow latent target can constrain many "smaller" targets, which constrain even more fast inference-action cycles of interpretation and action. [Deep temporal active-inference models already consider this](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5998386/), marking the importance of constraints on downstream targets, which emphasizes the need to understand that what we model with this bow-tie architecture is just a slice of what is actually occurring in reality. There is no true "frozen" state of being, instead these nested architectures are constantly running, overlapping deeply, and they do not just cleanly map out in a linear fashion. As a simple example, a target of "solve this math equation" lasts for one minute, while producing many constrained sub-targets like "subtract 5" which branch out of the organizing target and have their own bow tie, which then produces even smaller targets like hand movements. Likewise, while reading a paragraph, the target of understanding the text overlaps with smaller-scale targets involving eye movements, maintaining attention, and other small-scale targets which unfold together across the same span of time.

Now we can turn to the field and how it relates to the bow tie. The field is an object containing instantiated variables, not a hidden latent space. It is a configuration, emergent from conditions like active priors, context, and bodily state. A bow tie follows one target across time, while the field "tracks" a cross-section of every active bow tie at one moment, encapsulating targets of "survive" all the way to "move the eyes." In each bow tie, the field is an instantiation of what the right side generates, checked against input on the left side. The bottleneck is not experienced directly. It is instantiated in the field as an essence, a felt sense that is often carried through the body, and the field's specifics are generated from that essence, the exact color and position of each object. It therefore shows what target-relevant compression is working towards and means in a given moment. In a human at a given moment, many bow ties are running, with one or some "at the top" of their model, constraining everything beneath. Which bow ties sit at the top is set by precision weighting, the relative weight of competing predictions including targets (section 1.2). The field is the attempted synthesis of all of them, in a way the most important act of binding our system does.

It's important to mark the difference between inference and learning, as the two clearly differ in their mechanics. Fast cycles, inference, mostly instantiate and update the current field, while repeated prediction error and "successful" action can more slowly retune the underlying structure and memory that give rise to the field configuration. This emphasizes the importance of studying and understanding structure and memory and how they change: if we can deeply understand how these things work, we have the potential to do many great things, like speed up memory processes, and [reduce suffering.](https://opentheory.net/2023/07/principles-of-vasocomputation-a-unification-of-buddhist-phenomenology-active-inference-and-physical-reflex-part-i/) [Vasocomputation](https://opentheory.net/2023/07/principles-of-vasocomputation-a-unification-of-buddhist-phenomenology-active-inference-and-physical-reflex-part-i/) is one example of this, as knowing how contracted smooth latches form can give us insight into how that structure gives rise to field configurations filled with negative valence. This then draws a (not so hard) boundary between output that becomes new input right away, like bodily signals, and learned structure that eventually becomes persistent across moments of time.

As stated in the field section, our current field is constantly changing based on rapid perceptual changes. Now, because of this, we must ask the question of how exactly a stitched-together continuous experience is perceived if our sensory experience is constantly changing, which feeds back into the changing of the structure and field itself, in more shallow ways. To expand on this, it must change in shallow ways (when dealing with small timescales) because if, for example, structure dealing with the sense of self was constantly in flux, our system would end very quickly due to ineffective action within the context it finds itself in. If we keep the changes "shallow," we can take effective action and use the invariant compressed variables produced from the bottleneck to support these shallow changes. This results in relative stability across nested timescales when measuring the reliability of deep priors. This is exactly what's seen on the diagram: the priors closest to the bottleneck point are seemingly the most compressed, so they change the slowest and carry over the most from moment to moment. And what this results in is a perceived stitched-together continuous experience. Every "new" moment inherits a large amount from the previous moment, like the sense of self. Then nested timescales come into play by varying levels of interpretation: fast variables, like changing light, depend on and are constrained by slow variables, like "I am me." "I am me" constrains the act of encoding changing light by assigning each change to one continuous perceiver, so the light reads as changing for the same "me" and not as a new experience each moment.

For instance, light changing in the environment cannot be opposite to "I am perceiving this light" if that is a slower prior your system is using. Speaking to the loop, what you just experienced changes your internal state, which changes how the next new input is interpreted, which changes output. Because many of these processes overlap and occur within each other, we cannot definitely draw boundaries between temporal depths and timescales, like trying to say that was then and this is now. So continuous experience, which is a thing and things are constructed, seems stitched together because each present state of being is a result of multiple complex overlapping processes whose slower variables persist while faster ones continuously change. In this sense, continuity is not the preservation of something frozen, but the repeated regeneration of deeper invariant relations across changing moments. Each overlapping bow-tie cycle is therefore something like a structure-preserving transformation of the last while enough organizing structure remains "the same" for the system to experience sequences of moments as one ongoing gestalt.

Next, I want to note the importance of invariance and structure-preserving transformations (SPTs for short). This core property is one reason why this architecture works so well for our system. Compression here, moving from left to right in the bow-tie model, is important because it discards transformations that do not matter to the currently weighted target, while preserving those that do matter. What is meant by transformations? By transformations, I mean changes in the input that can occur while some target-relevant relation remains the same. For example, a water bottle can be moved or rotated while still being interpreted as the same object; the surface-level changes are transformations, while the invariant relations, the "bundling together" of priors, useful to the target are preserved. Under SPTs between slices of input, the inference side discards what does not matter for the slice's observed target, making the generative side preserve broad variables, while regenerating context-specific ones. Importantly, these more specific variables are constrained by the broad ones, meaning "water bottle" must come from a branch that is downstream of one that interprets the input as an object. Equally as important in SPTs is the tracking of action-relevant variation through equivariance. The bottleneck does not "erase" every difference that occurs in input, rather it can preserve both a stable organization and structured information about how its present interpretation has changed. So, an SPT is therefore a change that alters the surface interpretation(s) while preserving some deeper organization that matters to the system because of salience.

{rewrite start}

We can have:

bottle at angle A → bottle at angle B

The orientation of the object changes, but object identity and other deeper organizations remain invariant and allow for broader patterns to remain stable. Object-ness stays, while angle calculation, the more specific interpretation, changes.

Invariance's sibling is equivariance. While invariance means that some representation remains stable across a transformation or change to input, equivariance means that the representation changes in a predictable way along with the transformation.

We can have:

rotate the bottle by +30°\
identity: "bottle" → "bottle" (invariant)\
represented orientation: A → A + 30° (equivariant)\
reach: grip A′ → grip A′ + 30° (equivariant)

This bottle rotates, and its identity as a bottle can remain invariant while its represented orientation changes equivariantly. Our subsequent actions will then match that transformation. Our muscles might move in a different way to match the rotation of the bottle, but the identity of the bottle remains the same. We must differentiate between these two concepts because compression does not simply discard all variation. Some variation must remain encoded in an organized form when it is relevant for prediction and subsequent action. In this sense, invariance can emerge through the integration of equivariant changes as the system learns what lower-level variables change together while a higher-level relation remains stable across lots of input.

The inference side of the bow tie, the side dealing with compression, can be thought of as "preparing" input for what needs to be preserved and what can be thrown away depending on current context and targets. Certain variables must be preserved so the system can survive, as having to reproduce broad organizing tools from scratch in every single moment would cost too many resources, such as time and energy, for example.

The right side then "reverses" this process by using generative freedom. What this means is that the system can take the preserved deeper invariants and branch from those in such a way that looks like creativity. [Michael Levin states that precisely](https://youtu.be/c8iFtaltX-s?si=I_YBxVPChW1V7UeM): “you have to be creative when you interpret your own engrams.” Again, crucially, how agents act creatively is in service towards current concerns, not some universally privileged position. Context, like broader social narratives, is always shaping our creativity, how we use SPTs to navigate complexity. The right side then is creative in the sense that we must search through a space containing a very large amount of possibility, and we must commit to actionable branches. This gives us more understanding about deep priors as well: a deep prior is one that remains stable across a larger range of input and therefore has a larger invariance span. This would be in comparison to a more specific prior, one that is more shallow, which would be more volatile and have a lower invariance span. More specific priors often track how the current paths to a target change within stable constraints or boundaries, making their expressions more equivariant. For example, the broad predictions about a bottle can remain stable while more specific ones of its position or orientation change with the present context. Moving towards the bottleneck of the bow tie can therefore be seen as increasing invariance span, while moving outward often increases specificity in equivariant transformation and subsequent action. Keep in mind this is a general pattern rather than a strict rule, since invariance and equivariance can be observed at every level of the hierarchy depending on the privileged perspective one is taking.

This line of thinking directly expands to intelligence itself, at least what we know of as intelligence in humans. [William James (indirectly) defines intelligence as "reaching the same goal by different means"](https://ia600909.us.archive.org/33/items/principlesofpsyc01jameuoft/principlesofpsyc01jameuoft.pdf) in the context of goal-directed problem solving under changing context, where the end remains fixed but the path(s) used to reach it can vary. Crucially, here the "same goal" can be thought of as an invariant, while the different means can be the specific branches that spawn out of that deeper organization. From this perspective, intelligence involves both discovering what should remain invariant and tracking relevant changes equivariantly to ensure output is matched accordingly. The system must preserve what matters across ranges of input without becoming blind to the specific differences needed for "representation" (the knowing of interpretations) and action. It can then use this combination of broad stability and structured variation in specifics to find different means of reaching the same goal.

Say we have the variables A, B, C. These may look different at the surface, like maybe 3 facts that seem isolated, but intelligence can discover some relation that preserves these 3 facts, so R, the relation, is invariant across all 3. We can then use R to generate a novel variable, D.

This mechanism can be observed across many adaptive systems, such as within salamanders and [moths](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0001736). In moths, a learned aversion to certain odors can survive the process of metamorphosis even though the original nervous system is largely rebuilt; the memory stays invariant while its implementation changes. In the case of salamanders, specifically polyploid newts, extra copies of the genome make individual cells much larger, yet the system still builds a normal-sized kidney tubule by changing how it is constructed. Instead of 8-10 cells coming together to form the tube, one giant cell can bend around itself to produce the same final structure. [As Levin's paper on this states](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37156924/), “in the service of a large-scale anatomical goal, different molecular mechanisms … are deployed as needed for an unexpected change.” The invariant here, the more robust "broad" target, can be thought of as working towards the correct tubule morphology, while the transformation itself comes from cellular implementation. In my view, this is something like a biological SPT, in which the cells change while the target-relevant structure, the tubule, stays the same.

In regard to the bow tie, this shows both sides working together. The inference side compresses input into a usable point from which the particular system can commit, and the generative side finds different paths to that same end. The system "understands" from the compression process that what survived is what is valuable, so it does not have to rediscover what matters before acting. This seems like a very efficient system as there's no need to find structure from scratch while creatively attempting to reach an end point (setting aside nested processes and systems). Crucially, invariance, preserving what is important, is always in service towards a target and the scale at play. "This food should not be thrown away" does not have to be fully invariant across contexts. For instance, if you're not hungry, the food might be marked as "okay to throw away" because finding something to eat is not a relevant target at that time. And because of this, sub-targets, like reaching for the food, will remain constrained by their top-down parents, such as wanting to satisfy hunger. This illustrates the importance of understanding behavior and beliefs within complex, nested architectures that are not immediately obvious. It is naive to view generation within a confined, isolated frame not taking into account how input arrives or what other top-down constraints are at play.

Clearly, this idea of the system using SPTs to efficiently compress and decompress maps onto active inference by using targets as active constraints on behavior to reach future preferred states. However, constraints do not mean they lock down the system to just one path, instead the SPT preserves the ultimate target as the end, and policy selection works towards that target and weighs different means. In the dependency tree, many pathways may be available to take, but the system must commit to only one. And linking this to learning, holding and preserving the same variables gives our system, structure and memory, the chance to develop and maintain the dispositions that give rise to these important invariants. We can go from a novel solution we've not yet seen, to the process of practice, to maturing the relevant pathways, which leads to the relevant priors being reliably instantiated when they are called for.

Having said all this, invariance on its own does not mean goodness, it is simply the way our system evolved for survival. This idea of intelligence can be good or bad (in relation to suffering), all depending on the context. A person preserving the organization of "everything is dangerous" across a vast amount of diverse input would be considered generally bad. Same thing with a rigid ideology that is harmful to others: the invariants track, even when coming across opposing evidence.

Now, something underlying all of this talk so far has been the bow-tie diagram itself, which must be analyzed from a privileged human perspective which brings about interesting dynamics of modeling. Meaning, specificity, and width of bow ties can vary depending on the analysis one is performing. This changes the questions one might ask about an organism or adaptive system, thus it changes the answers you will find, so it is important to recognize how we can cleanly model the whole of something. So, getting into it, it can be said that the size of a bow tie is relative to the spatiotemporal and problem-space sizing of the target being analyzed. If we are looking at a top-down target of an adaptive system, like "survive," this dictates the sizing of all three levels of the bow tie: the inference side, the bottleneck, and the generative side. Larger-horizon targets, those that span across a large spatiotemporal scale, require the integration of a broader range of relevant input and can organize larger trees of nested subtargets and information, while smaller-horizon targets integrate and coordinate comparatively local states.

A bow tie can then be thought of as the dependency tree serving a selected target. There is input and information selected and framed for the target, the most compressed state that matters for commitment, and the dependency tree that "serves" it.

Think of a large-horizon target like "become a doctor." This requires the system to "consider" both larger subtargets, like going to medical school, and extremely specific targets like "reach towards the notebook" for when you're studying. Through this, the system must consider a vast range of input that is being analyzed for what is considered relevant for the long-term target of becoming a doctor, which constrains the nested targets. These nested targets are of course smaller from both a spatiotemporal scale and problem-solving level, how many means to reach the same endpoint, which means the sizing of these bow ties will be smaller in comparison to those of the constraining, more dominant ones. Because of this sizing difference, we find that the model itself is now constrained in new ways: the overall size of the inference side and the specificity level of the generative side are both scaled in proportion to that of the target and context being considered. When looking at the target of becoming a doctor, the generative side of this bow tie will be massively specific in terms of ways the dependency tree manifests itself. Looking at a smaller scale, like a cell wanting to maintain ion balance, it's clear both the inference and generative sides will be smaller in scale, meaning the system has to integrate and act on a smaller range of variables and possible states. A larger bow tie can imply the system is acting on a larger temporal scale, possibly a large spatial scale, lots of nested subtargets, more constraints, and a larger space of knowledge. Crucially, the high-level top-down state of a bow tie is not necessarily specific in its nature. In fact, these high-level organizing target states are quite abstract. Becoming a doctor is more abstract than "contract this finger muscle," and the reason for this is precisely that its specificity shows up far downstream, calling for action only indirectly further down the line.

More abstract constraints drive more long-term specificity. This also appears in the bottleneck section: in the case of the cell, that maximum compression point that is useful to commit from will be massively "small" compared to a much more complex system like a human wanting to become a doctor, if we assume we are looking at the highest-order bow tie in an agent's system. In a large bottleneck, many invariants are kept in place and branch out into the generative side, and acted upon through nested trees, making the larger system more specific in its own top-down tree.

Coming off this, Levin's idea of the cognitive light cone fits in neatly. This light cone concept is demarcated as "[the scale of the largest goal, in both space and time, which a system is capable of working towards](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13197194/)." Originally I had made the assumption that we can simply model a system's light cone by looking at its "whole" bow tie, but quickly this comes apart when it is recognized that a system does not have one singular bow tie or target operating at once, even in simple systems that might seemingly have one high-level target spawning nested bow ties. The cognitive light cone instead marks the outer boundary of the largest-scale target the system is capable of pursuing, not the size of every process running within the system. This naturally produces a question concerning how we know the largest-scale target a system is capable of pursuing. In general, this can become known through testing and tracking what occurs in the system when you prescribe perturbations that offset invariant variables. Where you can "stop" observation in accordance with the system as a whole is when you can no longer find evidence that the larger state is being actively restored across many disturbances. Crucially, we must first begin observation with a potential target that might track across many perturbations. Without a starting prediction, it will become impossible to course-correct findings into knowing the relevant highest-level target.

Let's say we have a starting prediction that a person wants to get home safely. We might add in many perturbations like delaying their train, their phone dying, or a stranger talking to them. When these perturbations occur, it is crucial to ask if behavior still reaches towards the target of getting home safely. If the target remains invariant, and nested actions are in service towards the target, we can say that at that level of analysis, the preserved higher-order variable is "getting home safe." Now, to find out the cognitive light cone of the whole system, we need to ask a different question. For this, we might start with something like "survival" or "maintain viability." Many lower-level variables change continuously, like eating different things, changing cells, turnover of molecules, or changing internal temperatures, while some high-level organization persists, constraining all of these low-level variables. Here though, we must be careful not to confuse this with pure "survival." Here, viability is simply a useful high-level invariant for explaining a broad range of output, but it does not mean the system is acting in agentic ways towards the known mechanism of survival. [Levin specifically notes](https://osf.io/preprints/osf/p23se_v1) that collective cells can scale from local homeostatic variables toward much larger target states such as anatomical configurations, enlarging the spatiotemporal scale of targets they can pursue: "the scaling enables cellular collectives to enlarge their 'cognitive light cone' – the spatio-temporal scale of the target states they are able to pursue." So then, the light cone of what we are observing is like an outer envelope surrounding the largest bow tie the system maintains across its existence. This implies that a larger cognitive light cone enables the capacity for larger-scale bow ties to exist. In a way, the generative side of the largest bow tie of an adaptive system can be said to reflect the scale of its cognitive light cone.

The size of the bow tie and the light cone is not fixed. [Levin writes](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02688/full) that the borders of a cognitive light cone "can grow or shrink, on evolutionary or ontogenic time scales, as the organization of an agent changes." In a human agent, what grows is compressed structure and capacity to hold that structure. As memory builds up and priors become deeper and more specific, the system can hold larger targets on a spatiotemporal level. Children clearly show this. [They do not consider future rewards as most adults do](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/5_Psychological_Science_33_%28Green%29.pdf), and [their attention explores broadly instead of pursuing one goal deeply](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7293160/). So development enlarges the cognitive light cone, and deepens the richness one holds in their system. A human agent's light cone can also shrink on smaller timescales. [Under scarcity or threat](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23118192/), our attention can be tunneled, reducing attention available for longer-term concerns.

If a cognitive light cone of a system specifies the largest spatiotemporal goal the system can pursue, measured by how far its behavior keeps restoring a target under perturbation, and the scale of a bow tie specifies the range of salient variables integrated and possible actions organized for a goal, measured by which inputs change that behavior and how many levels of means reorganize when a path is blocked, then the light cone can be treated as an upper bound on the scale of goal-relative bow ties possible to that system. These are separate axes, meaning we should be able to reliably assess two different things: a system should never integrate inputs about, or reorganize its means toward, a state farther in space or time than the farthest target it restores under perturbation, and change in size of one should track change in the other. A dog should never be found changing its behavior because of an event next month in another city, for example.

I want to speak to the physical constraints of this bow-tie architecture. All three parts, the inference side, the bottleneck, and the generative side, are constrained by the system's physical architecture itself, and different embodiments bring about different measurable variables and offer different affordances on the world. My guess goes like this: bow ties are bounded on the inference side by what they can sense, at the bottleneck by what they can integrate and preserve as target-salient organizations, and on the generative side by what they can control. In humans for instance, we can sense light, sound, chemicals (smell and taste), touch, and internal bodily states, we can integrate and preserve a certain amount of information because of structure and memory, and we can control external things through our physical bodies and tools. One might argue that capabilities that contribute to cognitive light cone size, like human thoughts, are not necessarily "physical." Without getting into arguments surrounding physicalism and emergence, I would push back on this by saying these capabilities are only possible because of the underlying architecture at play. Emergent capabilities may be hard to model mathematically, as they are inherently surprising, but through privileged external observation, these capabilities are still observable as constraints on what the system is capable of doing. So, if we are trying to find a more general labeling of the three parts that scale up continuously, it might go roughly: observability → internal control state → controllability → loop.

Here, we can use the concept of the Markov blanket to recognize the difference between two boundaries: what a system can sense and affect, and what it can reach. Put simply, a Markov blanket is a boundary of channels: what can cross between the system and the world. Sensing carries "the world" in, and acting carries the system's influence out. For a human, we can roughly say this involves the [sensory receptors](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsif/article/10/86/20130475/35150/Life-as-we-know-itLife-as-we-know-it) and the muscles. The light cone is a boundary of reach, showing how far in space and time the system's targets extend. [Levin illustrates this](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02688/full) using a tick, a dog, and a human stating: "a tick has a relatively small cognitive boundary, having very little memory or predictive power in the temporal direction, and sensing/acting very locally. A dog has much more temporal memory, some forward prediction ability, and a degree of spatial concern. However, it is likely impossible for a dog’s cognitive apparatus to operate with notions about what is going to happen next month or in the adjacent town. Human minds can operate over goals of vastly greater spatial and temporal scales, and one can readily imagine artificial (organic or software-based) Selves with properties that define every possible shape in this space." The cone can also shrink in both dimensions at once. Dealing with cancer in humans, he writes, "the scope of the coherent Self of a cell reduces from being as large as the boundary of a whole body, to that of just one cell’s surface," and its temporal shape shrinks from decades to "a mere year." The blanket and the light cone can come apart, as our channels only ever sample the current state of the sensory boundary, yet our targets can span decades. That reach [comes from structure and memory inferring the future from the present](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5998386/), not from the senses themselves. Crucially, the blanket limits the light cone but does not set it. A system cannot pursue a target over something it has no channel to sense or affect, even indirectly. Cybernetics states this as [Ashby's law of requisite variety](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/computer-science/requisite-variety): ["only variety can destroy variety."](https://ashby.info/Ashby-Introduction-to-Cybernetics.pdf) Working within those limits, the structure at the bottleneck sets how far targets can reach. Two people can have the same senses and body, but only one may have the structure and memory to hold a target that spans decades.
