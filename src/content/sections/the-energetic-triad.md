This framework’s ontology rests heavily on how the mind-body system manages metabolic and computational cost. I use “cost” here in a relative sense, not as a measure of calories burned. At any moment, the brain has limited processing capacity and attention to go around, and different possible interpretations of what you’re perceiving compete for resources. “Cost” means how much of that limited capacity an input demands and how much additional capacity is used to process it.

Our system runs on a finite budget of processing capacity and spends much of it maintaining a stable, workable interpretation of ongoing input. For every input, the system estimates how much processing it will require and then registers how much capacity it actually demands. This is always happening in experience because sensory input is constant. Here, processing refers broadly to the resources used to handle an input, while integrating it means attempting to “place it into” the field, a process that subsequently produces a felt sense if deviations are consciously noticeable.

One main source of an input’s obvious feeling tone is the system reporting the difference between the estimated cost and the registered cost of processing it. Put simply, this is integration-cost prediction error pertaining to the predicted versus actual integration cost of an input. Successful compression here can reduce the cost of integrating an input by allowing the field to organize previously separate, unchunked elements into a single workable structure.

Some practical proxies for whether an input is high cost to integrate are time (rereading a passage), effort (using “extra focus”), and bodily tension (like a furrowed brow). For an input that is low cost to integrate, some proxies include immediacy (clicks fast), freed attention (gained capacity for more input), and immediate tension release (relaxation).

However, cost difference is not the only source of feeling tone; a second source is an input’s relation to the current field’s target. When facing threatening input, say if you saw a lion, the field’s most heavily weighted target would be something like “survival at all costs.” The input can integrate cleanly and quickly, as when you immediately recognize the lion, but because the situation threatens the heavily weighted target of being in a state of remaining alive, the felt tone is negative. These two sources of feeling tone can come apart at the edges, which is why an intrusive thought, like being eaten by the lion, can integrate easily but still feel bad. It should also be noted that these two sources can contribute simultaneously to the felt tone of an experience and with different strengths. For example, if the lion is hidden in some grass and making noise, the input may both cost more to integrate than expected when trying to disambiguate hearing from seeing, and threaten the target of remaining alive, causing the two sources to reinforce each other.

Setting aside targets to focus on the main structure here, this pattern of a fit-baseline with positive and negative spikes shows up across pretty much all situations in experience. The fit-baseline is the level at which integration cost roughly matches the predicted cost, while spikes or differences in either direction occur when processing cost is more or less than what the system expected. It is important to seperate two things here that may get confused for being one "baseline." The fit-baseline describes moment-to-moment processing of input, while background felt tone is the overall felt tone level experience lies at, set by factors like physiology and by how much capacity the system has in general. This capacity is bottlenecked by held contraction, and the pushing away or pull torwards targets the system cannot satisfy (later expanded on through taṇhā).

As a helpful metaphor, we might describe this whole thing as the ocean. The background felt tone is the level at which the ocean sits, while the fit-baseline is the surface resting at that level. Most of the time, water is calm, meaning input is processed using capacity that is correctly predicted. However, sometimes waves occur which we can call positive or negative difference from the fit-baseline level. A swell up is when input is cheaper to process than predicted and a down trough (or an imagined "reverse wave") is when input comes in more costly to process than predicted. These are both deviations from the relatviely stable, flat surface of the calm ocean. Crucially, a singular wave of the same size will hit different levels based on the ocean level that day. If it's high tide, the same wave will crest high, while barely reaching on low tide. Here, what changed was the baseline level, or where the ocean was sitting at overall. This represents that the same insight that is processed less costly than predicted can be experienced either mundane one day or exciting another day due to the background felt tone sitting at different levels.&nbsp;

Because  input is constant and most input integrates near their predicted cost, these fit-baseline-level integrations produce a&nbsp;cumulative embodied tone of fit at whatever level the floor sits at, a gestalt that organizes our experience of processing costs. This fit-baseline is what differences are compared against.&nbsp;Note that the fit-baseline is not the same as a constant positive difference, since a positive difference is a noticeable deviation, not a baseline level.&nbsp;You can see this three-level pattern, positive spike, fit-baseline, negative spike, across aesthetic experiences, a math proof, a painting, or sensations in the body, since part of an input's feeling tone comes from the system registering these deviations against the level it is currently sitting at.

For the three levels, we can categorize them as positive, **fit-baseline**, and negative, with positive and negative representing differences in opposing directions. **Fit-baseline** is where registered cost roughly matches predicted cost, with the trunk of the dependency tree structuring incoming input without major surprises in either direction. A negative difference occurs when an input costs more to integrate than predicted, often because it cannot be compressed into a single gestalt, so the system spends extra resources parsing it. A fully predictable input that is also predicted to be cheap to integrate, like elevator music, produces little or no difference and sinks into the **fit-baseline** as mundane rather than producing an obvious positive difference. A positive difference occurs when an input expected to be costly, perhaps because of its perceived richness or complexity, instead fits neatly into the active field as a valid gestalt and integrates more cheaply than predicted, requiring fewer processing resources than expected. In fast cases like seeing an object, this can happen within fractions of a second, though slower forms exist, such as a math proof that unfolds over minutes.

This is one reason why input that is hard to organize like going to an event for the first time can feel bad on its own, separate from anything threatening. Going to an unfamiliar event costs capacity to process which the system attempts to calculate and organize which is very hard to do with input there is not much history about.

This helps explain why internal representations matter so much. Representations can retune the field’s properties at a given moment, lowering the cost of integrating an input by making fit more substantial. This is why an already well-modeled input can integrate cheaply, while a poorly modeled one produces an obvious negative difference. A poor model will have you attempting to integrate the input, like a question, but there will be lots of struggle because of increased processing cost. A good model lowers the integration cost of inputs relative to encountering them without it, and it does so across many input types. For instance, a math problem is much easier to parse when the necessary prerequisites are already in place, making a model with those prerequisites better. Remember, the claim is that a good model lowers the cost of integrating future and novel inputs, not that low cost in the present necessarily means learning is happening, since our models need to integrate a range of varied and conflicting inputs. A flat-earther can update their model by watching YouTube videos and easily integrate a great deal of input, but only within a restricted subset, such as an echo chamber. The key is that good representations continue reducing integration costs for inputs you cannot plan for and across long time horizons.

This triad connects back to the dependency tree in important ways. The size of any spike compared to the **fit-baseline** depends on where in the tree the affected priors sit. A branch that sits high in the tree (i.e., not dealing with foundational priors) is cheap to change and hardly registers as an event, while violations that occur deeper in the tree affect load-bearing priors and force changes to everything else built on top of them, resulting in a much larger and more obvious felt tone difference. Hence why some surprises or changes in experience feel trivial while others feel like deep fragmentation: the same core mechanism occurs, but the depth and magnitude of the priors being affected differ greatly in cost. Because the tree rests on deep priors, updating them is incredibly expensive for our system to integrate, so the negative felt tone produced when deep priors are violated reflects the system accounting for that cost. This is why shame can hurt so badly, since it affects deep priors dealing with the sense of self and security, such as “I am bad,” which produces many discrepancies in the integration of input, even if low-level. The foundational trunk of the dependency tree is therefore slow to update because, according to the triad, such integration is extremely costly, while branches that sit higher are much cheaper to change. But keep in mind that higher branches are where most updating happens (which can have cascading effects downward), whereas deep priors, which are active in nearly every moment and rarely violated, contribute to much of our ongoing feeling tone, mostly through steady **fit-baseline** integration with occasional obvious spikes of felt tone.

It’s important to mention the temporal variation that comes with these processes. This section so far has focused on one-shot differences tracking fast integration time slices, but what about the way we track feeling tone across longer slices of time? The main measurement we can use here is compression progress, the rate at which integration cost is reduced relative to a target. We can model this in three broad ways: too easy, productive struggle, and too hard. Of course, these slopes do not always appear in such simple forms; they are just practical proxies for measuring feeling tone over time. The “too easy” signal tracks experiences over slices of time in which there is little integration cost left to reduce, which typically results in feelings like boredom. The “productive struggle” signal tracks experiences in which integration cost decreases over time with input that starts as costly. If cost falls steadily, this can result in feelings like enjoyment or flow. Interesingly, there seems to be a “Goldilocks Effect” in cognitive science and learning which people disengage from sequences that are either too predictable or too surprising. The “Eighty Five Percent Rule” rule demonstrates something very similar in which “there is a sweet spot in which training is neither too easy nor too hard, and where learning progresses most quickly.” Lastly, the “too hard” signal tracks experiences in which integration cost does not decrease over time or even increases, which commonly results in feelings like frustration or overwhelm.

It is also possible to extrapolate this cost-slope function into a temporal property of phenomenology, meaning the rate at which an ongoing experience becomes more structured relative to what the system is trying to do. For instance, “too easy” means the field is producing dominant, low-cost gestalts, so experience feels mundane. “Productive unfolding” means uncertainty remains, but the field is gradually finding structure, producing curiosity, absorption, insight, aesthetic pleasure, or flow. “Too hard” means that no workable structure emerges in experience, so unresolved input accumulates into tension and overwhelm. This is not a complete theory of feeling tone but one proposed contributor to phenomenological experience.

Crucially, compression progress is always relative to a target, like finishing a problem set today or becoming a mathematician over years, which operate across different time slices. An activity is not by itself too easy or too hard; it is evaluated relative to what the system is currently trying to accomplish through its heavily weighted targets. So the same activity, such as solving a problem set, can show different rates of progress relative to each target. For example, solving a problem set in a math textbook may feel like little immediate progress while still feeling meaningful based on the long-term target of becoming a mathematician. Likewise, a more difficult problem may feel locally productive while seeming irrelevant to a larger target, like learning side branches in math that aren’t necessary for becoming a mathematician. Several targets can remain active at once, and precision weighting determines which of their progress signals contributes most strongly to the feeling tone of the moment. When these targets align, short-term work can inherit meaning from longer-term targets. When they conflict, the same activity can produce mixed feelings. Felt tone in any moment is therefore shaped by compression-rate signals across several nested targets, such as short-term targets within long-term ones, with deeper, more heavily precision-weighted targets carrying greater weight and tending to dominate feeling tone. In this way, felt tone of experience over time is a signal of whether integration cost is falling as quickly as the field predicts relative to its active targets.

This cost-prediction model is not meant to claim that the brain literally reports exact metabolic cost in experience. It is more like a phenomenological synthesis of predictive processing, interoception, allostasis, processing fluency, and aesthetic prediction error. The Free Energy Principle models perception and action as processes organized around reducing uncertainty or prediction error. Active inference and allostatic models then help inform this synthesis through bodily regulation, where we predict and manage our physiological needs rather than reacting after the fact. Dealing with aesthetics, processing fluency theory says that “easier” processing tends to produce more positive aesthetic response, and predictive models of art say that pleasure often comes from the reintroduction of order after prediction has been disrupted due to complex input. All this supports the fact that results of the difference between expected cost and actual integration cost of input is one contributor to the felt tone, or valence, of the field rather than felt tone itself. The test is obvious: a stable stream of input, like reading a book, produces differences in feeling when harder to integrate passages are read. Because nothing about threat or reward changed between differing levels of passages, the change in cost to integrate is an isolated variable, not tied to another factor. However, it is not the only thing moving felt tone levels, since factors like satisfaction of targets can come into play as well

Lastly, I want to touch on the importance of biological and physiological factors at play here. A big part of the **background felt tone**, which contributes to the fit-baseline, is not fixed but is set by the body’s overall state, which is where physiology comes to matter. The felt tone of this triad is modulated by allostasis, which is the body’s proactive regulation of its own resources: “Efficient regulation [of the body] requires anticipating needs and preparing to satisfy them before they arise.” Other modulators at play include the state of the nervous system, stress hormones like cortisol, and inflammation, which are all processed into the field through interoception, which is “the process by which the nervous system senses, interprets, and integrates signals originating from within the body, providing a moment-by-moment mapping of the body’s internal landscape across conscious and unconscious levels.” Another well-supported finding is that inflammation can produce sickness-like behavior such as fatigue, minimal activity, social withdrawal, and a depressed mood. Relatedly, heavy stress can bias perception and attention toward potential threat, which causes feedback loops of threat-detection. This means a drained or threatened body with little slack has lower capacity to handle complexity and interprets more inputs as costly to integrate before any specific input is received.

What is being described here is not the **fit-baseline** that positive or negative spikes are compared against, which lies wherever the system is currently at, but the background felt tone the whole system uses. What modulates **the background felt tone** is how much capacity is already used before input is integrated, which includes capacity spent holding contraction and running second-order targets on experience. By releasing held contraction that is not globally useful and lowering the precision on second-order targets, we can gain capacity and raise the background felt tone, the way chronic stress and bad health lowers it. Put simply, **the background felt tone** (which is a force multiplier) partly reflects the brain’s expectations about bodily state and available resources.

Importantly, these expectations can include long horizon targets, not just short-term bodily needs. A future target is held in the current field as an expectation that present actions are moving the system toward it, and the body can regulate differently depending on whether that target feels achieved, achievable, or threatened. This helps explain why someone can feel a sense of deep okay-ness while doing difficult work if the system trusts the current actions to reach the long-term target, or remain tense when feeling pleasure if a deeper target still feels unachieved. In this sense, feeling tone is partly about whether the system models its highly weighted targets as sufficiently handled, not just pleasure. Handled here can mean either changing the world to reach the target, changing the action(s) that are supposed to reach the target, or simply changing the target itself. To be clear, the general claim that bodily state sets a background influence on affect is well supported, but the more specific predictive-processing mappings (like tying neuromodulators like dopamine or serotonin to specific prediction roles) are debated, which I treat more loosely but still consider meaningful. To be clear about the **three-layer** stack of **the compression gradient**, all of these things, the physiological factors, are **part of the embodied layer that sets the background felt tone**, not something separate.

Luckily, we have agency over a portion of this physiological aspect of the **background felt tone** since it is not exactly fixed. The things that are so far best studied to help are exercise, sleep, morning light, slow breathing, humming, and dancing, but I suspect the literature is far behind what is possible by an individual, so I would recommend not constraining yourself to what the current “science says.” For instance, much of the literature on meditation pertains to novices or relatively inexperienced practitioners, and advanced practitioners/states are understudied, which is a limitation when trying to understand the upper limits of human agency in this area. If you were to experiment, finding ways to reduce background processing demand or increase the system’s available capacity, whether by releasing held contraction (more on this below), loosening rigid priors, or supporting the body so it has greater physiological and psychological capacity, would be a helpful guiding principle.
