> \
"We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble."
> 
> – William James, [*What Is an Emotion?*](https://emotion.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/1353/2020/11/James_1884_What_is_an_Emotion.pdf)

What is an emotion? This question has been attempted to be answered [*a lot*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/)*,* along with their load-bearing downstream effects on our mind-body system. I'd like to offer an account of emotions that works within this framework that may point to a unique direction. Truthfully, any attempt to name an emotion or define them is a failure due to moving up the compression gradient which leaves characteristics left out, but nonetheless attempts are still useful in their own right.

An emotion is a context-sensitive field configuration that structures signals from the body and environment, felt tone, conditioned concepts, targets, and actions into a unifying gestalt like fear or anger. What is felt in experience as an emotion is not a raw signal but the organization the field produced. Emotions are not just properties of the field because their construction is downstream of many properties coming together and forming a coherent generative structure. The purpose of emotions naturally arises out of its definition and placement in this framework. [It seems as though](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5390700/) emotion concepts are resuable chunks that organize bodily and situational context which would normally be ambiguous. For example, fear is not just a stored object that gets released or expressed, it is more like a context-sensitive organizational tool reconstructed from prior structure. Researchers like to define emotion episodes which are more specifically temporally unfolding configurations that “[comprise different components such as appraisal of the situation, action preparation, physiological responses, expressive behavior, and subjective feelings](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Scherer2019TEPEA.pdf)”, but this framework will just use the term emotion across the board. Crucially, emotions are not feelings. An emotion is a whole field configuration, while a feeling is how that configuration becomes known from the inside. Felt tone also is not a feeling because it is narrower, referring only to the configuration’s valence. For example, *warmth* is a feeling, but we would not consider this an emotion because it is not necessarily a structural category. To clarify terms further, mood must be distinguished as well. [Mood might be considered](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms7149) as a slower background configuration that structures and biases how ongoing input gets interpreted. It does not require one absolute clear gestalt, and it can create feedback loops in which current felt tone influences perceived outcomes and how they feed back into mood.

Taken as the field contains many properties and mechanisms at play, such as sensory evidence, attention, context, and learned concepts from others, it is clearly bodily signals are not simply read and then labeled as a certain emotion. As the system attempts to infer what is going on with its current state and what to do next, all of these different things brew together which pulls the system in many different directions. [Ultimately, precision weighting helps determine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5062100/) which things, like sensory signals, exert the most influence on the field’s current interpretation and action.

Emotions are indeed gestalts. They compress related configurations rather than exact, formulaic structures. For example, moments of fear share enough properties across time and with enough repetition that fear becomes a deep structure built into the load-bearing tree, made up of priors leaning on the body and context. The instances of fear are chunked together as the system attempts to save capacity so it does not have to process each component of fear everytime they show up together. Crucially, instances of fear can slightly differ in contraction patterns, felt tone, attention, and action, but they are still chunked together as one gestalt, which can result in the "misclassification" of future instances due to wide pruning. And crucially, just as the compression gradient is a continuous unfolding of compression rather than being rigid, boundaries between [emotions are not discrete, they are fuzzy](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1702247114). This means that categorical emotions lie within a continuous space, connected by intermediate states rather than separated by hard boundaries. This suggests something like a structural-threshold modeling in the sense that the field gradually climbs through ambiguous configurations until one learned one organizes enough to become a clear structuring gestalt. This means "failed" emotional gestalts can exist, or ambiguous components that don't cohere into a whole. This can be seen at the beginnings of a felt sense about an experience: the feeling is there but it has yet to cohere into a nameable thing.

When it comes to targets, emotions complicate things. Not every emotion has just one target built in to its known configuration, though often times a singular target is what is perceived, like a need for safety known through its action policy such as "I need to get away from this situation" in episodes of fear. Emotions can be seen as a sort of modulator that relate and connect differing targets when it comes to current or predicted future input. They they organize the field in relation to these targets in such a way [that relate to preferred states, needs, and capacity](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Scherer2019TEPEA.pdf), like how targets of anxiety might get someone to move to state of avoidance to ensure the system is not overwhelmed by bodily sensations. The most relevant target then in a given moment caused by a clear emotion has multiple ways of being known, such as being implicit, distributed, uncertain, or violated. Because of the overlap and nuance of targets when dealing with emotions, action policies are re-weighted without determining one fixed action across the board. This means an emotion may make a number of actions, like escaping or freezing, more reachable depending on the field, without any singular policy encapsulating the emotion itself. [A large contributor to this is context](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103113001728), which is why emotions can be so hard to analyze or know since context is always interpreted and organized in such a way by the field. When it comes to targets and their action policies, precision matters since it can be distributed across a wide net of things across the field like sensory evidence, emotions, threats, and available actions. Because of this, high precision on action policies of an emotion can coexist with the belief that the action will have any meaningful outcome at all. My system can place high precision on the action of running from a situation to avoid the sensations, but in the background the belief that this is not a long-term solution can still exist.

When it comes to emotions, the embodied layer is crucial. Emotions are embodied, meaning bodily changes are not just a result of a thing that was created entirely in the brain first. Sensations like tension and heart rate have the ability to influence the emotional experience and provide interoceptive evidence the system uses to predict and regulate its state, like through homeostatic priors. The body is essentially a part of the field configuration that is producing the emotion itself; they cannot be decoupled.
