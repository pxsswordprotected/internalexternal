To talk about the importance of resonance, we must turn to Eugene Gendlin. Gendlin trained as a philosopher and later became widely known for his work in psychotherapy and Focusing. In the 1950s, he worked with Carl Rogers’s group at the University of Chicago, using a clinical setting to study how people symbolize experience and how felt experience becomes words, concepts, and change. Gendlin himself thought many therapy theories were too focused on content, such as “the client has hostility.” He did not declare the content wrong; instead, he believed that descriptions alone do not explain how change actually happens. In “A Theory of Personality Change,” he argued that knowing what is wrong is different from the felt process of changing it: “Yet it is clear that there is a major difference between knowing the conceptual explanation of personality (which one can devise in a few hours) and the actual feeling process of changing (which often requires years).” His research moved from what the client talks about to how the client talks and senses while talking. A client can express the same thing in two completely different ways depending on their bodily connection and presence. Experiencing is about the client’s manner of relating to experience, not only the content of speech. Skipping ahead, Gendlin and his colleagues arrived at a central finding in their research: clients who improved paused, turned their attention inward, sensed something unclear but bodily present, searched for words that fit it, and then felt some kind of movement or shift. This is the core of his process known as Focusing.

Next, we should turn to the felt sense, a term coined by Gendlin. The felt sense is not just an emotion; it is a vague, holistic bodily sense of a situation. A common example of the felt sense is when, while talking to someone, a murky feeling comes up in the body, somewhere like the stomach. It’s not yet a clear thought; it’s more like a bodily holding of the situation before language finds a way to compress it. This sense might make you feel like something is off or that you want to exit the conversation, but the feeling is not yet entirely clear to you. This should be differentiated from felt tone since the felt sense is the implicit bodily whole of a situation, while felt tone is its positive, negative, or neutral quality.

Working from the felt sense, Gendlin then named the felt shift. This is when you find a word, image, or sentence, which he calls a handle, that fits the felt sense, and something in you changes physically or emotionally. This feels like a release of contraction resulting in expansion, a sort of lightness. This, for Gendlin, is where the real change happens. The person experiences a whole new way of relating to the thing they were holding onto. We can extrapolate this beyond a clinical setting to a broader pair of phenomena called resonance and friction. Resonance is a noticeable positive felt response that occurs when an input fits the field and integrates more cheaply than expected. Friction is the corresponding noticeable negative felt response that occurs when an input costs more to integrate than expected. Because an input’s relation to a target can also produce positive or negative felt tone, not every pleasant response is resonance and not every unpleasant response is friction.

Moreover, friction can also signal that a constraint is blocking movement toward a valued attractor or that targets at different levels of the system are in conflict. This goes back to the field section of this framework. If friction persists after adjustments of a current state, then the reachability of the state you are trying to reach needs to be resurrected in a manner that makes more viable pathways available. Keep in mind that these two generators of friction, barriers to attractor states and conflicting targets, still contain the underlying principle that the attempted transition through the field costs more than the system predicted.

Resonance is that feeling when a word or name is on the tip of your tongue, and once it is found and said out loud, it feels like a relief. There is a shift like, “Oh yeah, I got it, so I can move on now.” Before finding the right name, someone else might say other options out loud, like “Is it X, Y, or Z?” while the response is, “No, no, not that one.” Finally, once the other person names it, it feels like a matching puzzle piece for your current understanding. During the initial attempt to think of the name, there was a holding onto something, an attempt to search through a vast internal landscape of intuition, as Bessis would put it. Then, when the correct name comes out, there is an obvious felt sense of correctness. To quote Grothendieck, “It is the naming that makes the awareness that has appeared irreversible, ineffaceable… [it is] a silent welcoming of a knowledge appearing in deeper layers of our being, where thought has no access.”

(In a related manner, the Bio-Emotive Framework by Dr. Douglas Tataryn uses what it calls Resonance Testing to identify and express feelings, sometimes producing expansion or crying.)

Crucially, resonance is only a felt sense of fit with our current field, not an objective truth-seeking mechanism. What one finds resonant is highly individualized and dependent on conditioning. Of course, in some ways, we are all conditioned by many of the same things, especially if we are part of the same culture. The felt sense of rightness is partly driven by how fluently an answer comes to mind, not by whether it is factually correct. As the common saying goes, “repetition builds intuition.” If we are constantly repeating something that is factually wrong but fits according to our embodied layer, an image can still produce resonance even if it is not based on “truth.” This is visible in trauma-related hypervigilance, where a threat-oriented field may make safe cues unexpectedly costly to integrate while making threat interpretations unusually fluent. In the same way, we might carry an incorrect representation of a math formula, but that image still remains resonant because we have yet to update it accordingly. This updating occurs through the loop of refinement which will be described later. Friction is equally dependent on the current field because it indicates that something was unexpectedly costly to integrate, not that the input was objectively false. A true but unfamiliar idea may produce friction, while a false but condioned one may produce resonance. Similarly, an incorrect representation may remain fluent and easy to integrate because it fits a field confirugation, but it produces resonance only when it integrates more cheaply than expected.

Returning to the field ontology of this framework, resonance and friction are pre-verbal embodied responses to input being tested against the field’s current configuration. They are changes in felt tone, not the same thing as Gendlin’s general felt sense. Resonance and friction are the two opposing directions an integration-cost difference can take, with the former signaling input that integrates more cheaply than expected and the latter signaling input that costs more to integrate than expected. When an input satisfies the field’s active properties, it fits; if it also integrates more cheaply than expected, we feel resonance. When an input violates or strains a sufficient property and consequently costs more to integrate than expected, we feel friction, even before we can name what was violated. Because there is always a default field active through low-level, fundamental priors like “thing-ness,” there is a background feeling of fit in experience, in which things are constantly tested for fit and happen to satisfy the field. Because of this, an obvious sense of “clicking” or feeling “off” reflects how far an input’s felt response deviates from the constant background baseline, with deeper priors producing stronger resonance or friction. As the Energetic Triad established earlier, resonance and friction can be understood as opposing differences between predicted and actual integration cost. 

Importantly within the field, resonance and friction also can change the field’s configuration. Under active inference, these two properties can change what happens next, which therefore has feedback loops of action that can reinforce interpretations and behavior. Resonance has the ability to increase the precision of the successful branch in the load-bearing tree taken pertaining to the input, which then reinforces precision and capacity allocated. Friction can redirect precision away from the branch taken, which can result in updating models or taking action in another direction. Both of these things reinforce a loop of integration and action that retune the field in the direction of where precision is steered.

To put this in the frame of The Compression Gradient, resonance and friction can become especially useful signals when moving between layers. Each time you compress a felt sense up into words or decompress someone’s words back down into your own embodied layer, resonance or friction can tell you whether the translation integrates more or less successfully than expected. The felt shift can signal a successful move between layers, where the sentence corresponds with your field and fits the embodied material more successfully than expected. When a move costs more to complete than expected, friction is introduced. Friction means the system sensed something that violates a constraint of the field; the attempt to find fit with input in the layer below or above the one you are moving from does not produce a correspondence that works without additional processing. Because the two lower layers are not fully legible from the outside, resonance and friction act as internal compasses for figuring out what works and what does not. They guide exploration, but they do not replace external testing or formal verification.

This is where the field’s “fit distance” property matters. This property tells you whether an input sits closer to or farther from the field’s current configuration. Take the tip-of-the-tongue example. There might be active variables and constraints like “the name starts with a certain letter” or “the name is of a celebrity.” If an externalization is presented that violates these properties, it will not resonate with the current field. The presented name sits at the far end of the fit-distance spectrum, so it fails as an input for the current search even if it remains salient. If processing that mismatch costs more than expected, the candidate may also produce friction. Fit distance affects whether an input can integrate, but closeness alone does not guarantee resonance and distance alone does not guarantee friction; the cost must differ noticeably from what the field predicted.

This also explains the experience of feeling that something is “off” about an externalization. In this case, the system detects friction but has not yet built an explicit representation of the cause of that friction. The embodied layer registers this negative difference in felt tone before the representational layer can identify which variable or constraint was violated. There is noticeable friction but no located cause yet. Having no explanation for the friction does not mean the signal is a failure; it simply means an explanation has not yet been constructed. A clear example of this is demonstrated in the TV show Mad Men, season 3, episode 2. In one scene, the team produces a commercial and shows it to a client. Both the client and, later, one of the ad men, Harry Crane, agree that it is a good commercial but that something about it is off. None of them, however, can say what makes it feel off. Ultimately, the client rejects the commercial even though it is almost exactly what they asked for and regards it as a failure. Both the client and Harry are describing the embodied layer registering a mismatch between the ad and their felt sense of what it should be, so the system registers friction. However, the key point is that no one has activated an explicit representation that can locate which property is producing the friction. They can feel that something was violated without first having to name it or put it into a representation. It could be said that this is part of the point of hiring experts like these in the first place: they can detect that variables or constraints were violated in a field before they can necessarily determine which ones. They can turn the vague felt sense of something being off into concrete work that identifies and reduces that friction. The power of a well-calibrated field is that it can register subtle contextual violations quickly, though locating their cause may require further work.

Importantly, checking for fit internally does not always happen slowly or consciously. A representation does not need to show up as a vivid image or a live simulation for resonance or friction to occur. However, implicit or rapid fit does not automatically count as resonance, and an ordinary expected mismatch does not automatically count as friction. In speech especially, which is a form of notation, the checking process often runs implicitly and quickly. Deep priors and repeated use matter because using a cached expression like “how are you doing” means the system does not need to actively search for whether it fits. The representation is either very “small,” like a quick flash, or bypassed because the expression has become a cached output. Because this expression is expected to integrate cheaply, it will usually sink into the baseline rather than produce resonance. However, when someone is speaking from a felt sense, the process changes. When speaking, successive words or sentences may be checked against the field in real time. When a person pauses and says something like, “What I’m trying to say is…,” they are feeling friction between the words they just expressed and the active field. When a later phrase lands unexpectedly well, the resulting positive difference becomes noticeable as resonance. Fluent externalizations such as cached speech can appear to skip activating an explicit representation, but repeated use and conditioning make them fit the field so reliably that the checking happens quickly and implicitly, without becoming noticeable.

To hit on a point made earlier about background compression and decompression, it seems as though most “aha!” moments of insight are resonant. This is because, as our system works in the background without conscious direction, it implicitly reorganizes the field and the representations active within it. When the embodied layer registers this update as an unexpectedly successful fit, it releases the small cognitive tension it was holding, just as a well-fitting handle does when it lands. A sudden moment of clarity in the shower is the system updating itself, which feels resonant, and because it is going on in the background rather than through our steering, it is a surprise when it happens. This is why the process is something like this: you’re not really thinking about the thing → a sudden insight comes that feels resonant → you say something out loud like “finally,” which is not the insight itself but the notational expression of the cognitive tension being released, resulting in a sense of expansion. As a reminder, resonance means an unexpectedly successful fit, not factual correctness. A moment of insight may fit your system at the moment, but that does not mean the insight cannot later be updated.

An interesting thing about resonance and friction is that they fluctuate temporally. This means the same object can be resonant one day and less so the next or produce friction in one context but not another. I can cry when I watch a film because something in it resolves or fits unexpectedly well, and when I rewatch it the next month, I can feel close to nothing. As our field is changing moment to moment on varying scales based on context, it makes sense that different properties will be salient at different points in time, which determines whether we feel resonance or friction. A wide variety of things seem to influence this, like sleep, mood, recent input, physical state, current worries, the environment and physical context, and novelty or repetition. Considering the field is a deployment of our priors that are weighted moment to moment, it makes sense that salient properties will change based on the specific context. It also seems like the integration of a thing into our field, say a film, influences the intensity of felt senses, since the differences lose strength. Watching the same film over and over can generally cause the stream of input to be integrated into the baseline tone, which lessens the spike in felt sense and makes the film feel dull because it is predicted to be easy to integrate due to repetition. Potential for resonance can also return once lost if the properties of the current field are ones that make the input resonate. Maybe a year later, I watch the film again, and a recent life event allows old disconnected material to organize through it in a new way, producing resonance.
