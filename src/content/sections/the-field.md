(This section deals a lot with predictive processing. If you are unfamiliar with the theory, it would held to know the basics)

The field of experience is the active, moment-to-moment configuration of our active priors, bodily state, context, attention, targets, and available actions which functions as a deep mobile frame that constantly reconfigures what is relevant to the mind-body-environment system. Priors are the probability distributions stored within a generative model, and the field is what they become when that model is instantiated in a moment of time. The field’s properties, like variables and constraints, are the active criteria and characteristics the field is identified and known by, which interact with input. By input, I mean both raw sensory experience, such as seeing or hearing, and the things that experience is organized into, like a dog, a cat, or a mathematical object. Properties of a field are not something added, but instead legible markers the instantiation presents, emerging from interactions with active priors, state and context

A quick note on the substrate in which the field is embedded in: this framework assumes priors are held in some persistent substrate, but does not claim to know what exactly that is or where it is located. One possibility is the nervous system, but work and research like [Michael Levin’s](https://allencenter.tufts.edu/our-team/michael-levin) on [bioelectric pattern memory](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstb/article/376/1821/20190765/31461/Bistability-of-somatic-pattern-memories-stochastic) suggests that biological information storage and regulation may be [distributed across non neural cells and tissues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10770221/) in ways we do not yet fully understand, which would provide a more full picture than just the nervous system. Later it is proposed that deep priors are stabilized in the body as contraction, but this is just one partial explanation. The main claim being made is that priors persist and are reliably regenerated context-to-context from the substrate they come from.

The field is always active; we are always using a sliding window through which to experience things, whether implicity or explicitly. At any given moment, the field uses a subset of properties, like which are downstream of deeper factors like active priors and bodily state, to determine if input fits within its current configuration. The most important properties here seem to be constraints, variables, and fit distance. These are properties specifically in place to test input's fit within the field. Variables are the parts that can change and alter the prediction of the system, constraints are the binding rules or limits that shape what can fit within it, and fit distance tells you whether input feels closer to or farther from the field’s properties, like a puzzle piece fitting into place. These are not the only three properties active within a field at once; others include [actions that feel](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5652650/) [possible](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2440773/), [felt tone](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2002-08416-007) (valence), and targets. Felt tone is valence, meaning the positive, negative, or neutral quality of experience.

A crucial mechanism helping the field is precision weighting. This is [essentially](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-026-04356-0) "the estimated reliability or confidence assigned to priors or sensory evidence, which then determines their influence on updating." It dictates how much attention and trust is given to specific things over others. There are many specific directions precision can take such as: precision of sensory evidence, precision of preferences or targets, and precision over policies or action selection. This weighting comes from conditioning through past experiences. Like the priors it acts on, precision is shaped by past input and inferred from current context, so the system learns which kinds of signals have been reliable in which contexts and weights them accordingly through interactions within a complex web of predictions. This means precision weighting can be conditioned over time (a person trained to treat their environment as dangerous will keep threat signals weighted high by default), but it can also shift rapidly in the moment as input and context changes. When a threat is detected, the system heavily weights survival variables and constraints and turns down the importance of logical constraints, forcing input to pass a very different test for fit. This rapid reconfiguration of the field is how the system instantly shifts what is relevant without needing to build an entirely new one from scratch which is a result of precision reweighting, which includes sensory precision, the confidence assigned to sensory input

Another key job of precision weighting is that it can alter the current relative influence of competing prior preferences whose context-sensitive weighting helps determine the currently prioritized target. Prior preferences are prior probability distributions about which future outcomes the system prefers. In this framework, a target is the currently prioritized preferred outcome of a situation, while policies are possible action sequences selected partly by how well they are expected to reach the target. Simply, targets organize coordinated action by giving more or less weight to certain policy selections across short and long time horizons. For example, if conditioned enough, a survival target can gain control over social or long-term targets, but those other targets can still be “left over” if the survival target is fulfilled, and the left over targets still have the ability to influence things. The relative weight among competing prior preferences determines which target currently dominates, while how rigid that target is held is target precision. This means our current experience, which contains context (variables, interactions, etc.), changes both what evidence is trusted and which outcome is currently preferred.

For nuance, nested subsystems such as cells, tissue, organs, clusters of priors, and our nervous system can pursue different targets at once which is why target relevance matters. This is something like Levin's [multiscale competency architecture](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Multi-scale-competency-architecture-biological-systems-occur-as-nested-layers-of-scale_fig2_397353430)**.** Target relevance here refers to the dominant target or synthesis of targets currently organizing attention and action. Other targets can remain active beneath the relevant target, producing mixed responses when their preferred paths to fulfillment conflict. A conscious target could therefore dominate knowable attention while an embodied protective target constrains what feels possible in its current state. This explains why someone can explicitly want a relationship with someone else while their field still makes avoidance more available than connection. For this framework, when I am speaking of targets I am generally meaning the most relevant to the current context of a situation.

So to be clear, this framework uses precision in at least two ways: [sensory precision determines what sensory evidence is trusted](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/integrative-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnint.2018.00039/full), and target precision determines how tightly a [preferred outcome](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8956124/) is held. Within that is a range of outcomes that will satisfy the target, showing that preferred outcomes contain a property of how narrow they are. Which target currently dominates the field is the relative weight of competing preferences set by context and interpretation.

In the literature surrounding this, there is also [policy precision (γ, attributed to dopamine), which is how decisively the system commits to one course of action among competitors once a target is instantiated](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4631836/). Low policy precision across the board would mean something like freezing, where the system does not commit to a set of obvious actions. There also exists [state transition precision (ω, attributed to noradrenaline), which is how reliably the current situation and context are expected to hold](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10793076/). Its inverse reads as volatility, a property of the field.

All of this to say, typically this framework just uses "precision" as a whole, since all of these point to the same process of giving weight to factors within the field. It's important to understand all the different ways our system weights and commits to certain interpretations and actions, but it's best not to get lost in detail that is overbearing. What's important to know is that precision is a core mechanic the system uses to weight factors that govern much of our experience, and knowing how precision works can give us insight to how we live, act, and believe things.

In every moment, our “default” field is active. This default field is incredibly deep and structures our experience in the most foundational ways. It is primarily instantiated and consciously felt through the embodied layer, without needing to become explicit. It is a result of deep priors, which structure encompassing abstractions like “thing-ness,” “center reference point of experience,” or “that is a person.” These deep priors make the field feel like reality itself because they shape the way in which we constantly experience things. They do the most work to compress sensory totality, by which I mean theoretical sensory input before perceptual organization.

In terms of how [priors themselves are structured, they lie in a hierarchy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7187369/). In the literature, the priors that are the [most abstract and slowest to change are called high-level and rest at the top, and low-level is a term meant to convey priors closer to immediate sensory input and faster to update.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-018-1768-x) This framework calls the high-level ones deep and foundational, and places them at the trunk of a tree structure with more specific interpretations branching out. The trunk of the tree is a structural position, so nothing about the literature's hierarchy is changed. [Carhart-Harris and Friston write that psychedelics "relax the precision of high-level priors," and match the effect to "the highest or deepest level of the brain's functional architecture."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6588209/) In other words, psychedelics affect deep priors that make up the trunk of the tree.

For instance, the concept of “thing,” a deep prior for objecthood or thingness, sits at the trunk, while the concept of “water bottle” sits further out on the branches, being made up of not just “thing” but many other priors like “resource for survival,” each of which rest on different dependencies. There are also deep bodily priors called homeostatic priors, which are innate and evolutionarily ingrained and provide information about the body’s internal physiological state, such as temperature. These homeostatic priors allow us to continuously generate predictions that anticipate bodily needs and keep us within a survivable range through bodily functions and actions. Because they track survival directly, the system gives them very high precision, so they carry strong organizational authority over depends on them and can be difficult to override. These priors give the system a physiological floor from which it makes predictions to ensure we do not bottom out at a biological level. Dependent on these priors is what researchers [Micah Allen and Manos Tsakiris call the “first prior”](https://academic.oup.com/book/9637/chapter-abstract/156706805?redirectedFrom=fulltext) which is a stable sense of self-awareness grounded in the body’s regulation. As shown, the default field is like the reference against which everything else is compared and on which everything else depends, so it cannot be "bypassed" easily.

In a way, the default lives in the embodied layer to “act out” its predictions and reduce the error between what is happening and what it predicts will happen. This is active inference, a process in which the system takes action to make input match its model. Incoming sensory experience is structured by default priors, and behavior expresses or fulfills predictions shaped by them. The default field is deeply conditioned, while additional field properties are situationally activated by context, such as representations, and are generally recognizable as an explicit lens. The transition from the default field to something “extra” is like a gradient, not a binary either-or phase change. Repetition makes the extra properties imported from situations and objects sink deeper into the embodied layer, into the field. Additional field properties that are activated and used long enough may eventually become the new default way of seeing things, hence why experts pick up on small things so quickly. The expert has default deep priors running in the embodied layer as a way of perceiving and acting.

I say the field constantly reconfigures based on moment-to-moment experience because ever-changing sensory input is always activating different priors. If I am talking to a friend one moment, then switch to using my phone in the middle of the conversation, the context and object of the phone bring new properties into the field. Talking to a friend activates priors like “I need to listen,” “I need to sit a certain way,” “conversation moves like this,” and “my face and tone matter,” while using the phone activates different priors like “I look down,” “my fingers move like this,” “awareness collapses,” and “my posture changes.” Action and the nervous system then come to matter a great deal, since the field shapes the body for what it prepares to do. Changing input has the ability to affect posture, muscle tension, awareness, attention, breathing, and what actions can be taken. This shows us that the field itself is embodied, as it is not just a way of structuring input but also a way of preparing, responding, moving, and regulating. Most of this stays non-conscious.

Another way to picture what the field is doing is through prediction trees, or a probability distribution over competing hypotheses and possible future trajectories. A prediction tree is the space of potential interpretations of input and the branches the field could resolve into, weighted by precision, which also come with their own action policies modulated by targets. Conscious perception usually commits to one dominant branch in moment-to-moment experience, while competing branches remain represented with lower probability and can still influence inference. Under active inference, one branch gaining enough posterior probability, meaning it becomes the most likely interpretation after taking current evidence into account, increases confidence in its associated action sequences. A dominant branch therefore organizes how the input is interpreted while increasing the weight of the action policies associated with that predicted state. Simply, the system selects actions expected to move it toward its preferred state. But, this selection is not purely pragmatic. [Epistemic value](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1570070/1/Friston_Active%20Inference%20Curiosity%20and%20Insight.pdf) exists as the information a policy is expected to give about hidden states. Without this, the [dark-room objection](https://direct.mit.edu/isal/proceedings/isal2019/31/40/99205) naturally comes up: if the goal were only to match predictions, the best policy is to sit in a silent dark room. With epistemic value, interactions that result in more information about the world in relation to selected action policies are favored so as to reduce uncertainty about the hidden states generating input. This gives meaning to actions like play and research, which feel worth doing, not intrinsically painful.

Through active inference, it minimizes prediction error, the gap between expected input and actual input, either by acting to make the world match the prediction or by updating its model. At the same time, the system’s actions can reduce target error, a term specific to this framework for the gap between the system’s current and preferred state (though related terms like [risk in the free energy principle](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6848054/) and [error signal in perceptual control theory](https://www.iapct.org/pct/what-is-pct/) exist). Prediction error and target error often interact, but they describe different temporal variations. Prediction error signals mismatch with observations that have already occurred (tens of milliseconds to a few seconds), while target error looks in the future to measure the difference between expected future outcomes and preferred outcomes (seconds to years, depending on the target's horizon). Because the system cannot effectively explore every branch, it must narrow the tree through pruning methods like chunking. Chunking groups many separate prior structures into a single unit, such as “water bottle,” allowing the system to treat the package as one thing rather than evaluating each part separately. In doing so, a good deal of the lower-level richness is left out, but the search space shrinks and attention is freed for other input.

So far, the ideas presented can connect in what is called the dependency tree, which is the structure that makes the field’s current configuration possible. The hierarchy is the dependency structure, with deep priors at the trunk supporting everything that sits on them, while the branches represent competing predicted states made possible by that foundation. The perception that an object is a “water bottle” can only become a branch because “thing” is part of the foundation. There exists the trunk of the dependency tree which is not something you can simply choose from; it is the foundational set of priors that makes almost any branching or action possible at all. Branching happens mostly happens further out, where multiple interpretations are in competition, such as whether a shape is a water bottle or a thermos, while the deep prior that renders both as “a thing” remains relatively stable. It makes sense, then, that changing a branch is much easier than changing the trunk. Altering a deep foundational prior would disrupt much of what is dependent on it. Deep priors are slow to update for good reason, since changing them can present a massive threat to the system as a whole.

A short list of deep/foundational priors that make up the trunk of the dependency tree includes things like causality, time, tribal belonging, anthropomorphism, status and hierarchy, reputation, conformity, threat bias, disgust, body ownership, and a continuous self, which are all made up of even more foundational priors like thing-ness and agency. All of these are not necessarily at the same levels, and some may sit more downstream than others so they can be modulated more, but they are still foundational in the way the average human structures experience.

An internal representation, such as an image or metaphor, is not itself a total field. It is an object we “place onto” the field that has its own implicit or explicit properties, and in doing so, it reshapes the landscape of our current field’s properties. Take the metaphor “argument is war.” This metaphor introduces new branches and reweights existing ones, changing which interpretation becomes dominant and, through that shift, altering the field’s active properties towards war-like things, such as attacking and defending. These updated variables and constraints were not alive before the moment the “argument is war” metaphor became active; they only appeared once we took that metaphor as a way to look at things. In terms of the dependency tree, a representation mostly affects the outer branches rather than the trunk because it reweights shallow interpretations through precision, which is why it can restructure how you interpret input in a moment without changing the deep priors it rests on. Because of this, representations are typically cheap and fast to apply and remove, since they are not changing foundational structures. It’s important to note that a representation begins as a test case against the current field, and when it fits the field’s properties, it is integrated and reshapes the field. Over time, repeated use of a representation can train its structure into deeper parts of the dependency tree, reshaping the parts of the tree dependent on them.

For instance, if I am on the roof of a building and have intrusive thoughts of falling off, the image of myself falling matches the spatial awareness of being at a high elevation (variable), the physical laws of gravity (constraint), and the survival prior of “threat detection.” Crucially, a representation that fits your current field does not have to produce a positive feeling tone, as in the intrusive-thought example. This is because two properties of the field can come apart: valence and target. In the falling-off-the-roof example, the currently prioritized preferred outcome, the target, might be something like “survival by not falling off,” so representations that are coherent with these threat-related properties of the field will fit. However, the representation of falling off the building still produces negative valence because it is a signal that influences action selection to satisfy the survival-related properties of the field (ie generated unpleasant images of falling off the building causes you to avoid such a thing); this is active inference doing its thing. If the image were pleasant, we would be more motivated to fall off, but since it feels very bad, there is less incentive to give high precision to branches that lead to violation of survival.

Interestingly, representations do not only model present reality or intellectual knowledge, they can also preserve or simulate the properties of a target, a preferred state, that the current environment has made unavailable through processes like suppression. This is related to a function of fantasies in which they keep a preferred state active internally when the system does not believe that state can be realized externally due to conflicting reasons. By comparing several fantasies, or representations, the system can detect which properties remain stable across them, such as safety, agency, belonging, or adventure. Learning then consists of separating those deeper properties from the original form, like a fantasy, and finding new representations or actions that preserve the same properties. This is called a structure-preserving transformation. For example, a fantasy of living on another planet may contain targets like freedom, exploration, belonging, and wonder, which can then be separated from the fantasy setting and expressed through new actions or environments in ordinary life.

Phenomenologically, the field is like a thing we see through that can never itself be seen. This is [phenomenal transparency](https://www.blogs.uni-mainz.de/fb05philosophieengl/files/2013/07/79_Phenomenal_transparency.pdf), the characteristic of a representation whose construction is unavailable to attention, so only its content appears and never the process producing it. For instance, to look at things with an active representation is to deploy a kind of map in front of us, like putting on goggles that highlight some things and dim others in the latent space of knowing. Often, it is so implicit that we cannot get behind its curtain to watch how it applies structure to experience. As a distinction, raw experience, or sensory totality, is a theoretical, chaotic, high-entropy landscape that our systems never meet directly. Deep, high-level priors filter and reduce this entropy into the embodied field which will be explained later on through the mechanism of contraction. While the embodied layer is rich and feels like unfiltered reality, it is always structured when we perceive it. The field’s job is to select what is and is not relevant, leaving us with a workable set of options. Some might recall Aldous Huxley’s ‘reducing valve’ mechanism: “To make biological survival possible, Mind at Large has to be funnelled through the reducing valve of the brain and nervous system. What comes out at the other end is a measly trickle of the kind of consciousness which will help us to stay alive on the surface of this particular planet. To formulate and express the contents of this reduced awareness, man has invented and endlessly elaborated those symbol-systems and implicit philosophies which we call languages.” In other words, we must compress as we move up the three layers of the compression gradient because the richness of the bottom layers is not something we can act on directly. The field compresses sensory totality into a workable structure for us to live within, and as we move through the world, the field changes. Even when the embodied layer is perceived as a vast and rich landscape, that perception is itself a theoretical limited model.

Importantly, sensory totality itself is still a model, something that cannot be experienced directly so any mention of it is theoretical. However, there is substantial evidence to support the claim that, even though we cannot experience this totality, overwhelming, uncompressed sensory input outside of normal structure can still occur. The first being the [entropic brain hypothesis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3909994/) by Carhart-Harris & Friston. Their [REBUS paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6588209/) says psychedelics act “via their entropic effect on spontaneous cortical activity” and “relax the precision of high-level priors or beliefs, thereby liberating bottom-up information flow.” This suggests psychedelics [increase neural entropy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110594/) and reduce top-down constraint from the [default mode network](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10032309/) which predicts flooding, dissolvement of boundaries, and overwhelm. Interestingly, some [Jhana](https://meditation.mgh.harvard.edu/files/Chowdhury_24_NeuroImage.pdf) and [5-MeO-DMT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12010161/) appear to [increase brain entropy while producing less complex experience](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16146), like whiteouts or [cessation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10843092/), which adds more nuance to a simple “more entropy = more flooding” reading of sensory totality. Secondly, we can look at filtering pathologies like schizophrenia: “[Sensory gating refers to the central nervous system’s ability to filter sensory inputs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2820396/),” and poor gating may cause “[sensory inundation and ‘flooding.’](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2820396/)” We can also look at migraines as another example: migraines show “[altered sensory processing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s10194-023-01618-w)” with “hypersensitivity to visual stimuli” and “a lack of habituation.” Third, we can look at the neuroscience of gating in general: “The [TRN](https://cogsys.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2016/09/Ward-2013-WIRES-Thalamus.pdf) [thalamic reticular nucleus] gates the relay of sensory information to cortex,” and high gain can “disconnect” the relay from cortex. Information theoretic estimates put human behavioral throughput at about “[10 bits/s](https://www.cell.com/neuron/fulltext/S0896-6273%2824%2900808-0)” against sensory inflow of “no less than 1 gigabits/s.” And lastly, meditation progress seemingly points to the phenomenology of this reduction in compressing sensory totality as [Laukkonen & Slagter](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34139248/) points out that meditation acts as something like predictive deconstruction: “deconstructive meditation brings one closer to the here and now by disengaging anticipatory processes.” So even though sensory totality in itself as whole cannot be directly pointed to, there is evidence our systems are doing some type of filtering of rich entropy which leads to our moment-to-moment experience.

The mechanism of attention should be addressed. Attention is a process of that directs precision across the field, which includes the dependency tree, whether done automatic or manually. It is the control mechanism that determines which properties are weighted more or less in the moment. Attention has three core roles in this framework: moment-to-moment weighting, dwelling on layers, and sculpting.

Moment-to-moment weighting determines which properties and parts of experience count as being relevant, like placing attention on bodily signals rather than signals from other people. Dwelling on layers is the act of resting attention on a specific layer in the stack, which can result in downstream retuning of the field when the proper mechanisms are in place, such as safety and time. For example, staying with a sensation at the embodied layer long enough retunes the field to the sensation’s characteristics. This is a part of how “aha” moments happen and why background compression and decompression occur. Lastly, there is sculpting. Whatever receives repeated attention gets reinforced and eventually sinks into the trunk of the dependency tree, which is why years of practice develop an expert’s deep tacit knowledge which is a part of the embodied layer. These three roles map onto three timescales the literature separates with [state inference belonging to a moment](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10115398/1/1-s2.0-S0022249620300857-main.pdf), [parameter learning occurring across repetitions](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348153427_A_Step-by-Step_Tutorial_on_Active_Inference_and_its_Application_to_Empirical_Data), and [structure learning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.07092) happening "offline." These different roles matter because attention is one of the only direct controls we have on the field, and most of the time we use it like it doesn't matter. What it changes depends on its temporal variation: moment-to-moment changes what is immediately available, dwelling retunes the field, and repetition hits the trunk. Insight alone is clearly not enough, since practices hits all three levels of attention.

It’s important to note that there is a map of different ways the field can be instantiated through richness. On one level of the map, we can have an explicit “lens” in which we view experience through, on another level there is [amodal perception which is the field affecting things so implicitly that it is hard to even recognize.](https://philpapers.org/browse/husserl-horizonality) An example of this would be the prediction that the back of your hand exists if you are looking at it from the front, or that when you turn a corner on the street you are walking on you will see a different side of a building you’re looking at. Awareness (which is less “obvious” than attention) can hold a wider but already structured configuration, while more amodal or abstract cognition compresses experience into highly integrated representations that are easier to manipulate but carry less of the original sensory richness. The present moment is therefore not instantiated in one fixed form but rather, the system can move between diffuse embodied richness and highly compressed abstract structure depending on its goals.

Because deep priors are foundational and often tied to survival itself, the system treats threats to them as a big deal, and the felt sense of safety by itself does not seem sufficient for updating them. What is actually needed is slack, which is capacity not currently committed to holding the active field configuration in place. This allows the system to encounter prediction error without immediately resolving it through habitual defensive active inference. Section 2.3 will expand on this more.
