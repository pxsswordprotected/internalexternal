(This section deals a lot with predictive processing. If you are unfamiliar with the theory, it would held to know the basics)

The field of experience is the active, moment-to-moment configuration of our active priors, bodily state, context, attention, targets, and available actions which functions as a deep mobile frame that constantly reconfigures what is salient to the mind-body system. Priors are the stored generative structures that organize experience, and the field is what they manifest as when instantiated in a moment of time. The field’s properties, like variables and constraints, are the active criteria used to test input for fit. By input, I mean both raw sensory experience, such as seeing or hearing, and the things that experience is organized into, like a dog, a cat, or a mathematical object.

The field is always active; we are always using a sliding window through which to experience things, whether implicity or explicitly. At any given moment, the field uses a set of properties, which are downstream of deeper factors like active priors and bodily state, to determine if input fits within its current configuration. The most important properties seem to be constraints, variables, and fit distance. Variables are the parts that can change and alter the prediction of the system, constraints are the binding rules or limits that shape what can fit within it, and fit distance tells you whether input feels closer to or farther from the field’s properties, like a puzzle piece fitting into place. These are not the only three properties active within a field at once; others include actions that feel possible, feeling tone, and targets. Felt tone is valence, meaning the positive, negative, or neutral quality of experience.

A crucial mechanism helping the field is precision weighting. This is [essentially](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-026-04356-0) "the estimated reliability or confidence assigned to priors or sensory evidence, which then determines their influence on updating." It dictates how much attention or trust is given to specific things over others. There are many specific directions precision can take such as: precision of sensory evidence, precision of prior beliefs, precision of preferences or targets, and precision over policies or action selection. This weighting comes from conditioning through past experiences. Like the priors it acts on, precision is shaped by past input and inferred from current context, so the system learns which kinds of signals have been reliable in which contexts and weights them accordingly through interactions within a complex web of predictions. This means precision weighting can be conditioned over time (a person trained to treat their environment as dangerous will keep threat signals weighted high by default), but it can also shift rapidly in the moment as input and context changes. When a threat is detected, the system heavily weights survival variables and constraints and turns down the importance of logical constraints, forcing input to pass a very different test for fit. This rapid reconfiguration of the field is how the system instantly shifts what is salient without needing to build an entirely new one from scratch which is called sensory precision.

Another key job of precision weighting is that it can alter the current relative influence of competing prior preferences whose context-sensitive weighting helps determine the currently prioritized target. Prior preferences are prior probability distributions about which future outcomes the system prefers. In this framework, a target is the currently prioritized preferred outcome of a situation, while policies are possible action sequences selected partly by how well they are expected to reach the target. Simply, targets organize coordinated action by giving more or less weight to certain policy selections across short and long time horizons. For example, if conditioned enough, a survival target can gain control over social or long-term targets, but those other targets can still be “left over” if the survival target is fulfilled, and the left over targets still have the ability to influence things. This precision weighting among prior preferences helps instantiate momentary intention by deciding what gets chosen, when, and with how much weight. This process is called motivational precision. This means our current experience, which contains context (variables, interactions, etc.), changes both what evidence is trusted and which outcome is currently preferred.

For nuance, nested subsystems such as cells, tissue, organs, clusters of priors, and our nervous system can pursue different targets at once which is why target relevance matters. Target relevance here refers to the dominant target or synthesis of targets currently organizing attention and action. Other targets can remain active beneath the relevant target, producing mixed responses when their preferred paths to fulfillment conflict. A conscious target could therefore dominate knowable attention while an embodied protective target constrains what feels possible in its current state. This explains why someone can explicitly want a relationship with someone else while their field still makes avoidance more available than connection. For this framework, when I am speaking of targets I am generally meaning the most relevant to the current context of a situation.

So to be clear, there exists two type of precision in this framework: ‘sensory precision’ which determines what evidence is trusted, and ‘motivational precision’ which determines what targets most strongly organize perception and action. Typically I like to just use ‘precision’ since both versions are pointing to the process of giving weight to factors within the field.

In every moment, our “default” field is active and influencing things. This default field is incredibly deep and structures our experience in the most load-bearing ways. It is primarily instantiated and felt through the embodied layer, without needing to become explicit. It is a result of deep priors, which structure abstractions like “thing-ness,” “center reference point of experience,” or “that is a person.” These deep priors make the field feel like reality itself because they shape the way in which we constantly experience things. They do the most work to compress sensory totality, by which I mean theoretical sensory input before perceptual organization. In his streamlined enlightenment protocol, Roger Thisdell proposes that priors act like a reverse house of cards in which our lowest-level ones sit at the bottom, making up the foundation for the things built on top of them. For example, the concept of “thing,” a low-level prior for objecthood or thingness, sits almost at the very bottom level, while the concept of “water bottle” sits much higher, being made up of not just “thing,” but many other priors like “resource for survival,” each of which survive on a hierarchical model. There are also deep bodily priors called homeostatic priors, which are innate and evolutionarily ingrained and provide information about the body’s internal physiological state, such as temperature. These homeostatic priors allow us to continuously generate predictions that anticipate bodily needs and keep us within a survivable range through bodily functions and actions. Because they track survival directly, the system gives them very high precision, so they carry strong top-down authority and can be difficult to override. These priors give the system a physiological floor from which it makes predictions to ensure we do not bottom out at a biological level. Built on top of these priors is what researchers Micah Allen and Manos Tsakiris call the “first prior” which is a stable sense of self-awareness grounded in the body’s regulation. As shown, the default field is like the reference against which everything else is compared or on top of which it is built, so it cannot be bypassed easily. In a way, the default lives in the embodied layer to “act out” its predictions and reduce the error between what is happening and what it predicts will happen. This is active inference, a process in which the system takes action to make input match its model. Incoming sensory experience is structured by default priors, and behavior expresses or fulfills predictions shaped by them. The default field is deeply conditioned, while additional field properties are situationally activated by context, such as representations, and are generally recognizable as an explicit lens. The transition from the default field to something “extra” is like a gradient, not a binary either-or phase change. Repetition makes the extra properties imported from situations and objects sink deeper into the embodied layer, into the field. Additional field properties that are activated and used long enough may eventually become the new default way of seeing things, hence why experts pick up on small things so quickly. The expert has default load-bearing priors running in the embodied layer as a way of perceiving and acting.

I say the field constantly reconfigures based on moment-to-moment experience because ever-changing sensory input is always activating different priors. If I am talking to a friend one moment, then switch to using my phone in the middle of the conversation, the context and object of the phone bring new properties into the field. Talking to a friend activates priors like “I need to listen,” “I need to sit a certain way,” “conversation moves like this,” and “my face and tone matter,” while using the phone activates different priors like “I look down,” “my fingers move like this,” “awareness collapses,” and “my posture changes.” Action and the nervous system then come to matter a great deal, since the field shapes the body for what it prepares to do. Changing input has the ability to affect posture, muscle tension, awareness, attention, breathing, and what actions can be taken. This shows us that the field itself is embodied, as it is not just a way of structuring input but also a way of preparing, responding, moving, and regulating. Most of this stays implicit and low-level.

Another way to picture what the field is doing is through prediction trees, or “a probability distribution over competing hypotheses and possible future trajectories.” A prediction tree is the space of potential interpretations of input and the branches the field could resolve into, weighted by precision, which also come with their own action policies modulated by targets. Conscious perception usually settles on one dominant branch in moment-to-moment experience, while competing branches remain represented with lower probability and can still influence inference. Under active inference, one branch gaining enough posterior probability, meaning it becomes the most likely interpretation after taking current evidence into account, increases confidence in its associated action sequences. A dominant branch therefore organizes how the input is interpreted while increasing the weight of the action policies associated with that predicted state. Simply, the system selects actions expected to move it toward its preferred state. Through active inference, it minimizes prediction error, the gap between expected input and actual input, either by acting to make the world match the prediction or by updating its model. At the same time, the system’s actions can reduce target error, a term specific to this framework for the gap between the system’s current and preferred state. Prediction error and target error often interact, but they describe different discrepancies. Because the system cannot effectively explore every branch, it must narrow the tree through pruning methods like compression. Compression groups many lower-level prior structures into a single higher-level unit, such as “water bottle,” allowing the system to treat the package as one thing rather than evaluating each part separately. In doing so, a good deal of the lower-level richness is left out, but the search space shrinks and attention is freed for other input.

Prediction trees and the reverse house of cards therefore connect in what we can call the load-bearing tree, which is the dependency structure that makes the field’s current configuration possible. The house of cards is the vertical structure, with deep priors at the bottom holding up higher-level ones, while the branches represent competing predicted states made possible by that foundation. The perception that an object is a “water bottle” can only become a branch because “thing” is part of the foundation at the bottom. There exists the trunk of the load-bearing tree which is not something you can simply choose from; it is the foundational set of priors that makes almost any branching or action possible at all. Branching happens mostly at the higher levels, where multiple interpretations are in competition, such as whether a shape is a water bottle or a thermos, while the lower-level prior that renders both as “a thing” remains relatively stable. It makes sense, then, that changing a high-level branch is much easier than changing something near the bottom. Altering a deep foundational prior would disrupt much of what is built on top of it. Deep priors are load-bearing and slow to update for good reason, since changing them can present a massive threat to the system as a whole.

A short list of deep/foundational priors that make up the trunk of the load-bearing includes things like causality, time, tribal belonging, anthropomorphism, status and hierarchy, reputation, conformity, threat bias, disgust, body ownership, and a continuous self, which are all made up of even more low-level priors like thing-ness and agency. All of these are not necessarily at the same levels, and some may sit more downstream than others so they can be modulated more, but they are still foundational in the way the average human structures experience.

An internal representation, such as an image or metaphor, is not itself a field. It is an object we “place onto” the field that has its own implicit or explicit properties, and in doing so, it reshapes the landscape of our current field’s properties. Take the metaphor “argument is war.” This metaphor introduces new high-level branches and reweights existing ones, changing which interpretation becomes dominant and, through that shift, altering the field’s active properties towards war-like things, such as attacking and defending. These updated variables and constraints were not alive before the moment the “argument is war” metaphor became active; they only appeared once we took that metaphor as a way to look at things. In terms of the load-bearing tree, a representation mostly affects the upper branches rather than the trunk because it reweights high-level interpretations through precision, which is why it can restructure how you interpret input in a moment without changing the deep priors underneath. Because of this, representations are typically cheap and fast to apply and remove, since they are not changing foundational structures. It’s important to note that a representation begins as a test case against the current field, and when it fits the field’s properties, it is integrated and reshapes the field. Over time, repeated use of a representation can train its structure into deeper parts of the load-bearing tree, reshaping the parts of the tree built on top of them.

For instance, if I am on the roof of a building and have intrusive thoughts of falling off, the image of myself falling matches the spatial awareness of being at a high elevation (variable), the physical laws of gravity (constraint), and the survival prior of “threat detection.” Crucially, a representation that fits your current field does not have to produce a positive feeling tone, as in the intrusive-thought example. This is because two properties of the field can come apart: valence and target. In the falling-off-the-roof example, the currently prioritized preferred outcome, the target, might be something like “survival by not falling off,” so representations that are coherent with these threat-related properties of the field will fit. However, the representation of falling off the building still produces negative valence because it is a signal that influences action selection to satisfy the survival-related properties of the field (ie generated unpleasant images of falling off the building causes you to avoid such a thing); this is active inference doing its thing. If the image were pleasant, we would be more motivated to fall off, but since it feels very bad, there is less incentive to give high precision to branches that lead to violation of survival.

Interestingly, representations do not only model present reality or intellectual knowledge, they can also preserve or simulate the properties of a target, a preferred state, that the current environment has made unavailable through processes like suppression. This is related to a function of fantasies in which they keep a preferred state active internally when the system does not believe that state can be realized externally due to conflicting reasons. By comparing several fantasies, or representations, the system can detect which properties remain stable across them, such as safety, agency, belonging, or adventure. Learning then consists of separating those deeper properties from the original form, like a fantasy, and finding new representations or actions that preserve the same properties. This is called a structure-preserving transformation. For example, a fantasy of living on another planet may contain targets like freedom, exploration, belonging, and wonder, which can then be separated from the fantasy setting and expressed through new actions or environments in ordinary life.

Phenomenologically, the field is like a thing we see through that can never itself be seen. For instance, to look at things with a representation active is to deploy a kind of map in front of us, like putting on goggles that highlight some things and dim others in the latent space of knowing. Often, it is so implicit that we cannot get behind its curtain to watch how it applies structure to experience. As a distinction, raw experience, or sensory totality, is a theoretical, chaotic, high-entropy landscape that our systems never meet directly. Deep, low-level priors filter and reduce this entropy into the embodied field which will be explained later on through the mechanism of contraction. While the embodied layer is rich and feels like unfiltered reality, it is always structured when we perceive it. The field’s job is to select what is and is not salient, leaving us with a workable set of options. Some might recall Aldous Huxley’s ‘reducing valve’ mechanism: “To make biological survival possible, Mind at Large has to be funnelled through the reducing valve of the brain and nervous system. What comes out at the other end is a measly trickle of the kind of consciousness which will help us to stay alive on the surface of this particular planet. To formulate and express the contents of this reduced awareness, man has invented and endlessly elaborated those symbol-systems and implicit philosophies which we call languages.” In other words, we must compress as we move up the three layers of the compression gradient because the richness of the bottom layers is not something we can act on directly. The field compresses sensory totality into a workable structure for us to live within, and as we move through the world, the field changes. Even when the embodied layer is perceived as a vast and rich landscape, that perception is itself a theoretical limited model.

Importantly, sensory totality itself is still a model, something that cannot be experienced directly so any mention of it is theoretical. However, there is substantial evidence to support the claim that, even though we cannot experience this totality, overwhelming, uncompressed sensory input outside of normal structure can still occur. The first being the entropic brain hypothesis by Carhart-Harris & Friston. Their REBUS paper says psychedelics act “via their entropic effect on spontaneous cortical activity” and “relax the precision of high-level priors or beliefs, thereby liberating bottom-up information flow.” This shows psychedelics increase neural entropy and reduce top-down constraint from the default mode network which predicts flooding, dissolvement of boundaries, and overwhelm. Interestingly, some Jhana and 5-MeO-DMT appear to increase brain entropy while producing less complex experience, like whiteouts or cessation, which adds more nuance to a simple “more entropy = more flooding” reading of sensory totality. Secondly, we can look at filtering pathologies like schizophrenia: “Sensory gating refers to the central nervous system’s ability to filter sensory inputs,” and poor gating may cause “sensory inundation and ‘flooding.’” We can also look at migraines as another example: migraines show “altered sensory processing” with “hypersensitivity to visual stimuli” and “a lack of habituation.” Third, we can look at the neuroscience of gating in general: “The TRN [thalamic reticular nucleus] gates the relay of sensory information to cortex,” and high gain can “disconnect” the relay from cortex. Information theoretic estimates put human behavioral throughput at about “10 bits/s” against sensory inflow of “no less than 1 gigabits/s.” And lastly, meditation progress seemingly points to the phenomenology of this reduction in compressing sensory totality as Laukkonen & Slagter points out that meditation acts as something like predictive deconstruction: “deconstructive meditation brings one closer to the here and now by disengaging anticipatory processes.” So even though sensory totality in itself as whole cannot be directly pointed to, there is evidence our systems are doing some type of filtering of rich entropy which leads to moment-to-moment experience.

The mechanism of attention should be addressed. Attention is a process of that directions precision across the field, which includes the load-bearing tree, whether done automatic or manually. It is the control mechanism that determines which properties are weighted more or less in the moment. Attention has three core roles in this framework: moment-to-moment weighting, dwelling on layers, and sculpting.

Moment-to-moment weighting determines which properties and parts of experience count as being salient, like placing attention on bodily signals rather than signals from other people. Dwelling on layers is the act of resting attention on a specific layer in the stack, which can result in downstream retuning of the field when the proper mechanisms are in place, such as safety and time. For example, staying with a sensation at the embodied layer long enough retunes the field to the sensation’s properties. This is a part of how “aha” moments happen and why background compression and decompression occur. Lastly, there is sculpting. Whatever receives repeated attention gets reinforced and eventually sinks into the trunk of the load-bearing tree, which is why years of practice develop an expert’s deep tacit knowledge which is a part of the embodied layer.

It’s important to note that there is a map of different ways the field can be instantiated through richness. On one level of the map, we can have an explicit “lens” in which we view experience through, on another level there is amodal perception which is the field affecting things so implicitly that it is hard to even recognize. An example of this would be the prediction that the back of your hand exists if you are looking at it from the front, or that when you turn a corner on the street you are walking on you will see a different side of a building you’re looking at. Awareness (which is less “obvious” than attention) can hold a wider but already structured configuration, while more amodal or abstract cognition compresses experience into highly integrated representations that are easier to manipulate but carry less of the original sensory richness. The present moment is therefore not instantiated in one fixed form but rather, the system can move between diffuse embodied richness and highly compressed abstract structure depending on its targets, precision weighting, bodily state, and context.

Because deep priors are load-bearing and often tied to survival, the system can treat threats to them as a crisis. However, safety alone does not seem sufficient for updating them. A better requirement is sufficient slack, meaning the system has enough regulatory, cognitive, temporal, social, and representational capacity to encounter prediction error without immediately resolving it through defensive active inference. Safety contributes to this slack by reducing immediate threat, but controllability, bodily resources, social support, available attention, time, and the presence of a viable alternative model also matter. Research suggests that learning often occurs within a kind of Goldilocks zone in which a mismatch must be large enough to destabilize the existing model, but not so complex that the system becomes overwhelmed and falls back on rigid policies.

In terms of this framework, slack allows the system to keep both old priors and the contradicting input active at the same time without prematurely pruning one of them. The slack provides enough room to delay habitual action, tolerate the richness that is lost when compressing, search alternative branches, and allow a new representation to form. Controllability matters because a system that expects its actions to have no effect has less reason to explore new policies, while social support can share some of the regulatory burden required to remain in contact with destabilizing material. Without sufficient slack, the system is more likely to increase the precision of its existing model and return to its predicted state. With sufficient slack, it can leave the prediction error unresolved long enough for the deeper structure to reorganize. This seems like a core underlying driver of deliberate practice, which “consists of individualized training activities specially chosen to improve specific aspects of a student’s performance through repetition and successive refinement. It’s mindful repetition on performance tasks just beyond the edge of one’s capabilities” according to Justin Skycak.

Deep change, then, becomes most likely when there is enough destabilization to loosen the relevant parts of the load-bearing tree, enough stability to avoid complete overwhelm, and enough time and alternative structure for a new field to become embodied.
