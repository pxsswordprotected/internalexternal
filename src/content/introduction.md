title: Introduction
---
As things march along the process of entropic evolution, what naturally arises is a sense of increase in complexity. More and more things start to connect and depend on each other, creating systems that have feedback loops and downstream effects, eventually becoming chaotic. But through this process is a demarcation of specificity, the act of things becoming more pointy.

The human story very obviously outlines this: we’ve gone from primitive living in caves to developing and living in chaotic cities with vast interconnected economic systems and advanced architecture. It is clear creativity is of upmost importance here, as without it, the human species would be doomed to a state of static repetition, something seen in even our closest relatives, chimpanzees, [who cannot innovate tools across generations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratchet_effect#Cultural_anthropology). The specific question I am interested in is why does our mind-body system and phenomenology allow for creation of such grandiose things? And if we can find that out, what other questions and answers does that lead to?

I want to try to answer this, working with years of experience studying human perception, psychology, and behavior. In this piece, I attempt to sketch out a working ontology explaining what we are doing in perception that is so important, and how this affects what we do and the systems we participate in. I do not claim to have solved anything, or that my work is 100% correct in all areas, or I am an expert so you should take my word for it, but I do think my reframing of certain problems is generative and valuable, which has practical implications.

As a whole, this piece is about exploring a unified framework for the phenomenology of human experience. A set of principles that explain how perception, memory, emotion, and much more follow from the same underlying processes. This matters because the way we design things such as schools, tools, media, technology, medicine, and whole societies depends on models of how people work. If the model we are using when building these things is wrong, we build them in such a way to work against us. If we can deeply understand the rules that shape subjective experience, which in turn shapes "objective" experience like scientific discovery, we can design for human flourishing in a much more intentional way.

> “There is indeed the inexpressible. This shows itself; it is the mystical.”
> 
> — Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 6.522

Some things, ethics, aesthetics, love, are only fully comprehensible when not put to language. For Wittgenstein, the mystical here is the boundary where language breaks down and cannot express what the one who speaks is attempting to convey. As long as we have to communicate in a finite, compressible manner, we will never be able to share what hangs in our deepest depths of knowing.

Language is not a complete failure as it allows us to transport seemingly fleeting experiences into symbols that others can understand. However, it fails in the sense that the fleeting experience is never fully captured because to turn it into tokens of interpretable symbols, we must use compression. Compression in this framework means preserving contextually important structure while leaving other characteristics out.

Knowledge (which I am defining here as information encoded in a form that can shape perception) shows up in the world through many forms such as a book, a television show, a conversation, or a diagram. Below the obvious surface of notation are two more layers, each dense with information. Compression of knowledge loses richness but gains portability, stability, and shareability. To externalize deeper layers beyond surface level notation is to compress them, and most of what we know, what we feel, cannot be compressed in totality.
